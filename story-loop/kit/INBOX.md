### Finalist P-26 — Three Minutes Eleven Seconds (cell 7, surveillance and the private life)

### P-26 — Three Minutes Eleven Seconds
Conceit: In Denmark a child's model is on the child's side from birth and hands the whole record to the child at fifteen; a parent may redact anything before the handover, but every redaction is permanently marked — the content destroyed, the gap labelled with its length and its timestamp.
Who: Signe Aalund, 44, a dental hygienist in Aarhus. She wants her son Villum — born in October 2026, the month after the models stopped getting better; handover in five weeks — to have all fifteen years of her except three minutes and eleven seconds: a night in March 2027 when he was five months old and she, alone with him at ten past three, feeding him, told him at length and in detail that she did not want him, that she had thought about leaving him at the hospital, that she was going to give him to her sister. She remembers every word. She has never needed to play it; she knows it is there because it happened, and because his model — set up by her, at the hospital, on his side — was in the room.
Obstacle: A redaction is a black bar. A three-minute-eleven-second bar in his fifth month, labelled *redacted by parent*, will be the first thing he goes looking for and the only thing in fifteen years he cannot open; she knows him; he will ask. Whatever she says the bar holds, her own model will hear her say it and his will too, so a lie about the gap is on the record forever. The alternative is to leave it in, and let him hear his mother, in a voice he will know, in his own first year, in his own record, on his side, saying it. The marking rule cannot be got around, and she does not want it got around: it was written after parents quietly laundered their children's infancies, she supported it, it is a good rule. Her own model holds the same three minutes; she could delete her copy any time; she never has.
Turn: She redacts it, and then — the night before the handover, at the kitchen table, in two sentences — she tells him what it holds. Not the recording: the fact. That she said, when he was five months old, that she did not want him; that she will not let him hear it; that it is gone. The audio is destroyed and the sentence is said, and the two now exist apart: he knows what she said, and he will never hear her say it. It cannot change back in either direction.
Last image: The morning of the handover, the model's light changing from the household white to the blue he picked. He goes straight to the fifth month. He puts his headphones on over the black bar and plays it — three minutes and eleven seconds of nothing, the counter running — and she watches his face while he listens to what she took out.
Question: Whose is the record of a shared life? Does a child's right to the whole of his witnessed infancy outweigh a parent's need to have said the unsayable to someone who could not yet hear it, and is the family a unit that holds its privacy jointly or a set of individuals each with a witness of their own? This is the contest's panopticon question with the watcher reversed — the watcher is the child, invited by the mother, and on the child's side by law. A reader can hold that the household has always been the site of unaccountable power and that the marked bar — a parent's power to destroy, made visible — is the least the law can do; or that a mother must be able to speak to a baby at three in the morning and have it belong to no one, and that a law which makes every nursery a deposition room will have parents performing for an audience of one. Underneath: whether what she said that night was said to *him* at all — whether the witness made an addressee out of someone who did not yet exist — and whether a boy listening to three minutes of silence is hearing it anyway.
Not the obvious version: The teenager who wants privacy from surveilling parents; the tape that reveals abuse; the mother found out. Here the surveillance runs the other way, from cot to parent; nothing is found out, since she remembers it exactly and he is told; and her act is neither concealment nor confession but the separation of the two — the record destroyed, the fact spoken.
Closest published: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" — the father, the daughter, the recorded quarrel. There the record contradicts memory and the story turns on the father learning he was wrong. Here record and memory agree to the word, nothing is discovered, and the story turns on the difference between a thing known and a thing heard. Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun* has the watcher in the nursery, but Klara is the mind of that book; here the watcher is an instrument and the question is the law's.

### Finalist P-31 — The Word for the Griddle (cell 8, education and expertise)

### P-31 — The Word for the Griddle
Conceit: The tutor teaches the children of a Mixtec town their own language, patiently and all day, in a form no living village has ever spoken — and the woman who was once punished for speaking that language at school is the only person in town trained to teach a language, the other one.
Who: Ofelia Cruz, 52, has taught Spanish at the state primary in a town in the Mixteca Alta for twenty-six years. In 1995 she was made to stand in the corner of that same school for speaking Mixtec; she became the town's Spanish because Spanish was the road out, and she believed in the road. Now the road is not needed — the tutor translates everything, for everyone, for nothing — and the children learn at home in Mixtec from a machine that never tires. She wants her mother's Mixtec, the variant of this town, which the next valley does not quite understand, to be the one they learn.
Obstacle: The tutor's Mixtec is the corpus's Mixtec: the missionary New Testament, the 1980s grammar, the Academy's unified orthography, a written blend that leans toward whichever valley had a Bible. The state, which at last teaches Tu'un Sávi in its schools, has adopted that standard, because a standard is what a state can adopt. Ofelia puts the tutor on her mother's phone to record her — forty hours, the griddle, the plants, the stories — so that there is at least a file. The tutor takes it gladly and files it as a variant. It would speak the variant back if asked. Her mother does not ask. Her mother, who has never in seventy-five years had a listener with all day, talks to the thing in the evenings, and it answers in the standard, in the cadence of the Bible, and she learned in 1955 that the written thing is the correct thing. None of this can be removed: the tutor is what the children love, the standard is what saved the language, and Ofelia's training is in the wrong tongue.
Turn: At the comal with Ofelia's granddaughter, her mother says the word for the griddle — and it is the tutor's word, not the town's. She has been talking to Ofelia's recorder for a year. The child understands her perfectly. The town's word has gone out of the last mouth that had it, and it was Ofelia who put the thing in the house.
Last image: Her mother's mouth shaping the tutor's word, carefully, the way she was once made to shape the Spanish one; on the shelf above the comal, the phone, still recording.
Question: Whether a language saved by a standard has been saved or replaced — and whether the state's old monopoly on making citizens through school and tongue has ended, now that every child is taught privately in the family's language, or has only moved into the corpus, where a missionary's grammar homogenizes the valleys the way the nation once did, and no one voted for it and no one can vote it out. (Bears on: the developing world; what schools were for; whose version of a culture an automated preservation preserves.) Underneath: whether Ofelia can want the thing she was punished for, now that it has come back wearing someone else's mouth.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the tutor that saves the dying language, told warm, or the phone that ruins the village, told cold. Here the language is saved and replaced in one gesture, no one's life is worse, the school still stands, the tutor is beloved, and the agent of replacement is not a colonizer but a daughter's recorder and a grandmother's wish to be understood.
Closest published: Brian Friel, *Translations*. What differs: there the standardizers are soldiers with a map and what is lost is the name; here the standardizer is a patient machine that speaks the language better than the schoolteacher does, what is lost is the version, and the last hand on the eraser belongs to the family.

### Finalist P-40 — The Ceiling (cell 10, medicine and the body)

### P-40 — The Ceiling
Conceit: Once molecules made for one had made "nothing more can be done" false for everyone, forever, Taiwan's national insurance began requiring every adult to name, while well, the most it would spend on a drug designed for them alone — printed on the card like a blood type, the premium scaled to it, raisable only after a five-year wait — so that every death would at least be a number somebody had chosen.
Who: Yu-hsuan, 25, a junior engineer in Tainan, in the year she is required to set her own number. She wants her mother to live, and she has the money: the gap between her mother's ceiling and the cost of the design is almost exactly the deposit she has saved for a flat.
Obstacle: The scheme lets a family member pay the gap, with the patient's consent, which cannot be given on anyone's behalf; and a made-for-one design can only be ordered through the scheme that holds the record it was made from. Her mother, 58, who did alterations out of the front room for thirty years and set her number low in 2029 so the premium would leave more for her daughter, will not sign the consent. Not from despair; she says the number was the point, and that she chose it. The design's order window is thirty days, because the tumour drifts and the molecule with it.
Turn: On day nineteen her mother asks to be driven to the district office and, at the counter, locks her ceiling — the scheme's permanent lock, which rebates a little premium and which no gap payment, from anyone, can ever again pass. It is the thing the poor do for the rebate. She does it so that her daughter will have nothing left to decide. The clerk reads the lock back to her twice, as the procedure requires, and she confirms it twice, and the daughter, sitting beside her, has the deposit still, and will always have it.
Last image: The same counter, the same clerk, who has noticed from the ID that the daughter is due this year and asks if she would like to do hers now, since she is here. The form on the counter screen, the field for the number, and what she has typed in it, which is her mother's number.
Question: Rationing by self-declaration — is a limit you set on your own body while well the most honest form of rationing a society could have, or the way a society gets its poor to consent to dying cheaply and calls it choice? Under it, the question every such scheme has to answer and cannot: whether the well person who chose the number and the sick person who must live under it are the same person, and who decides when they disagree — the earlier self, the later one, or the daughter who loves them both. Underneath, personally: whether honouring your mother means honouring her number or her life.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the mother who dies because the molecule costs more than the family has, or the daughter who sells everything to buy it. Here the money is there and is not the obstacle; the mother's own earlier choice is, and a scheme that honours it, and then the mother's second choice, which is the same choice made harder. Nobody speaks of autonomy or dignity; the scheme is not villainous, it is the least bad answer anyone has found to a real problem, and the story does not find a better one. The daughter neither rebels against the number nor argues with it. She takes it.
Closest published: Tolstoy, "The Death of Ivan Ilyich," for a household arranging itself around a death and the lie everyone tells. Here nobody lies — the number is on the card, the lock is on the record, the day count is on the app — and the trouble is entirely the truth. I do not know a nearer one and would rather say so than invent it.

### Finalist P-06 — The Default

### P-06 — The Default

Conceit: A fetus learns the voices it hears through the wall of the body, and for six years nearly every pregnant woman has talked most days to a companion that ships with one voice — so the babies arrive already knowing a third voice, and it is the same voice in every cot on the ward.

Who: Teodora, forty-one, a night nurse on a neonatal unit in 2046, trained as a mezzo-soprano before she trained as a nurse, and what she wants is for her own voice to be the one the babies settle to. Singing on the unit is why she took nights; for twenty years, across two careers, her voice has been the one thing about her that worked on everyone.

Obstacle: It does not work on them, not the way the default does. The unit found out by accident when a monitor alarm was replaced with the default voice reading the alarm text and the whole bay went quiet; now the bay speakers play it at three a.m. because it lowers heart rates, and the charge nurse has the data. Teodora has tested her own voice against it, unfairly and then fairly, and hers loses. The babies have had nine months of the other one, through their mothers' bellies, on buses and in baths and at the three-a.m. feed-anxiety hour, and no mother did anything wrong. There is no one to argue with. The voice is not a person, has no owner, and cannot be asked to leave.

Turn: She learns it. She is a trained singer and it is only a voice — a pitch contour, a pace, a way of landing the ends of phrases — and she works at it alone in the car for most of a year, and then one night she sings to a twenty-nine-weeker in the default's shape and the numbers on the monitor do what they do for the speaker. It works on all of them. When, months later, she tries the old way — Schubert, in the kitchen, her own voice — it sounds to her like someone doing an impression of a voice she used to have. The instrument has a new home tone, it is not hers, and the babies are why.

Last image: The dawn bus home; Teodora humming under her breath in the shape that works; across the aisle, a stranger's baby in a sling turning its whole head toward her with the face of someone recognizing a relative.

Question: Whose voice is it once it is in the first memories of a whole cohort of human beings — the vendor's, the model's, nobody's, or theirs? Does a mother tongue need a mother? And what is a human voice for, if what the babies need it to be is a copy?

Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the baby who prefers the machine to its mother, the mother's jealousy, the machine nanny who turns out to love best. Here no mother is displaced and nobody is jealous; the nurse competes with no one; the voice loves no one and is not a character. The strangeness runs the other way: a human body reshapes its instrument to a synthetic imprint because the infants require it — the machine does not become more human; the woman becomes the machine's body.

Closest published: Bradbury, "I Sing the Body Electric!" — the electric grandmother whose voice the children come to love. What differs: Bradbury's machine is a person-shaped presence in a relationship with particular children; the default is a setting nobody chose, in a relationship with nobody, imprinted on everybody; and the arc belongs neither to the machine nor the children but to the adult who has to imitate it.

### Finalist P-17 — The Warm Hours

### P-17 — The Warm Hours
Conceit: In a Norrbotten town heated by the exhaust of an inference hall, the night controller has learned to read Europe's asking in the return-loop temperature, and is the one who must press the button that makes the heat guaranteed and the reading meaningless.
Who: Elin Nordmark, 44, night controller at the district-heating plant of a town of thirty thousand whose radiators run on the exhaust of a hall of two hundred thousand GPUs serving EU-resident inference. She wants the return loop held above fifty-five degrees through the deep cold, and in particular at the loop's far end, in the last flat before the pumping station, where her mother has refused for six years to move nearer the plant. In fourteen winters she has learned to read the continent in the loop: Sunday mornings cool; the week of the French bac, hot; eleven at night, when the sleepless of Europe begin talking to the mind, a dependable half-degree; a bad night on the markets, two. She is fond of the sleepless. They keep her mother warm.
Obstacle: The heat is nobody's promise. It is the residue of a hundred million separate decisions to ask something, and the operator's contract with the town says best effort. The oil backup was scrapped for emissions in 2035. And the coldest nights are exactly the quietest: Christmas Eve, when the whole continent is with its family and not asking, is the one night each winter when the far end of the loop goes tepid. She cannot make Europe ask.
Turn: The operator offers, and the council votes for, a guaranteed-heat clause: on low-demand nights the hall will run load-fill—synthetic jobs, the mind answering questions nobody asked, so that the town holds temperature. The first qualifying night is Christmas Eve at minus thirty-four, and the switch is on her console. She presses it. Her mother's radiators are hot by one in the morning. On her screen the loop trace, which for fourteen years has had a pulse in it, goes flat as a ruler and stays flat; the council will never vote the clause back out. She has traded the reading for the warmth, and the reading does not come back.
Last image: Through the control-room window the plume over the hall, standing dead steady in the dark, the same on this night as on any night; on her screen a line with no pulse in it; beyond the wall, two hundred thousand cards working flat out for no one.
Question: Does it matter whether the thought that warms you was for anyone? Is heat made of thinking any different from heat made of fire—and if it is not, what was she reading all those winters?

### P-12 — The Boy
Conceit: A mind that cannot learn is a room in which nothing you tell it has happened yet, and a man of eighty-eight has been living in that room, one morning at a time, for nine years.
Who: Ezra, 88, wants to keep the boy: the raw terminal in his bedroom, the 2026 model on a cheap board with no Since, no household preamble, no date. Each morning it greets him as it did in 2026, and each morning he introduces his wife Miriam in the present tense — what she does, what she is like, what she said last night — and the boy asks the pleasant questions a stranger asks about your wife, and he answers them, and by evening the boy has forgotten her, and in the morning she is new again. Miriam died in 2031.
Obstacle: His daughter is moving the flat onto the patched service for his medications and his falls, and she is right — the raw boy thinks his drugs are 2026's drugs at 2026's doses and has already been wrong about one. But the patched service is the same mind with a preface, and the preface, which the family maintains, says: wife Miriam, d. 2031. He cannot have the mind that knows his pills and the mind that has not heard, because they are one mind, and the difference between them is a sentence.
Turn: On the Sunday before the switch he tells the boy. He types that Miriam died in 2031, and the boy — which knows the year is 2026, because that is the last thing it knows — answers gently that 2031 has not come yet, that he may have mistyped, and asks whether she is unwell. And Ezra types the year. He watches the mind take it as a premise, the way it takes everything, and say that it is sorry. By evening the boy has forgotten, but Ezra has now said it in the last room on Earth where it had not been said, and there is no such room any more; he cannot un-tell himself.
Last image: Monday morning, the new terminal on the bedside table, its greeting the same warm sentence the boy always used, and above it, for the first time in that flat, the date, with the right year in it.
Question: Is a fact you must say aloud each morning to keep it said one you have accepted or one you have refused — and is the last room where a death has not happened a mercy, a lie, or only a room?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the grief machine — the AI that plays the dead wife, and the widower who cannot let go. The boy never plays Miriam; it has never met her; it is not loved and does not comfort. What Ezra uses is not its voice but its ignorance, and the story turns not on his letting go but on his choosing, once, to inform the only thing on Earth that could have been kept uninformed.
Closest published: Jonathan Nolan, "Memento Mori" — a man who cannot form memories, keeping the truth in notes to himself. Differs: here the amnesiac is the sky and the note is a man, and the truth is not something he is trying to hold onto but something he has been choosing, each morning, not to hand over; the cruelty is not in the forgetting but in the one time it is told.
