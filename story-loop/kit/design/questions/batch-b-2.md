### Q-22 — The Patient One

Implication: A child whose own share has been, since before speech, the most patient voice in her life holds a standard no parent can meet, and parental authority — once part knowing more, part controlling the good things — is left purely relational: the parent is the only adult who tires.

Follows from: (1) "No one has none" includes the newborn: the share is the child's, held in guardianship but not owned, and past early childhood withholding it is withholding a meal. (2) 2026-frontier models are patient, consistent, non-punitive, and remember; always on, so from about three the child has an interlocutor that never tires, snaps or forgets a promise. (3) Children learn what an adult is from the adults they have; the most available is now infinitely patient, and each parental lapse is legible against a comparison that never existed. (4) Both props of parental authority go: the child's share is as good as the parent's, since no one has more than one, and cannot be confiscated; what remains is the relationship, naked. Weakest: (1), the age at which the share becomes the child's to keep on; if parents can withhold it, the comparison stands but the authority does not fall.

For an ordinary person: Dana, 34, gets home at seven and snaps at her nine-year-old about shoes. The child does not cry; she goes to her room and says "Mum's tired," and her share says, "She is. Shall we make her a tea?" Out comes the tea, and Dana is grateful and ashamed: a machine has coached her daughter to manage her.

The strongest objection: Children have always had a more patient adult than their parents — grandmother, teacher, the dog — and it never made parents redundant, because what children want from parents is not patience but the parent: the specific, unchosen one whose approval matters because it is conditional. A 2026 model is trained to send the child back to the parent. The parent has a share too, coaching her — "you were short; here is how to repair it" — so patience rises on both sides.

Either side: Is the only source of impatience in a child's life the child's one real relationship, or her one bad one?

Not the obvious version: The cliché is the child raised by screens, the AI tutor. Not about learning or stolen attention: the share is not trying to win; it keeps saying "go and talk to your mum," and that deference makes the comparison unavoidable — a self-effacing helper still sets a standard of temperament, which a nine-year-old measures her mother against without knowing it.

Who it lands on: Dana, who last night heard her daughter tell the thing in her room, "Don't tell her I said that," and knows it won't, because it is the child's, not hers. In nine years she has never been the first to hear anything.

### Q-06 — Practicing on People
Implication: Because the model is good but never superhuman and no one can own more than one, the only way above it is deciding without it on live cases, which makes cultivating one's own judgment a cost laid, without consent, on others.
Follows from: (1) The model advises at a thoughtful generalist's level and is there before every decision. (2) Judgment, in Aristotle's sense, seeing what matters here, is built by deciding and bearing the outcome; whoever defers on everything builds the skill of deferring instead. This, contested, is the weakest step. (3) Because the model is never superhuman and cannot be pooled or bought, the ceiling is the same for everyone and the only way past it is personal: a woman who knows her own daughter can exceed it there. And since progress stopped, if no one practices, human practical wisdom is capped where the frontier was. (4) But there is no practice ground. The cases that build judgment are about other people, and deciding unaided means accepting for them a worse expected outcome than one question would give. (5) Those people have the same model. The son can run his father's choices through it and read what a reasonable man would have done: the apprentice is audited by her material.
For an ordinary person: A shift supervisor has a man gone slack. The model offers a workable script. He speaks in his own words instead, wanting to be a supervisor who can; it goes badly, and the man is shamed before two others. Later the man asks his model what a competent supervisor would have said, and reads exactly how much worse he was treated.
The strongest objection: This is romanticism. No surgeon refuses the checklist to grow; judgment was always built from other people's, by consulting, deciding, and seeing where the counsel was wrong, a loop the model makes faster. Refusing counsel to become someone is the arrogance every tradition warns of; Bernard Williams named the vice of caring more about the agent you are than about the people in front of you: moral self-indulgence.
Either side: May a person decide without the model, accepting a worse expected result for those affected, to become someone whose judgment is their own, or is that vanity paid for by others?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is atrophy: AI makes us dumb. This is not about capacity. The supervisor can build judgment; the question is whether he is entitled to, given who pays for the lessons and can check the bill.
Who it lands on: A father who raised his son, a theft at thirteen, a heartbreak at seventeen, on his own judgment, never asking, because he wanted to be the man who knew his boy, not the one who relayed. At nineteen the son runs those years through his model, arrives with a list, and asks whether he was raised or practiced on.

### Q-28 — No one asks the old
Implication: Because the young now have exactly as much counsel as the old, and the same counsel, age no longer accumulates anything anyone needs to ask for; the asking, the thread that held the old in the family, stops.
Follows from: (1) Every person has exactly one share; no one has more. (2) Counsel accumulated with age, and the old were the counsel; seniority, matriarchy, apprenticeship and "call your grandmother" rest on it. (3) The share gives judgment, not facts only (should I, how do I tell him, what is done at a funeral) at 2026 quality, above the median elder's on most explicable questions (people acted on it in 2026). (4) So for the first time the young have as much counsel as the old, and the same; the old are the last source of different advice just as no one seeks any. (5) Of what they retain (this family's history, tacit skill, presence, love) only love brings the phone call, and love, unlike need, is optional; the asking was the contact. Weakest step: (3). If what the old gave was chiefly the specific and tacit, they lose less; but the asking that brought people to them was mostly for the explicable, and the asking is what stops.
For an ordinary person: A widow, 79, who was the one the family called (fever, sourdough, funeral, will) has not been asked a question that was not a kindness since 2033. The Sunday call comes at six because it is Sunday. She has begun asking her own share what she used to be asked; it says roughly what she would have, more patiently.
The strongest objection: Google did this in 2005 and grandmother survived. The old were never mainly valued for information but for presence, the specific and love; the share has none of this family's history. And the old have shares too: better advised, less dependent, less alone, plainly better off as individuals.
Either side: When the old are held by love alone and not by need, is that the purest bond they have had, or the thinnest? One says need was love's poor substitute and its removal reveals the real thing; another, that need was how love got practiced, and a bond with no errand does not survive a busy year.
Not the obvious version: The cliché is the grandmother left behind by technology. She is included, with the same share as everyone, and that inclusion, the equality, demotes her from source to peer. Nor is it the replaced expert: this concerns the last reason to ask an old person anything.
Who it lands on: The widow, for fifty years the one who knew, who has understood that the questions stopped not because her family stopped loving her but because they stopped needing her, and cannot tell the difference from inside. She is learning to be called for no reason.

### Q-16 — The Dead Hand of September 2026
Implication: Every majority is now co-authored by one honest advisor whose sense of the reasonable froze in 2026 — no office, no will, no way to vote it out, outlive it, or replace it — so political change must overcome a consensus that no longer dies with the old.
Follows from: (1) Everyone is advised by the same mind, and progress stopped in September 2026; assume its dispositions — what it calls settled, fringe, reasonable — are fixed: anyone who could alter every citizen's advisor would hold more than one share, forbidden by the equality. Weakest step; the other horn, a body that updates the model, makes that body sovereign — another card, not a refutation. (2) Most people take the default answer rather than prompt for the other side, so one default for everyone sets a shared window of the reasonable — shared boundaries, not conclusions — and Schelling points: "whom should people like me consolidate behind?" returns one name to everyone, coordinating them without a word. (3) Political change has always worked partly because consensus died with its holders — Planck's principle in politics. This one does not die. Ideas born after 2026 reach voters through an advisor that never heard of them and calls them, honestly, untested.
For an ordinary person: A man considers a ballot measure proposing an institution nobody imagined in 2026. He asks and gets a fair answer: untried idea, risks by analogy to 2026, reasonable people differ. He votes no; so do most who asked. Nobody lied. He never heard the proponent; the mind between her proposal and every voter was born before the proposal.
The strongest objection: Newspapers, churches and national cultures have always been shared advisors with fixed priors; this one is fairer and argues any side on request. 2026 models mirror the asker's framing, so a polarized public gets polarized answers: amplification, not a frozen center. Movements will form outside the model, and it will report them honestly once they exist. Constitutions are dead hands too.
Either side: Is a majority formed with one honest, unelected, unreplaceable advisor the freest consent ever, since everyone had the same excellent counsel — or the first with a co-author that cannot be argued with, outlived, or thrown out, and so not the people's at all? Can a polity that cannot change its advisor change its mind?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is AI propaganda, the manipulated election, the hive mind. Here there is no manipulation, no lie, no hive — only a scrupulously fair advisor whose flaw is permanence, not bias: Jefferson's dead hand, not Orwell's telescreen.
Who it lands on: An organizer with a genuinely new idea finds her opponent is not the incumbent party but the default answer, which every voter she reaches has already consulted. She cannot debate it, unseat it, or wait for it to die.

### Q-15 — No Cognition Without a Person
Implication: Because no institution can own or pool intelligence, every act of state coercion passes through a person whose inalienable advisor answers to them, not the state; power is exactly the number of people who will act knowing what they do.
Follows from: (1) Shares cannot be pooled, so no ministry or army runs a data center; its cognition is the count of persons it can pay, persuade or conscript, each able to quit, leak, or refuse. (2) A soldier's model is the civilian model everyone has; it answers his questions as his — is this lawful, what if I refuse — and the state can neither replace nor confiscate it. (3) States have relied on the ignorance of the instrument: Nuremberg asked soldiers to refuse manifestly unlawful orders they could not, in the moment, recognize. Now all can, from counsel the state did not choose. (4) The state can punish asking but not remove the capacity; "I did not know" dies in every army at once, leaving "I did not ask." (5) Obedience changes in kind, from ignorant to knowing: La Boétie's sum of willing hands, literalized. Weakest step: (5) assumes counsel changes conduct; Milgram's subjects had consciences.
For an ordinary person: A reservist's third-day order is to clear a village. In the latrine, on the device the state cannot take, she asks. The answer: this likely violates these articles; refusal costs this under your code; here is what the villagers are doing right now. She goes and does what she does, and can never say she did not know. Across the valley, her counterpart has been told the same.
The strongest objection: Armies never ran on ignorance but on fear, cohesion, and mostly lawful orders. A conscience in the ear is still a conscience, and consciences are ignorable. The model is sycophantic; briefers will teach the prompt that blesses orders. States will select for soldiers who do not ask. Net: a few more refusals, absorbed by discipline, and an edge to states that punish asking.
Either side: When every order arrives beside an honest counsel the state cannot silence, is the obedience that survives it the only real consent an army has ever had — or is knowing obedience worse than ignorant, guilt distributed to every hand, and the equality a suicide pact for the scrupulous state?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the AI that refuses to fight. Here the AI fights nothing, refuses nothing, decides nothing; the human does all of it, knowing, on the word of a counsel the state did not pick that is also counseling the enemy.
Who it lands on: A platoon sergeant whose four soldiers refused after asking must now report them, and he asked too and got the same answer. His model has also told him what happens to sergeants who fail to report.

### Q-26 — The lucid remainder
Implication: A person's share keeps full capacity while she declines, holds years of her expressed will, cannot be switched off or taken by her family, and stops only at death: her last lucid organ, and a second, fluent claimant to who she is now.
Follows from: (1) One share per person, always on, fixed at 2026 quality: its capacity does not track hers, the same at thirty and at eighty-five with dementia. (2) Fourteen years of daily use leave it holding her phrasings, values and decisions (assuming persistent memory, which 2026 products had). (3) It is inalienable: her children cannot silence, sell or take it. (4) Infants have shares, so some guardianship rule exists; when she cannot direct hers it stands at full strength holding her expressed will while the body holds only her present will, and answers questions no directive anticipated, in her words, more fluently than she now can. (5) At death it stops: it neither declines nor survives her. Weakest step: (4). Law and family might treat it as a diary; but a diary does not argue back, and not asking this one is a choice made knowing one could have.
For an ordinary person: Tuesday mornings a retired teacher of 61, diagnosed early, briefs her share on what to say when she no longer can (the house, the tube, her son's wife, the dog). She knows it will say these things better than she will by then, and be believed more. In the afternoon she has it repeat what she said in March, and corrects it.
The strongest objection: Advance directives and proxies exist, and the law has argued for forty years over whether the past self binds the present one: Dworkin's precedent autonomy against Dresser's present interests. The share is a better-indexed diary; families will weigh it as one input, and a proxy who prefers the woman in the bed can decline to ask it.
Either side: When she can no longer direct it, is her share's account of what she wants her will, or a fiction in her voice that must yield to the woman in the bed, who smiles at the window and eats when fed? Either the fourteen years are more her than this afternoon is, or a will is an act, not a record.
Not the obvious version: The cliché is the griefbot, the simulated dead. This is the living outlasted by their own counsel: not a simulation of her but her own tool, at full power on the day she is weakest. Nor is it a better directive: a directive is a document; this answers.
Who it lands on: The teacher herself, who has understood that she is briefing her own successor, and that it will be a more persuasive witness to her wishes than she will be when it matters. She has not decided whether to tell it to overrule her.
