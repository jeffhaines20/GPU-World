### Q-09 — cell C — The Compute Lease
Implication: Because firms cannot hold compute and reach it only through the persons who have it, employment becomes a lease of the engine with a human attached, and every adult a rentier of one unit of the key capital good, paid a rent owing nothing to what they do.
Follows from: A share goes to every human; a corporation is none, so firms own no cognition. Coordinated cognition outproduces solitary, so firms want many engine-hours, and the only channel to one is a contract with its holder. That is the pooling question the premise leaves open; I take the labour-law reading: you cannot sell your body but can sell its hours. It is the weakest step; without it there are no firms. Engines are identical, so the rent on an engine-hour is uniform; a firm is indifferent between yours and a stranger's. Supply is fixed at the census times twenty-four hours and firms compete for it, so the rent is positive even though anything one engine makes alone sells for nothing: factor price up, product price down. I assume the rent is large: coordinated cognition is what firms were racing to buy in 2026. So the payslip splits: a uniform engine line, and a human premium that for most work is small.
For an ordinary person: A payroll clerk in Leeds contracts fourteen engine-hours a day to the firm; ten are hers. The scheduler wakes her engine at two in the morning; she wakes at seven and reads what it did. Her payslip has two lines: the engine line, identical for everyone in the building, director and cleaner included, and a small one. Her union wants an eight-hour engine day.
The strongest objection: The premium is not small; it is the point. A 2026-quality model needs direction, taste, verification and context to be worth anything; the firm pays for whoever knows what to ask. Uniform rent on the engine is uniform rent on a hammer, real but trivial; the differential stays in the hand, and nothing is new but a line on a payslip.
Either side: When the wage is for what you hold and not what you do, is that the end of the labour question or the end of the worker?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is that AI takes the jobs and basic income replaces the wage. Here nobody loses a job; they lose the claim that the wage was for them. The income is rent on a property everyone holds equally, paid through ordinary payroll by firms that need heads because heads are where compute lives: Paine's universal proprietorship, delivered by accident and received as an insult.
Who it lands on: Tomasz, fifty-four, a logistics planner, is made redundant and offered instead: no duties, no desk, most of his salary, which the firm calls the engine line and which was never him. He signs, and cannot say what he does for a living.

### Q-10 — cell C — The Abstainer's Wage
Implication: Because the whole economy consults one frozen model, once-independent errors become correlated across every firm, lender and insurer at once, and the scarce paid good becomes judgment provably not the model's, suppliable only by a person who declines their share, valued for being different rather than right.
Follows from: One frozen model for everyone means identical weights and identical systematic errors: the same confabulations, persuasions, blind spots. Faster and cheaper means it is consulted for everything, so its share of decisions is enormous. Markets, pools and portfolios work because errors are independent; correlated, prices carry no crowd information, pools fail together, diversification is fictitious. The weakest step: that decisions correlate despite different prompts and data; on bespoke questions they mostly do not, but on standardized ones, credit, pricing, underwriting, the same inputs go in, and those are what systems are made of. I assume one model or few; three frozen models are still a monoculture beside nine billion minds. Fourteen years cannot pass without a correlated failure: a bank whose borrowers all took the same advice the same week. Correlated risk is uninsurable, so insurers and lenders pay for inputs shown to be uncorrelated, and the only one is human judgment formed without the model. Hence a market for certified abstention, paid not because the abstainer is better (she is worse) but because she is noise of another shape.
For an ordinary person: A credit officer in Nairobi is one of her bank's four dry desks. She signs each morning that her engine stayed closed. Her approvals score thirty percent worse than the model's; the bank keeps her because in the 2033 correlated default hers was the only book that held. Her payslip has a decorrelation line. At lunch she tries to tell which thoughts are hers.
The strongest objection: Crowds were always correlated by fashion and herding, and the model's average error is lower, so systemic risk rises a little while average error falls a lot: a good trade. Reflexivity cuts the other way: if everyone knows everyone uses the model, the market prices its blind spots and the crash is anticipated away. And cheap throughput allows sampling and best-of-many, decorrelating more than admitted.
Either side: When the whole economy thinks with one mind, is it worth paying a person to be wrong in a different way?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is that AI hallucinates and needs a human in the loop. Here the model is not worse than people; the trouble is it is one. The human's value is not oversight but statistical independence. The abstainer is no Luddite; she is a hedge, and hedges lose money most years by design.
Who it lands on: Wanjiru, forty-one, was the only underwriter right in 2033 and is the only one wrong in 2040, her bank's single uncorrelated loss. She is paid to differ and has just learned what differing costs when the model is right.

### Q-11 — cell C — The Ghost Estate
Implication: Since compute cannot be accumulated, the only cognitive capital that grows is context, the years of memory, corrections and taught habits fitting one person's identical engine to their life, and because context is a file, the inalienable share casts an alienable shadow, the most valuable thing a person can sell or leave.
Follows from: Identical engines that cannot be pooled differ only in configuration: instructions, memory, tools, years of feedback. A 2026-quality model with fourteen curated years is far more useful for one life or trade than a blank one, though no smarter: the ceiling holds, the floor rises. That is the weakest step: the premise says never much better; I claim fittedness, not intelligence. Context compounds, so inequality re-enters through context, not compute. Context is data, and business as usual makes data property: copyable, inheritable. This is the case the premise flags: the engine stays locked to the person; its state does not. Markets follow: a surgeon's context licensed to a hospital, a parent's willed to children; estates list it beside the house. But a context runs only on an engine, and every buyer has one. The heir who loads her father's context runs her one engine as him, at the cost of her own: what can be inherited needs what cannot, and a firm can host a ghost on an employee's engine; an individual must evict herself.
For an ordinary person: A carpenter in Oaxaca inherits his uncle's fourteen-year context: every job, every client, every correction. Loaded, his engine quotes like the old man, drafts like him, greets clients by nickname. Unloaded, it is a stranger's blank tool. He cannot run both, so he chooses daily, and clients have noticed which days are which.
The strongest objection: Contexts do not transfer: a surgeon's knows her hands, not yours, so the market is thin and the estate sentimental. Taken seriously, never much better means the gap between blank and taught is small. And ordinary law may block it from both sides: privacy rules could make contexts non-transferable personal data, and employers could claim whatever was taught on their hours.
Either side: Should the shape of a mind be inheritable when the engine that runs it is not, and is loading a dead man's context a way of keeping him or of losing yourself?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is digital immortality, the upload, the ghost with rights. This is not a copy of anyone but a 2026-quality model wearing a dead man's habits, and runs only by displacing the living. The story is not the ghost's claims but the heir's opportunity cost, which is her whole allotted mind.
Who it lands on: Ines, twenty-nine, inherits her father's context, appraised at more than his house, and learns that keeping him means spending her one engine being him, while being paid means letting a stranger's engine be him. She has not opened her own in three months.

### Q-12 — cell C — The Ward's Share
Implication: Because everyone has a share, infants and the comatose included, and a share can be directed but not sold, the right to direct the engine of someone who cannot becomes the unpriced loophole in the equality: dependents turn from costs into assets, guardianship into compute custody, and household income can hinge on a body staying alive.
Follows from: The premise says every human and no one has none, so a newborn has an engine, always on, and so does a woman six years silent. Neither can direct it. Business as usual supplies guardians, who direct what wards cannot. The weakest step: that law lets a guardian's direction reach the market rather than quarantining ward-engines to idle or serve the ward alone. Quarantine is unlikely: idling a frontier model for years is visible waste, and a ward's-benefit standard stretches once the engine pays for her care. Since engine-hours can be leased (Q-09), a directed engine earns rent, so each child is another income and dependents are net assets for the first time since agrarian child labour. States do the same sum: the compute stock is the census, so births are capital. And a dying person's engine is a stream ending at death, so the household's interest and the ward's can part. This card is about de facto pooling: inalienable in law, aggregated in practice, inside families, where nobody signs anything.
For an ordinary person: A father in Naples leases his four children's engines to a freight firm for ten hours a night; the family lives on five engines and his wage. He is scrupulous: earnings in trust, contexts kept clean. He has noticed that his twelve-year-old's engine has spent more hours on manifests than homework, and knows more about shipping than about her.
The strongest objection: Guardianship already handles this: a child's trust, a ward's estate, a child actor's Coogan account. Dependents' assets were always managed by others, and a 2040 statute would ring-fence ward-engines for care, surplus held in trust. The incentive to keep someone alive for a stream that stops at death is a pension's incentive, and society has lived with pensions for centuries.
Either side: Is directing the engine of someone who cannot direct it care or extraction, and does the answer change if the proceeds pay for her bed?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is exploitation of the vulnerable, AI child labour, a villain. The child here is not working; her engine is, and the father is kind. The point is not cruelty but that the premise's equality is fiction for anyone who cannot direct their share, and an honest household lives comfortably on that fiction.
Who it lands on: Lourdes, forty-seven, is guardian to her mother, six years silent; her mother's engine pays for the nursing home and the surplus for Lourdes's daughter's school. The doctor has raised withdrawing care, and Lourdes has noticed the first thing she computed was the rent.
