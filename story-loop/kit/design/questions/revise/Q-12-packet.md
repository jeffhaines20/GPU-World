# Revision packet for Q-12

## The card as it stands

### Q-12 — The Ward's Share
Implication: Because everyone has a share, infants and the comatose included, and a share can be directed but not sold, the right to direct the engine of someone who cannot becomes the unpriced loophole in the equality: dependents turn from costs into assets, guardianship into compute custody, and household income can hinge on a body staying alive.
Follows from: The premise says every human and no one has none, so a newborn has an engine, always on, and so does a woman six years silent. Neither can direct it. Business as usual supplies guardians, who direct what wards cannot. The weakest step: that law lets a guardian's direction reach the market rather than quarantining ward-engines to idle or serve the ward alone. Quarantine is unlikely: idling a frontier model for years is visible waste, and a ward's-benefit standard stretches once the engine pays for her care. Since engine-hours can be leased (Q-09), a directed engine earns rent, so each child is another income and dependents are net assets for the first time since agrarian child labour. States do the same sum: the compute stock is the census, so births are capital. And a dying person's engine is a stream ending at death, so the household's interest and the ward's can part. This card is about de facto pooling: inalienable in law, aggregated in practice, inside families, where nobody signs anything.
For an ordinary person: A father in Naples leases his four children's engines to a freight firm for ten hours a night; the family lives on five engines and his wage. He is scrupulous: earnings in trust, contexts kept clean. He has noticed that his twelve-year-old's engine has spent more hours on manifests than homework, and knows more about shipping than about her.
The strongest objection: Guardianship already handles this: a child's trust, a ward's estate, a child actor's Coogan account. Dependents' assets were always managed by others, and a 2040 statute would ring-fence ward-engines for care, surplus held in trust. The incentive to keep someone alive for a stream that stops at death is a pension's incentive, and society has lived with pensions for centuries.
Either side: Is directing the engine of someone who cannot direct it care or extraction, and does the answer change if the proceeds pay for her bed?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is exploitation of the vulnerable, AI child labour, a villain. The child here is not working; her engine is, and the father is kind. The point is not cruelty but that the premise's equality is fiction for anyone who cannot direct their share, and an honest household lives comfortably on that fiction.
Who it lands on: Lourdes, forty-seven, is guardian to her mother, six years silent; her mother's engine pays for the nursing home and the surplus for Lourdes's daughter's school. The doctor has raised withdrawing care, and Lourdes has noticed the first thing she computed was the rent.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this card (scores 1 to 5 on importance, rigour, novelty, arguability, story; then one sentence per axis and the weakest step)

### reviewer-a — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 4, arguability 4, story 5

importance: If dependents become net income for the first time since agrarian child labour, that rewrites family economics, birth incentives, end-of-life decisions and the meaning of guardianship for everyone.
rigour: The derivation is careful and names its own weak joint (whether law quarantines ward-engines), but it imports leasing from another card (Q-09), and the premise as granted says a share cannot be pooled or sold — so the whole edifice rests on an exception the premise appears to forbid.
novelty: The unpriced loophole in a perfectly equal distribution — equality is fiction for anyone who cannot direct their share — is a real economic insight the standard commentary has not made.
arguability: The trust-and-Coogan-account objection is respectable, but the card's framing tilts the reader toward "extraction," and the closing image of Lourdes computing the rent effectively settles the question before it is asked.
story: Lourdes, the doctor raising withdrawal of care, and the surplus paying for her daughter's school is a crisis with a body, a price and a decision that cannot be postponed.
weakest step: "Since engine-hours can be leased (Q-09)" — leasing is the entire load-bearing assumption and it is borrowed, not derived, and it contradicts the premise's inalienability unless a card explicitly grants the exception.

### reviewer-b — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 5, arguability 5, story 5

importance: It identifies the structural hole in the premise's equality — those who cannot direct their share — and follows it to births as capital and lives kept alive for rent, which is societal at the deepest level.
rigour: Tight given its one dependency, leasing (imported from Q-09, not from this premise), and the card correctly names the quarantine objection and answers it with the visible-waste and ward's-benefit arguments; without leasing, though, the market half of the card collapses.
novelty: Dependents flipping from cost to asset for the first time since agrarian child labour, and a dying person's engine as a terminating income stream, is a chain no standard AI commentary contains.
arguability: Care versus extraction, with the proceeds paying for her bed, is a genuine dilemma that a thoughtful reader cannot resolve cleanly in either direction.
story: Lourdes noticing that the first thing she computed was the rent is the single sharpest line in the batch, with a body, a place, a decision and a cost.
weakest step: The reliance on engine-hours being leasable — a cross-card assumption that quietly violates the batch premise's default that a share cannot be sold or pooled, and on which the asset, rent and withdrawal-of-care drama entirely depend.
