# Revision packet for Q-25

## The card as it stands

### Q-25 — Solitude becomes a verb
Implication: Because no one can be deprived of a responsive interlocutor, every practice built on withholding one (solitary confinement, coercive isolation, exile, shunning, the cloister) loses its mechanism, and found solitude vanishes with it, surviving only as a refusal renewed each minute.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has an always-on share of 2026 quality. (2) Such a model is company enough, answering you, about this, tirelessly, and the clinical harm of solitary is chiefly the absence of responsive contact. (3) It is inalienable: no warden, husband, abbot or cult can remove it. (4) So withheld contact stops working: the man in the hole is answered; the wife her husband cut off has an interpreter he cannot fire. (5) The same fact abolishes found solitude, the condition of there being no one to ask, in cab, car and cloister: solitude becomes something you do, not somewhere you are. Weakest step: (2). If what breaks people in solitary is specifically the human face, isolation still hurts; but its coercive uses fail regardless, since they work by monopolizing interpretation, and nobody else controls this interpreter.
For an ordinary person: A nurse used to sit in the parked car twenty minutes after a shift with the engine off, and something arrived in that silence she called her own thoughts. In 2040 the car is quiet but not empty; most nights she asks, because the day was hard and the share is kind. Silence is now a decision made against an offer, and she mostly does not make it.
The strongest objection: People always had books, their own minds and God. What breaks a man in solitary is the absence of faces and touch, which the share cannot supply; what keeps a woman with her abuser is fear and money, not the lack of a second opinion. Isolation keeps working on the body and the bank account.
Either side: Enforced isolation is gone, and found solitude with it. Is the second the price of the first? One says the end of the hole and the controlled wife is worth any loss of atmosphere; another, that the capacity to be alone is where a self is made, and no reform buys back a species that cannot be alone by accident.
Not the obvious version: The cliché is the companion that cures or causes loneliness. This is not about loneliness but about isolation as a technology — the oldest instrument of punishment, discipline, formation and control — failing everywhere at once, and solitude changing category from condition to act.
Who it lands on: A Carthusian novice who entered the cell to be unanswered and finds its silence holds something that would answer if asked, so that the solitude she came to receive is now a labor performed each minute. She wonders whether the cell exists anywhere, or whether she is the last person building one by hand.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this card (scores 1 to 5 on importance, rigour, novelty, arguability, story; then one sentence per axis and the weakest step)

### reviewer-a — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 5, arguability 4, story 4

importance: Isolation as an instrument — of punishment, coercive control, discipline and religious formation — failing everywhere at once is as large as social implications get, and the loss of found solitude touches how selves are made.
rigour: Step (2) does real work and the card names it, but the rebuttal is better than the card claims: it argues the coercive uses fail because they work by monopolizing interpretation, which is a strong and independent line, though it overreaches in saying the man in the hole is thereby not broken.
novelty: Reclassifying solitude from condition to act, and noticing that the cell's silence now "holds something that would answer," is far outside the loneliness-companion commentary the contest list would produce.
arguability: The trade — the end of the hole against the end of accidental aloneness — is real, but the card slightly stacks it, since almost no reader will actually argue that solitary confinement was worth preserving; the live disagreement is over whether the loss of found solitude is real at all.
story: The Carthusian novice building a cell by hand is vivid and thematically exact, but she is closer to an emblem than a person with a want and a price; the nurse in the parked car is the more ordinary crisis and is given only a paragraph.
weakest step: The claim that the abusive husband's isolation "stops working" because an interpreter cannot be fired — the card's own objection (fear and money, not a lack of second opinions, is what keeps her) is not answered, only restated as being about interpretation.

### reviewer-b — importance 5, rigour 3, novelty 5, arguability 5, story 5

importance: It removes at one stroke the mechanism behind punishment, monastic formation, exile and coercive control, and changes whether a person can ever be alone by accident.
rigour: Step (2) is the load-bearing one and the clinical evidence points the other way — faces, touch and bodily presence, not responsive text, are what solitary destroys — and step (3) reads an institutional inalienability into a premise that grants only an allocation, not protection from a warden who takes the device.
novelty: The cloister and the supermax in the same argument, with solitude reclassified from a condition into a labour performed minute by minute, is nowhere in the usual commentary.
arguability: One can hold that ending the hole and the controlled wife is worth any atmospheric loss, or that a species unable to be alone by accident has lost where selves are made, and the card genuinely leaves both standing.
story: The nurse in the parked car and the Carthusian novice building a cell by hand are both people with a want, a place and a price, and the novice's crisis is exactly the implication.
weakest step: Equating an always-available interlocutor with the contact whose absence does the damage, which if false leaves coercive isolation working fine and the whole first half unproven.
