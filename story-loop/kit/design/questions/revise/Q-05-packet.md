# Revision packet for Q-05

## The card as it stands

### Q-05 — The Unasked Question
Implication: When a competent reading of your own life is one typed sentence away, ignorance stops being a condition and becomes an act, and not-asking cannot be told apart, even from inside, from trust.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has, always on, a model that answers what it is asked at a thoughtful adult's level, from what it is told. (2) "I didn't know" has always sheltered two things: facts you could not get and inferences you did not draw. The model supplies no facts; it draws the inference, free, for everyone equally. That shelter is gone. (3) But asking now costs something new: to type "is he lying to me" is already to have made him a suspect. (4) Trust used to be a default, because checking was hard; here checking is free, so not-checking is a daily choice, and the only trust that costs anything. (5) So one act, walking past the door, is blindness when done from fear and trust when done from love, and she cannot tell which, since motive is exactly what she would have to ask about. Weakest step: (3)–(4), which assume asking damages trust; if asking becomes as neutral as looking something up, not-asking is negligence and the dilemma collapses into a duty.
For an ordinary person: Her husband says Thursday will be late. Before, she would half-wonder and let it go. Now the wondering has a door whose shape she can feel, and she notices herself not opening it. Her son's silence, her mother's balance, the mole on her shoulder: each has a door. She walks past five a day and calls it faith.
The strongest objection: Trust was never ignorance. To trust is not to refuse to look but to look without suspicion. Asking "is he all right?" is attention, not betrayal; ritual not-asking is the superstition that kept people from opening test results. And the model does not know whether he is lying; it weighs what she tells it, which she could have weighed in her own head.
Either side: Is there a duty to ask your model about the people you love, given that you can, or is not-asking the last act of trust a person can still perform?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the transparent society: nothing hidden, no excuses. Here no one is watched; the model sees only what its owner points it at. What changes is not the world's opacity but the ownership of one's own, and "did you know?" gains a third answer: "I didn't ask."
Who it lands on: A woman who for eleven years never put a question about her husband to her model, a discipline she privately called loyalty; then he is arrested, and the detective asks, gently, whether she really never asked. She sees that her trust and her blindness were one act, and will never know which she was performing.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this card (scores 1 to 5 on importance, rigour, novelty, arguability, story; then one sentence per axis and the weakest step)

### reviewer-a — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 5, arguability 5, story 5

importance: Converting ignorance from a condition into an act rewrites the moral standing of every "I didn't know" in a marriage, a family, a diagnosis and a workplace, which is as close to a whole-life question as the batch gets.
rigour: It follows tightly from the granted premise and nothing stronger — no superhuman model is needed, only a competent one that draws inferences from what it is told — and the card correctly locates and names its own soft joint at (3)–(4).
novelty: "Did you know?" acquiring a third answer, "I didn't ask," and the inversion that the change is in the ownership of one's own opacity rather than the world's, is the freshest move in the batch and nowhere near the surveillance cliché.
arguability: Duty-to-ask versus not-asking-as-the-last-costly-act-of-trust is a genuine two-sided question, and the objection that trust was never ignorance is strong enough to flip a reader mid-card.
story: A woman whose eleven-year discipline of not-asking is exposed by a gentle detective's question, and who will never know which act she was performing, is the best-shaped crisis here — want, price, place and an ending she cannot resolve.
weakest step: Step (3)'s claim that the asking itself constitutes suspicion, which assumes the question stays legible as an accusation rather than normalizing into a lookup, exactly as the card concedes.

### reviewer-b — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 4, arguability 5, story 5

importance: It touches the structure of trust in every close relationship, which is a whole life for nearly everyone, and does it without needing surveillance or any capability beyond the premise.
rigour: The derivation is the cleanest here and requires nothing stronger than a competent private reasoner, but it leans on the model's inference exceeding what she could weigh herself, which the objection names and the card does not rebut.
novelty: The right not to know has a bioethics literature, so the shape is not unseen, but relocating it from test results into marriage and giving "did you know?" the third answer "I didn't ask" is a real advance on it.
arguability: Duty to ask versus not-asking as the last performable act of trust is a question a thoughtful person could finish the card still undecided on.
story: The eleven-year discipline, the arrest, and the detective's gentle question give a person, a price and an irreversible moment where the implication lands.
weakest step: If the model only reweighs what she already tells it, the door she walks past is a psychological prop and the crisis is her own suspicion rather than anything the premise created.
