# Revision packet for Q-14

## The card as it stands

### Q-14 — The Rights Run
Implication: Universal expert counsel lets every citizen exercise every right at once, and the state discovers its guarantees were sized for, and underwritten by, the incapacity of the guaranteed; it must expand without limit or ration out loud.
Follows from: (1) A 2026 model drafts pleadings, appeals, asylum claims and benefit challenges at paralegal level; everyone has one. (2) The marginal cost of asserting a right falls to nearly nothing. (3) Every state system is sized to mass non-exercise: about 95% of American criminal cases plead out; most denials are never appealed. (4) Much of that non-exercise came from the cost of counsel — the weakest step, since some is preference, fear, or the trial penalty, which do not move. (5) But exercise need not reach 100% to break a system built for 5%; 20% quadruples the docket. (6) The state cannot screen the flood by quality, since every filing is expert-grade; it must screen by quota, fee, or queue — rationing by ignorance becomes rationing by rule, written down and defended. (7) "You have the right to X" acquires an audible clause: "though only N of you may use it each year."
For an ordinary person: A woman is offered a plea. Her model reads the file: thin evidence, winnable case. She refuses; so does most of the holding cell, whose models read the same files. An office that brought 40,000 cases with capacity to try 800 now faces 8,000 trials. She asserts her right to a speedy trial; the court answers, honestly: no capacity. She waits fourteen months in a queue that is now visible.
The strongest objection: The system will change how it rations, not how much. Plea rates are driven by the trial penalty and pretrial detention, not ignorance; defendants with superb lawyers plead too. Prosecutors will overcharge harder, courts raise fees and lengthen queues; the non-cognitive costs were always the larger part and do not fall. England already runs a lower plea rate with public counsel.
Either side: Is a right the state can only afford to honor for a few — and has always honored only for a few — a right, or a lottery ticket the state must now call one? Should it expand toward an unbounded commitment, or ration openly — honest, and the first formal admission the guarantee was never universal?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is "a free lawyer for everyone," the access-to-justice utopia. This is the bank run: universal access reveals the bank never held the deposits, and the run breaks the institution that promised them.
Who it lands on: A public defender fought her whole career for this — counsel for everyone, no plea taken from ignorance — and now watches clients rot in jail because the trials she won them the right to demand cannot be scheduled before 2043. What she wanted broke what she needed.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this card (scores 1 to 5 on importance, rigour, novelty, arguability, story; then one sentence per axis and the weakest step)

### reviewer-a — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 4, arguability 5, story 5

importance: It goes to whether constitutional guarantees mean anything, touches every citizen's relation to the state, and names a fiscal-institutional crisis with no small version.
rigour: The arithmetic is honest and the card pre-empts its own weak link (that cost of counsel, not the trial penalty, drives non-exercise) with the correct move — 20% suffices where the system was built for 5% — though it still under-argues why a paralegal-grade draft changes a defendant's decision when detention and the trial penalty, not ignorance, are doing the work.
novelty: The bank-run framing (universal access proves the deposits were never held) is a sharper and less-travelled reading than the access-to-justice utopia, though "AI floods the courts" has been sketched before in legal commentary.
arguability: Both horns — expand toward an unbounded commitment, or ration openly and admit the guarantee was never universal — are defensible and genuinely uncomfortable, with no rhetorical thumb on the scale.
story: The public defender who won the right she cannot get her clients scheduled for is a complete tragic engine: want, price, place, and irony that is structural rather than decorative.
weakest step: Step (4) still: the card asserts that some meaningful fraction of non-exercise was cost-of-counsel, when the strongest empirical evidence (well-lawyered defendants plead at high rates too) suggests the fraction may be small enough that the docket never quadruples.

### reviewer-b — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 4, arguability 5, story 4

importance: A state discovering its constitutional guarantees were budgeted on the poverty of the guaranteed is a whole-society reckoning that touches liberty, not a niche.
rigour: The chain is honest and self-auditing — it names the trial-penalty objection at step (4) and rescues itself with the correct arithmetic at (5), that a system sized for 5% breaks at 20% — though it still leans on the unargued claim that models at paralegal level materially change plea decisions rather than just paperwork volume.
novelty: Access-to-justice is a well-worn AI theme, but the inversion — universal access as a bank run that proves the deposits were never held — is a genuinely fresh framing of rights as an underfunded liability.
arguability: "Expand without limit or ration openly" is a real fork, and the honest-rationing side is genuinely attractive rather than a straw man, so a reader can end on either.
story: The public defender whose life's victory becomes her clients' fourteen-month wait is a person with a want and an exact price, though the material risks reading as procedural rather than intimate.
weakest step: Step (4) as the card admits, but more precisely step (6)'s assumption that the state cannot re-impose cost — it can raise the trial penalty, charge stacking, and pretrial detention, restoring the old equilibrium without ever writing a quota down, which dissolves the card's whole "audible clause."
