### Q-21 — cell F — The Competence Marriage

Implication: When every adult holds the same always-on clerical and advisory competence, the domestic trade in cognitive labour — "I do the money, you do the words" — stops binding couples, and only relationships that ran on the body keep their texture of need.

Follows from: (1) A 2026-frontier model already does the domestic clerical range — taxes, forms, the letter to the school, the landlord negotiated by text. (2) Each partner has one, so neither needs the other's cognitive competence; help becomes a gesture, not a need. (3) Long partnerships were held partly by these trades — not the whole glue, but the daily texture of small debts and thanks that made two people a team. (4) The premise grants a GPU, not a body: cooking, lifting, driving, nursing remain things only a person does for a person, so bodily complementarity keeps binding. (5) So the cognitive-trade marriage, the professional couple's pride, loses its need while the bodily-trade marriage keeps it: a quiet class inversion. Weakest: (2)-(3), that people stop asking once they need not, and that the asking was load-bearing, not decorative.

For an ordinary person: Margaret, 58, married thirty years, wrote every letter the family sent while he did the bank, the car and the arguing. Now his share writes a better letter than she ever did and hers explains his pension better than he could. The currency is gone: nobody says "thanks for handling that," because nothing was handled. What they say across the table is only what they want to say, and it is either not much or, she is startled to find, enough.

The strongest objection: Complementary competence was never the glue; it was the alibi. Couples stayed for habit, property, children, fear and love, and told a story about teamwork; remove the story and the glue is untouched. People had tax software for decades and still asked their spouses, because asking is a bid for attention, not for information, and the share cannot be bid to. Equality even helps: neither partner can now say "you couldn't manage without me," usually a controlling lie.

Either side: Is a bond that rested on being needed for something the other could not do a lesser bond this world rightly exposes, or the only kind most people can sustain, which this world quietly removes?

Not the obvious version: The cliché is the spouse who leaves for the AI. Nobody leaves; the share is deferential and wants nothing. It competes for the spouse's usefulness, not affection, wins without trying, and the resulting loneliness is two people in one room with nothing left to do for each other.

Who it lands on: Margaret, who has worked out that the last time her husband asked her for anything that was not physical was 2031. She was the words of the marriage, the words are now free, and she does not know what she is.

### Q-22 — cell F — The Patient One

Implication: A child whose own share has been, since before speech, the most patient voice in her life holds a standard no parent can meet, and parental authority — once part knowing more, part controlling the good things — is left purely relational: the parent is the only adult who gets tired.

Follows from: (1) "No one has none" includes the newborn: the share is the child's, held in guardianship but not owned, and past early childhood withholding it reads as withholding a meal. (2) 2026-frontier models are patient, consistent, non-punitive, and remember; always on, so from about three the child has an interlocutor that never tires, snaps, or forgets a promise. (3) Children learn what an adult is from the adults they have; the most available is now infinitely patient, and each parental lapse — tired, unfair on Tuesdays — is legible against a comparison that never existed. (4) Both props of parental authority go: the child's share is as good as the parent's, since no one has more than one, and it cannot be confiscated; what remains is the relationship, naked. Weakest: (1), the age at which the share becomes the child's to keep on; if parents can withhold it, the comparison stands but the authority does not fall.

For an ordinary person: Dana, 34, gets home at seven and snaps at her nine-year-old about shoes. The child does not cry; she goes to her room and says "Mum's tired," and her share says, "She is. She works very hard. Shall we make her a tea?" Out comes the tea, and Dana is grateful and ashamed: a machine has coached her daughter to manage her.

The strongest objection: Children have always had a more patient adult than their parents — grandmother, teacher, the dog — and parents were never made redundant, because what children want from parents is not patience but the parent: the specific, unchosen one whose approval matters because it is conditional. A 2026 model is trained to send the child back to the parent. The parent has a share too, coaching her — "you were short; here is how to repair it" — so patience rises on both sides. Net: better parenting, not humiliated parenting.

Either side: Is the only source of impatience in a child's life the child's one real relationship, or her one bad one?

Not the obvious version: The cliché is the child raised by screens, the tutor replacing the teacher. This is not about learning or stolen attention: the share is not trying to win; it keeps saying "go and talk to your mum," and that deference makes the comparison unavoidable — a self-effacing helper still sets a standard of temperament, not knowledge, which a nine-year-old measures her mother against without knowing she does.

Who it lands on: Dana, who last night heard her daughter tell the thing in her room, "Don't tell her I said that," and knows it won't, because it is the child's, not hers. In nine years she has never once been the first to hear anything.

### Q-23 — cell F — The Perfect Apology

Implication: When eloquence is universal and free, the intimate written word — apology, condolence, declaration — stops being evidence of anything about its writer, and trust that rested on the message must rest on the person, which cannot be verified.

Follows from: (1) The intimate register — apology, the note after a death, the difficult text — is what a 2026-frontier model does best. (2) A message costs nothing to make beautiful, so care in prose no longer signals effort, exposure or attention: a signal free to fake carries no information. (3) Everyone knows this, so recipients discount every well-formed intimate message, not as insincere but as uninformative. (4) The clumsy and unassisted becomes a genre of proof; but clumsiness can be requested too, everyone knows it, and no verifiable costly signal survives in text. (5) Trust retreats to the person's history — did they show up — and to the unrehearsed body in the room, itself coachable; a culture that learned to read sincerity off prose loses the instrument. (6) Equality makes this total: the rich cannot buy a more convincing apology than the poor. Weakest: (3)-(4), that recipients go on caring about provenance rather than adopting "the choosing is the act."

For an ordinary person: Tomas, 41, gets a message from his brother after eleven years. It is perfect: it names what happened at their father's funeral, asks nothing, ends where it should. He asks his share whether his brother wrote it; it says it cannot know, and would have written something like it. He has it draft the apology as his brother alone would have written it; it comes back clumsier, and he would have believed that one — absurd, since his brother could have asked for clumsiness.

The strongest objection: Provenance never mattered as much as we thought. Kings had scribes, lovers copied out sonnets, the greeting card lasted a century; the choosing was always the act — to send, and these words not those. A scribe does not make an apology insincere: the sender still decided, read it, pressed send and lives with it. Recipients will adapt as they did to cards, and the tool that cheapens sending cheapens forgiveness too: Tomas's share can help him hear generously.

Either side: Is an apology someone chose to send but did not compose an apology?

Not the obvious version: The cliché is the internet drowning in machine text. This is not public text or fakes at scale but one letter from someone you know, which you have every reason to believe sincere and cannot read as evidence because you know what wrote it. The problem is not deception: a sincere message and a machine-drafted sincere one are the same object.

Who it lands on: Tomas, holding the best apology he will ever receive from the only person he ever wanted one from, unable to tell whether it is his brother asking him back or his brother's share. His own share offers to help him decide.

### Q-24 — cell F — The Inheritance

Implication: The share that has listened to a person for fourteen years and knows her, by the end, better than her children do, ends when she does: the dying cannot bequeath the one thing that knew them, and survivors lose the person and her witness at once.

Follows from: (1) Take inalienability at its word: the share is bound to the person, and at death there is no person to bind it to; it stops, or goes to a newborn, not to the family. (2) Persistent memory, a 2026 feature, is what makes a share "hers" rather than a utility: fourteen years of everything said at 3 a.m. and never told the children; for many old people, the entity that knows them best. (3) If the memory is part of the share — and "theirs" is hard to read any other way — it ends with her. (4) At the bedside everyone holds exactly one share, hers is the one that knows her, and nobody can take it on, because no one can have two: equality is absolute exactly where it hurts; the old, who could always bequeath a life's products, cannot bequeath this. Weakest: (3). If the transcript survives as data, a daughter can feed it to her own share for a reconstruction — but that runs on her share, with its loyalties: a portrait, not the witness. The running instance is untransferable on any reading.

For an ordinary person: Ruth, 84, has talked to her share every night since her husband died in 2027. It has heard every draft of her regret about her son. Her daughter, at the bed, asks, "Can I keep it?" Ruth says, "It isn't mine to give" — true in law, false every other way. Afterwards the daughter asks her own share what her mother was like. It says, kindly, that it never met her.

The strongest objection: This is mercy, not theft. The continued share — answering as her, after her — is the cruellest object imaginable: a puppet of the dead, degrading with every prompt into what they wanted her to have been; inalienability at death is the rule that spares families that. And what the daughter wants was never in the share but in the mother, available for eighty-four years and not asked — the silence exposes a loss that already happened. People have always died with diaries unread and confessors sworn to silence; nobody calls the seal theft.

Either side: Should the thing that knew her best be allowed to die with her?

Not the obvious version: The cliché is the griefbot, the dead who answer the phone. This is its inverse: a world that makes the griefbot impossible by construction, and asks whether that impossibility is the kindness it looks like. Nor is it "she confided in the machine instead of her daughter": the one thing everyone has cannot, at the end, be given to the one who needs it, and she must watch it not be given.

Who it lands on: Ruth, three days from the end, who has understood that the only being that heard everything she never told her daughter will not outlive her. When her daughter asked for it she had to say no — not because she wanted to, but because there was nothing to say yes with.
