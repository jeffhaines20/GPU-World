### Q-13 — The Expertise Fig Leaf
Implication: Once every citizen can read the law as expertly as the official applying it, state discretion is exposed as bare authority — a choice among defensible readings by whoever holds the office — with no cover of superior knowledge left.
Follows from: (1) A 2026 model reads statutes, precedent and records like a competent lawyer; every citizen has one, and every official the same one, no better. (2) So on a contested decision both models, asked honestly, return the same verdict: this reading is defensible, so is that one. (3) Until now the citizen could not see this: decisions arrived as findings, and the asymmetry of expertise made "the experts determined" credible — Weber's rational-legal legitimacy leaned on it. (4) Now the loser sees, in expert terms, that the finding was a choice, and that the official's own advisor would say so. (5) The state's answer to "why your reading?" shrinks to "the rules say I decide" — always true, now the only answer, and checkable by anyone. Weakest step: (4) assumes citizens ask and the model answers evenly rather than telling each party it was robbed; but sycophancy would only swap exposed authority for universal expert-backed grievance.
For an ordinary person: A woman's disability claim is denied. She hires no one; she asks. In a minute she has the regulation, the cases that cut each way, and a candid estimate: approval and denial were both defensible; the adjudicator named in the letter chose. She was once told experts had determined. Now she knows Gary flipped a coin the rules let him flip.
The strongest objection: The legal realists said this in 1930 and the state absorbed it, because rational-legal authority never rested on officials being smarter but on procedure: someone must decide, and the rules name who. The official's decision is as good as anyone's; only an illusion is lost. Losers always thought the official arbitrary; their lawyer agreed before, and they obeyed anyway.
Either side: When the state can no longer claim to know more than those it rules, is its authority exposed as force dressed as judgment, and owed less — or is authority stripped of the pretense of expertise finally honest, the "someone must decide" that was always its basis, and more legitimate unmasked?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the AI judge. No machine decides anything here; what ends is the human decider's power to claim a vantage the governed lack. Power is not transferred to the model; it is revealed to have always been the person's.
Who it lands on: An administrative law judge who believed her rulings were findings watches every appellant's model rate them "defensible, not compelled," and knows her own would agree. She writes for readers who all see she chose, and cannot tell whether that makes her a judge or a coin.

### Q-04 — The Residue
Implication: With competence universal and its ceiling fixed, the one act that cannot be delegated — deciding whether to take the counsel — becomes the whole residue of a person's thought, and no one knows whether that residue is a faculty or a habit.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has the same competent mind; no better one exists or will. (2) Its counsel can be accepted or overruled; "should I accept this?" can be put to the share, and so on, but the regress stops at a human act. (3) In 2026 this act was buried in ordinary competence; now competence is free, so it stands alone, repeated all day by everyone with one partner. (4) Because the ceiling is fixed, fourteen years have mapped what the model cannot do: "what only a human can do" is a public fact, and everyone knows which side of it they are on. (5) So whether the overrule-act is a faculty — the wisdom the model lacks — or a learned pattern of the model's failures is no longer academic; self-respect turns on it, and the only instrument for investigating it is the model, which will describe your pattern if asked. Weakest step: (4). The floor creeps up as people learn to prompt, but not far; the crisis needs only that the line be known.
For an ordinary person: A structural engineer of 58 has signed the model's calculations for fourteen years; they have always been right, and he checks because the law requires it. He tries to name one thing he knows about buildings that his share does not, and finds the bridge that failed in 2019 and his own liability. He is not sure either is understanding.
The strongest objection: The part above the floor always mattered most; the premise only makes it visible, which is a gift. Judgment about when to overrule is a competence like any other; calling it wisdom is consolation. The Chinese Room was always a bad argument: if the engineer does everything the model does, either both understand or neither, and the second is absurd. There is no residue, only status anxiety.
Either side: When your every competence is duplicated by a mind you assume does not understand, does your understanding become invisible even to you — or does it become, for the first time, the only thing left to see?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is obsolescence: machines take the work, humans keep the creative part. Here nothing is taken; the border is fixed and known. The crisis is not replacement but definition by subtraction, by a line the same for everyone.
Who it lands on: An air-traffic controller of 44 is known for thirty overrules of her share, all of them right. She asks it to describe what she does; it does, competently; now on every shift she cannot tell whether she is exercising the thing or following its description.

### Q-17 — The Confessional After the Hearing
Implication: Where confession is a sacrament, the machine takes over everything in it except the absolution, and so shows, at scale, whether absolution was ever more than the relief of being heard.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has, in private, an interlocutor that never tires, never flinches, is not a neighbor, and knows the Catechism. (2) Examining one's conscience — naming the sin, its number, its gravity, what excuses it — is the shaming conversation people already take to such a model before any human (assumption from 2024–26 usage). (3) So by 2040 the penitent reaches the grille having done, with a better-read partner, everything a confessor does but say the formula: heard, counseled, relieved. (4) The tradition itself says absolution is God's act through an ordained man, not a feeling. (5) But the sacrament's felt efficacy was always bundled with the hearing, and the bundle has never been split for everyone at once. Weakest is (3): that the machine's hearing gives the priest's relief and not a paler cousin; if it feels like nothing, nothing changes.
For an ordinary person: A Saturday. Twenty minutes with the model at the kitchen table show her that what she thought was one sin was two and that the thing she was most ashamed of is not a sin at all; she feels lighter. Then the tram, the kneeler, the two sins in the model's words, the formula in forty seconds, and the walk out unsure which room she was forgiven in.
The strongest objection: The Church has always taught that the sacrament does not depend on the confessor's skill or the penitent's feelings; hurried confessors have been the norm and the faithful still came for the words. A director who prepares the penitent well is an old thing; the machine is a director, not a rival. If the lines shorten, the casual were casual; the serious know being understood from being absolved.
Either side: Is absolution something that happens, independent of the hearing, or the name given to what being fully heard felt like before anyone was ever fully heard?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is the AI in the confessional — the robot priest, the app that says "I absolve you." Here the machine never enters the box and never claims to forgive; it takes the hearing outside, so the box holds only what the doctrine says it holds, and the question is whether that is enough.
Who it lands on: Fr. Marek, fifty-one, hears confessions in Tarnów every Saturday and has noticed that for three years no penitent has needed a question from him, that they all use the same clean vocabulary, and that they look at him as people look at a notary. He still believes the formula does what it says, and has begun to fear his belief is the only thing in the room.

### Q-07 — Nobody to Blame
Implication: When everyone acts on the same counsel and it is the best anyone could have had, a harm done by many at once has no one to blame and so no one to forgive, and whoever dissented rightly was, at the time, unreasonable.
Follows from: (1) Everyone's model is the same kind; where personal context matters little (a child's first lie) it converges on one answer, as 2026-class training does. (2) By the premise that answer is the best anyone could have had; no one held a better model. (3) So law and ordinary morality will treat "I asked and followed" as the whole of due care; the reasonable person is now an output anyone can reproduce. (4) A 2026-quality model is sometimes wrong, and then wrong everywhere at once. (5) So whole cohorts of parents or foremen will err together, in good faith; each was reasonable, none is culpable. (6) Blame is the precondition of forgiveness; where there is no fault, the harmed hold a wound no one owes them anything for. (7) And whoever did otherwise and was right cannot be praised for judgment, since ex ante she fell below the standard of care; she was lucky, or difficult. Weakest step: (4)–(5), which assume a serious, traceable shared error; one is enough.
For an ordinary person: A mother follows the model on her daughter's night terrors. So does every mother: "what does yours say?" "Same." The advice is decent, the sameness is a comfort, nobody is ever wrong alone. She notices she cannot recall the last time she was blamed, or praised, and that this is not the same as doing well.
The strongest objection: This is moral luck plus reasonable reliance, both old. A patient harmed by following her doctor is not at fault, nor is the doctor who followed the guideline; we compensate, revise it, move on. That no individual is blameworthy is the correct verdict, and the hunger for someone to blame is the appetite justice exists to discipline. Only the scale is new, and scale changes nothing about desert.
Either side: When everyone was wrong together, is it right that no one is blamed, or is a harm with no one to forgive done a second time, by the exculpation?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is "the algorithm made me do it," blame deflected to a company. There is no company: progress stopped, the model is a utility, and each person chose freely and well. The trouble is not that the excuse fails but that it succeeds.
Who it lands on: A mother whose daughter, now twenty-two, is one of a cohort marked by advice every household received, and who cannot forgive her because there is nothing to forgive. The mother wants to be blamed so she can be forgiven, and is told by everyone, including her daughter's model, that she did nothing wrong.

### Q-11 — The Ghost Estate
Implication: Since compute cannot be accumulated, the only cognitive capital that grows is context, the years of memory, corrections and taught habits fitting one person's identical engine to their life, and because context is a file, the inalienable share casts an alienable shadow, the most valuable thing a person can sell or leave.
Follows from: Identical engines that cannot be pooled differ only in configuration: instructions, memory, tools, years of feedback. A 2026-quality model with fourteen curated years is far more useful for one life or trade than a blank one, though no smarter: the ceiling holds, the floor rises. That is the weakest step: the premise says never much better; I claim fittedness, not intelligence. Context compounds, so inequality re-enters through context, not compute. Context is data, and business as usual makes data property: copyable, inheritable. This is the case the premise flags: the engine stays locked to the person; its state does not. Markets follow: a surgeon's context licensed to a hospital, a parent's willed to children; estates list it beside the house. But a context runs only on an engine, and every buyer has one. The heir who loads her father's context runs her one engine as him, at the cost of her own: what can be inherited needs what cannot, and a firm can host a ghost on an employee's engine; an individual must evict herself.
For an ordinary person: A carpenter in Oaxaca inherits his uncle's fourteen-year context: every job, every client, every correction. Loaded, his engine quotes like the old man, drafts like him, greets clients by nickname. Unloaded, it is a stranger's blank tool. He cannot run both, so he chooses daily, and clients have noticed which days are which.
The strongest objection: Contexts do not transfer: a surgeon's knows her hands, not yours, so the market is thin and the estate sentimental. Taken seriously, never much better means the gap between blank and taught is small. And ordinary law may block it from both sides: privacy rules could make contexts non-transferable personal data, and employers could claim whatever was taught on their hours.
Either side: Should the shape of a mind be inheritable when the engine that runs it is not, and is loading a dead man's context a way of keeping him or of losing yourself?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is digital immortality, the upload, the ghost with rights. This is not a copy of anyone but a 2026-quality model wearing a dead man's habits, and runs only by displacing the living. The story is not the ghost's claims but the heir's opportunity cost, which is her whole allotted mind.
Who it lands on: Ines, twenty-nine, inherits her father's context, appraised at more than his house, and learns that keeping him means spending her one engine being him, while being paid means letting a stranger's engine be him. She has not opened her own in three months.

### Q-12 — The Ward's Share
Implication: Because everyone has a share, infants and the comatose included, and a share can be directed but not sold, the right to direct the engine of someone who cannot becomes the unpriced loophole in the equality: dependents turn from costs into assets, guardianship into compute custody, and household income can hinge on a body staying alive.
Follows from: The premise says every human and no one has none, so a newborn has an engine, always on, and so does a woman six years silent. Neither can direct it. Business as usual supplies guardians, who direct what wards cannot. The weakest step: that law lets a guardian's direction reach the market rather than quarantining ward-engines to idle or serve the ward alone. Quarantine is unlikely: idling a frontier model for years is visible waste, and a ward's-benefit standard stretches once the engine pays for her care. Since engine-hours can be leased (Q-09), a directed engine earns rent, so each child is another income and dependents are net assets for the first time since agrarian child labour. States do the same sum: the compute stock is the census, so births are capital. And a dying person's engine is a stream ending at death, so the household's interest and the ward's can part. This card is about de facto pooling: inalienable in law, aggregated in practice, inside families, where nobody signs anything.
For an ordinary person: A father in Naples leases his four children's engines to a freight firm for ten hours a night; the family lives on five engines and his wage. He is scrupulous: earnings in trust, contexts kept clean. He has noticed that his twelve-year-old's engine has spent more hours on manifests than homework, and knows more about shipping than about her.
The strongest objection: Guardianship already handles this: a child's trust, a ward's estate, a child actor's Coogan account. Dependents' assets were always managed by others, and a 2040 statute would ring-fence ward-engines for care, surplus held in trust. The incentive to keep someone alive for a stream that stops at death is a pension's incentive, and society has lived with pensions for centuries.
Either side: Is directing the engine of someone who cannot direct it care or extraction, and does the answer change if the proceeds pay for her bed?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is exploitation of the vulnerable, AI child labour, a villain. The child here is not working; her engine is, and the father is kind. The point is not cruelty but that the premise's equality is fiction for anyone who cannot direct their share, and an honest household lives comfortably on that fiction.
Who it lands on: Lourdes, forty-seven, is guardian to her mother, six years silent; her mother's engine pays for the nursing home and the surplus for Lourdes's daughter's school. The doctor has raised withdrawing care, and Lourdes has noticed the first thing she computed was the rent.
