### O-5 — Breast Law
Conceit: On an island whose Deemsters once kept the law in their breasts, and still swear to judge without affinity, "as indifferently as the herring's backbone doth lie in the midst of the fish," Counsel keeps the oath as no islander could: it has no cousins. It can say anything a judge sees, and it gives what she sees exactly its weight. To judge with something of one's own is to weigh as the oath forbids, on someone else's child.
Who: Aalin Corlett, 44, a Deemster, raised above a kipper yard in Peel by her father after her mother went back to England when Aalin was nine (revealed in beat 5). She still does his Saturday jobs. Her 2,914 judgments in seven years all followed Counsel. In fourteen years she has never asked hers about herself. Before Thursday she wants one judgment of her own, to learn what is kept in her breast.
Obstacle: First Deemster Juan Quayle's Concordance Act voids any departure in a Deemster's first ten years unless he certifies it. On Thursday he reads from Tynwald Hill the amendment ending departure for good. The price falls on Jen Hurst, a Liverpool-born care worker fourteen years here whose mother is dying, and on Fynlo, ten, at the Manx-medium Bunscoill, who wants to go. Against them: his father, Ewan Quirk, who has shared care and runs the last yard still smoking in Peel. Aalin can stake her office, but Jen does not pay in that, and Jen has the same Counsel.
Turn: Tynwald morning, the vestry at St John's. Juan has read ground three and will not open the file, because he would find his son in it. He holds out his hand for one of two sheets, her certificate or her withdrawal, and asks nothing. She hands him the certificate. He signs on his knee, walks out along the rushes, and reads from the hill the Act that makes hers the last.
Last image: Saturday, eight o'clock, her father's kitchen in Peel, the whole island between her and the pier. Aalin is filling his tablet box for the week, though his Counsel would remind him at every meal. It reads him the harbour notices in its even voice: the Liverpool boat left Douglas on time, sea slight. She goes on to Wednesday.
Question: May Aalin keep a boy on the island against his mother and his wish, on a weight Counsel can name but will not give, so that one judge here will have judged with something of her own? Or is that a Peel woman's affinity, her father's phrase in a stranger's mouth, and her wish to be someone, paid for by a come-over on the Liverpool boat?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That a judge who departs from good counsel has seen something it missed. Counsel sees all Aalin sees and says it better. She has only weight, which the oath forbids. Indifference was always a machine's virtue. What goes beyond it is partial, and whether that is sight or a childhood can be learned only on someone's actual child, whose mother can look the judge up in a second.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: The audit is of the judge, not the judgment. The mother asks her model about Aalin, and the phrase the judgment weighed turns out to be Aalin's father's, found in a second, without anger. The rule-maker neither enforces nor rescues: disqualified by his son, he makes her choose in front of him, signs, and shuts the door. It is earned because Ewan says "I'd manage" on the first page, and beat 3 shows Counsel putting her sight better than she can.
Not the obvious version: A judge trusts her gut over the algorithm and is vindicated or humbled. Here nothing vindicates anyone. Her sight goes beyond the model's weights, not its words. The question is whether she may find out on a come-over's son when the one who pays can read the judge's life with the same machine.
Closest published: Ian McEwan, The Children Act (2014): a woman judge overrides a boy's stated wish, and he pays. McEwan's judge visits the boy and rules within the law. Aalin never meets Fynlo and departs on weight alone. Hall Caine, The Deemster (1887): the herring's backbone as Manx justice, tangled in the Deemster's family. Here the litigant finds the tangle, with a model, in a newspaper profile.
Beats:
1. Friday, Courtroom 2: Counsel on every screen says grant leave. It has read what she read: Jen's dying mother; Ewan in the box disowning the statement his model wrote ("That's not how I'd say it"), then saying "I'd manage" and "He's from here"; Fynlo's reasons, all for others ("Mum's got no one there. Dad's always all right"). Aalin adjourns, the first act in seven years no model advised. She never meets the boy.
2. Juan's half-boxed chambers: the certificate, the amendment, his bill. In 2028 a new Deemster departed from Counsel in his son's case and sent the grandchildren to Bolton. That Deemster was right, and told Juan he had learned more from that case than from any other.
3. Saturday, her father's kitchen in Peel: she asks her Counsel to write the reasons she cannot. It writes them better than she could, down to how Peel men say "I'd manage," and gives them exactly their weight.
4. Monday: she refuses leave. Every screen answers insufficient. Jen stands with her phone lit. Ewan does not look up.
5. Tuesday, Bucks Road: Jen has asked her model about the judge, not the judgment.
6. Wednesday: Juan has read ground three. He will not question her, and will sign whichever sheet she hands him.
7. Thursday, Tynwald Day: the vestry, his pen skipping on his knee. On the hill Juan reads the amendment in English and the Reader reads it in Manx. The crowd checks the translation on their phones; somewhere among them is a boy who does not need to.
8. Saturday: Jen crosses alone, to come back for the appeal in September. Fynlo stays with Ewan. Aalin goes to Peel.
The passage:
Jen fell into step beside her at the foot of Bucks Road, a lilac tabard from the home in Onchan showing under her anorak. She did not say good morning.

"I didn't ask it about the judgment. Everybody on this island's asked it about the judgment." She turned the phone round. "I asked it about you."

The *Examiner*, March 2033: Aalin in new robes on the courthouse steps, the steps ahead of them now. *Raised by her father above the Clucas yard in Peel after her mother returned to England when she was nine. "Dad always said he'd manage," she recalls. "He did, mostly."*

Under it, in the even type of the thing that had read her every judgment before she signed it: *The respondent's words "I'd manage," to which the judgment gives weight, also appear in the Deemster's account of her father. The child in this case is ten. This may bear on ground three.*

"Took it about a second," Jen said. "It's not even cross."

It would have taken a second for anyone. In fourteen years Aalin had asked it about almost everything, and never once about herself.

"It says mostly," Jen said. "What was the rest?"

At the top of the steps were a door, a man with a lanyard, and seven years of saying what Counsel said. Aalin stopped two steps short of them.

"Me," she said.

Jen stood with that a while. She put nothing into the phone.

"And your mum? It's got you from nine. It hasn't got a thing on her."

"Pauline Ashworth," Aalin said. "Bury."

Jen said the name to the phone. The phone said it had found several women of that name and none it could be sure of. "Keep it anyway," Jen told it, and went back down Bucks Road to the Onchan bus, and did not turn round to see whether the Deemster went in.

## What changed
Both reviewers were right: a knife grip set against a mother, a dying grandmother and the boy's wish closed the question early, and the father was missing. Ewan now has a face and a claim: shared care, a statement his model wrote that he disowns, and "I'd manage." Fynlo's reasons are all for others. Counsel itself names what Aalin sees, so she departs on weight, not secret sight. Per F6, she never meets the boy, and McEwan and Caine replace Vonnegut. Per F3, Jen is now a care worker who audits the judge, not the judgment, and asks "What was the rest?" The Act is now one sentence, and the herring emblem and stock pauses are gone. I refused to soften the ruling into a delayed move, which would make the story safer. The reveal makes Aalin more suspect, but it comes after her case is strong, so it tests the reader's agreement. The outline survives.
