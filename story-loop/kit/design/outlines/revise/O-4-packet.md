# Revision packet for O-4

## The outline as it stands

### O-4 — Eleven Fathers
Conceit: For eleven years a father has kept every earpiece in a bowl by the door, so that he, not the counsel, would know his children. His daughter runs her childhood through the counsel he never walked three steps to ask, gets a different reasonable father each run, and finds one thing holds steady: the curve of his apprenticeship, with her at the bottom and her brother at the top.
Who: Tess Haworth, 19, Hebden Bridge; a night care assistant in Halifax who follows every care plan to the letter. On Saturday she leaves for nurse training in Leeds, and leaves her brother Kit, 12, with their father, Rob, a tree surgeon. She wants one bill she believes (raised, or practised on?) to hand Rob first. Nightly she takes her bud from the bowl after he goes up, breaking the house's one rule, and reads a better childhood than hers.
Obstacle: The audit will not hold still: eleven runs, eleven fathers. No better auditor can be bought or borrowed, since every opinion she could ask runs on the same model, and her own judgment was made in the house she is auditing. And the debt bought something: the father now handling Kit is the one she paid for. To collect is to spend it.
Turn: She takes Kit's bud from the bowl in front of her father, who watches and does not stop her, and puts it in Kit's hand. The house where no one asked becomes one where the next child can check every word his father says; Kit cannot unlearn that a father's words are one try among several.
Last image: From the door, bag in hand: Kit's bud on the breakfast table between the cereal box and the milk, its small green light on, neither Kit nor Rob touching it, while Rob talks about the 9:14.
Question: Was her father entitled to learn, on her, to be the man who knew his children (she paid, she can read the bill, and the man who stops himself mid-sentence for Kit is what she bought), or was it vanity charged to a child who could not be asked?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That the harm of being someone's practice is the mistakes, which an audit can total; here harm and gift are one thing, since a child who cannot check her father's words grows round them, and what shields the next child from a father's errors also shields him from being known.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: The reader waits for the list to be read to the father and it never is: the climax is overheard through a wall, and the bill is paid forward to a brother their father may just have handled well. Because the bowl, the wobbling audit, the sentence she grew round and the curve come first, the hand-off reads as both protecting Kit and collecting from Rob, and she cannot say which.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version: the grown child reads the model's list to her father; he defends himself or breaks; they reconcile or part. Here the audit never yields a bill she believes, he never sees the list, and what she presents instead is his broken rule, in front of him, on someone else's behalf. Nor is it atrophy: his judgment is real; the question is who paid.
Closest published: "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling," Ted Chiang: a father checks his memory of raising his daughter against a lifelog and finds he was the one who wronged her. There the audit is of fact, from the parent's side; here it is counterfactual, from the child's, run on the counsel the father refused, and the question is whether it was his to try.
Beats:
1. Tuesday, 3 a.m., after a night shift, bud in against the rule: the eleventh run, forty-one entries, each beside what a reasonable father would have done. The eleventh father is kinder to Rob than the tenth, and she stops trusting either.
2. The worst entry: Boots in Halifax at thirteen, then the van: "I know why you did it. You wanted me to have to come and get you." The model offers three other reasons; she cannot tell whether his fits because it was true or because she has told her life in his words. Its father for that night says it better, to a girl who no longer exists. The cost moves from what happened to what grew.
3. Wednesday, groundwork for Rob on a beech in Heptonstall: he ignores the client's model's felling plan and lays the limb between a greenhouse and a trampoline; his saw sparks on fence wire deep in the trunk. "Grew round it." She sees how a master is made, nearly tells him, doesn't.
4. Wednesday night: the model finds the curve, his departures worse than counsel early and better later, Kit's years top right. The credits: fetched from a party at fifteen, a silent drive, toast; the model calls it "comparable to typical advice." It was the silence. Its bill is not hers either.
5. Thursday: the Co-op rings; Kit has a tin of peaches in his coat. Rob goes to get him. Through the wall, the bud listening with her, the old sentence starts, stops, becomes a question; a ring-pull; two forks. The model grades him well. Nobody, Rob included, knows if it was right.
6. Kit's room: syrup on his chin. "Why did I do it?" An answer rises in her, in her father's cadence. She does not give it.
7. Downstairs: Rob rinsing two forks; in the bowl, his light, Kit's, and the empty place that was hers. She takes Kit's; he watches and lets her. Upstairs: "It'll give you three reasons. None of them are mine." Kit's fingers close on it. Nothing is ever said to Rob.
8. Saturday morning: the last image.
The passage:
Through the wall she heard the bed take his weight. It had been her room until she was eleven; she knew which board his heel was on.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this outline

### reviewer-a — question 5, surprise 4, insight 5, grip 4, person 5, ending 4, prose 4 (total 31 of 35)

question: Both sides are acted out, not argued: the sentence Tess "grew round" is the apprentice's debt, the silent drive and the two forks are what it bought, and handing Kit his bud in front of Rob is protection and collection at once, and nothing settles it.
surprise: The reader waits for the list to be read to Rob and it never is, and arming Kit in front of a father who lets her is prepared by the bowl but impossible to foresee; still, once the Boots entry lands, a reader can guess the younger brother will repeat it.
insight: That the harm of being practiced on is not a sum of errors but a formation the auditor is made of ("the cost moves from what happened to what grew"), and that what shields the next child from a father's mistakes also shields him from being known, is a real reversal, sharpened by a model that returns eleven different reasonable fathers.
grip: The Saturday departure, the nightly broken rule and the overheard peaches scene hold hard, but beats 1, 2 and 4 are Tess alone at 3 a.m. reading model output, and a reader could drift before the Co-op rings.
person: Tess, a night care assistant who follows care plans to the letter, doing groundwork under a beech in Heptonstall for her tree-surgeon father and standing with her hand flat on the wallpaper of her old room, is a particular body in a particular valley.
ending: Kit's bud glowing between the cereal box and the milk, touched by neither while Rob talks about the 9:14, is earned and open: a new third party at the table, though the ignored object on the table is a familiar literary close.
prose: "Tines on metal, two sets, not quite together" and the tin he paid for at the till are first-rate, but the radiator-ticked, fridge-came-on pause is workshop stock, and the long penultimate sentence explains what the scene has already shown.
largest gap: The climax rests on Kit stealing, in Tess's last week, just as she did at thirteen, and that mirror coincidence makes the story's hinge feel arranged rather than found.

## The originality critic on this outline

**O-4 Eleven Fathers.** (1) Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" (2013), the outline's own choice. Same: a child's grievance against a father is put to a machine. Different: this audit is counterfactual, run from the child's side, unstable from run to run, and never delivered. (2) Chiang, "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom" (2019). Same: consulting machine-given alternatives to one's own life, nightly envy of the better version ("reads a better childhood than hers"), and finding that the alternatives settle nothing. Different: Chiang's prisms open onto real branches; Tess's eleven fathers are a model's guesses, and the subject is a father's apprenticeship, not free will. (3) Gawande, "The Learning Curve" (2002). "The curve of his apprenticeship, with her at the bottom and her brother at the top" is Gawande's learning curve with the eldest child as the early cases; the difference is that his is nonfiction about surgeons. (4) Alexander, "The Whispering Earring" (2012). The bowl by the door is a household refusing the always-right whisperer, and the outline's "Nor is it atrophy" answers the Earring's premise directly. Verdict: in conversation with Chiang, knowingly; otherwise familiar furniture, new house (MINOR at most). Three things are new to me: the climax overheard through a wall, the list never read to the father, and the debt paid forward to the brother instead of presented. In premise it is O-1's sibling.

**Between the outlines.** O-1 and O-3 are one story twice. In both, a father keeps a paper record of his judgments about his child, one book a year, written at night. The grown child (19 in O-1, 23 in O-3) spends the last night before leaving by boat for a working life (deckhand; junior doctor) scoring the books at the kitchen table, as "Right"/"Wrong" or as ticks and crosses. The deciding entry is a teenage episode where the father guessed what was happening and stayed away. The child's answer is that the father's coming was the thing dreaded ("Because you'd have come"; "if Dad comes up those stairs into the gallery I will die"). Both close on the kitchen table after the child has gone, with a line left blank and a pen held over it (O-1) or lying across it (O-3). The only real difference is the father's sign: Wade always departed from the model, Magnus never did. That mirror is interesting, but it does not make two stories. O-4 tells the same premise a third time. Its plot departs furthest from O-1, but it keeps O-1's main elements: the father who never asked the counsel, the 19-year-old leaving home, the learning curve with the child's early years at the bottom, a theft as a key entry, and the question "raised, or practised on?" word for word. O-2 and O-5 are one skeleton twice. In both, a practitioner sits at one end of a concordance scale (Noor's 97.1 is the province's lowest; Aalin's 2,914 judgments all agreed with Counsel). A retiring elder who knows from the inside what a departure costs lets one happen (Marga's five women moved for nothing; Juan's son and grandchildren, spent on another Deemster's education). The one departure falls on a stranger and rests on a perception that cannot be put into words. Then a working woman who reads instruments for a living ("the way she would read a gauge"; "the look she must give a monitor at four in the morning") runs her own model's fair review and asks whether the practitioner knows ("Do you know now?"; "Do you know you're right?"). The two passages are that same scene. All five end on a verdict withheld: a pen held over the line; "Again"; a tick crossed through and a last line left blank; "could not tell"; "No". The passages also share one writer's reflexes. Four cut from the hard line to an ambient sound ("The furnace came on"; "The Rayburn ticked"; "The radiator ticked. Downstairs the fridge came on."; "Rain ticked on the roofs of the cars"). Three have a hard line said flatly "the way" a job reads things (a gauge, a potassium, the wind getting up). Two have someone who "did not remember" a small act of the hand (O-1, O-4). Two have someone look "at the window, where" (O-1, O-2). Three of the five name the same Chiang story as their closest neighbour. That choice is wrong for O-2, and O-3 and O-5 miss nearer neighbours (F5–F7).
