### O-5 — The Herring's Backbone
Conceit: On an island whose judges still swear to judge "as indifferently as the herring's backbone doth lie in the midst of the fish," a model keeps that oath perfectly and a statute makes keeping it compulsory. The only way to become a judge who sees past the middle is to be partial, on a live case, with someone else's child.
Who: Aalin Corlett, 44, a Deemster of the Manx High Court in Douglas, raised above a kipper yard in Peel. In seven years she has given 2,914 judgments, and every one agreed with Counsel. Before the First Deemster climbs Tynwald Hill for the last time on Thursday, she wants to give one judgment of her own: to learn whether anything is kept in her breast, where Deemsters were once said to keep the law.
Obstacle: First Deemster Juan Quayle's Concordance Act (2031): in a Deemster's first ten years a departure from Counsel is void unless he certifies it, and on Thursday he reads from the hill the amendment ending departure for every Deemster, for good. The oath forbids "affinity," and her sight may be only Peel. The price falls on Jen Hurst, a Liverpool-born nurse taking her son Fynlo, ten, home to a dying mother and a job, and on the boy. It cannot be bought off: Aalin can stake her office, but that is not the currency Jen pays in, and Jen has the same Counsel.
Turn: On Tynwald morning, in the chapel vestry at St John's, Juan holds out his hand for one of two sheets: her certificate or her withdrawal. He will not look at the boy, because he would see his own granddaughter. She hands him the certificate. He signs it on his knee, walks out along the rushes, and reads from the hill the Act that makes it the last. Fynlo stays, his mother crosses alone, and Aalin has departed once and never may again.
Last image: Saturday, an hour after the Liverpool boat left with Jen aboard: at the heritage bench in Quirk's yard, Fynlo runs the knife down a herring's back and opens it flat with the spine down the middle. He hangs it on the tenterhooks among rows of opened fish, every backbone in the midst, and does not look up.
Question: May Aalin keep a boy on the island against his mother, on a sight she cannot put into words, so that one judge here will have seen past the middle? Or is that a Peel woman's affinity and her wish to be someone, paid for by a come-over on the Liverpool boat?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That impartiality is what makes a judge worth having; once a machine keeps the oath of indifference perfectly, only partial sight can exceed it, and that can be learned only on someone's actual child, whose mother can read the bill.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: The rule-maker neither enforces his rule nor rescues her from it: disqualified by his grief, he makes her choose in front of him, signs, and shuts the door behind her, so her one departure is the island's last and no later case will teach her whether it was right. It is earned because his grievance was never a departure that went wrong (the Deemster who learned fathers on his son was right), and because Jen's audit has already made Aalin say "No" aloud.
Not the obvious version: A judge trusts her gut over the algorithm and is punished and vindicated, or humbled when the machine proves right. Here nothing arrives to vindicate anyone, and Counsel is right by every measure anyone can check. The question is not whether she sees truly but whether she may find out on a come-over's son, and the price leaves on the morning boat, in plain view.
Closest published: "Harrison Bergeron," Kurt Vonnegut: a law handicaps everyone down to equality and the exceptional one breaks it. Vonnegut's cap is satirical tyranny and his rebel admirable. Here the cap was made by a man who paid, for reasons the story honors; the exception may be only affinity; and one named mother bears the cost.

"I've read it to mine," she said. "I want you to hear what it says."
