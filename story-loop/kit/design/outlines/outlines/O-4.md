### O-4 — Eleven Fathers
Conceit: For eleven years a father has kept every earpiece in a bowl by the door, so that he, not the counsel, would know his children. His daughter runs her childhood through the counsel he never walked three steps to ask, gets a different reasonable father each run, and finds one thing holds steady: the curve of his apprenticeship, with her at the bottom and her brother at the top.
Who: Tess Haworth, 19, Hebden Bridge; a night care assistant in Halifax who follows every care plan to the letter. On Saturday she leaves for nurse training in Leeds, and leaves her brother Kit, 12, with their father, Rob, a tree surgeon. She wants one bill she believes (raised, or practised on?) to hand Rob first. Nightly she takes her bud from the bowl after he goes up, breaking the house's one rule, and reads a better childhood than hers.
Obstacle: The audit will not hold still: eleven runs, eleven fathers. No better auditor can be bought or borrowed, since every opinion she could ask runs on the same model, and her own judgment was made in the house she is auditing. And the debt bought something: the father now handling Kit is the one she paid for. To collect is to spend it.
Turn: She takes Kit's bud from the bowl in front of her father, who watches and does not stop her, and puts it in Kit's hand. The house where no one asked becomes one where the next child can check every word his father says; Kit cannot unlearn that a father's words are one try among several.
Last image: From the door, bag in hand: Kit's bud on the breakfast table between the cereal box and the milk, its small green light on, neither Kit nor Rob touching it, while Rob talks about the 9:14.
Question: Was her father entitled to learn, on her, to be the man who knew his children (she paid, she can read the bill, and the man who stops himself mid-sentence for Kit is what she bought), or was it vanity charged to a child who could not be asked?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That the harm of being someone's practice is the mistakes, which an audit can total; here harm and gift are one thing, since a child who cannot check her father's words grows round them, and what shields the next child from a father's errors also shields him from being known.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: The reader waits for the list to be read to the father and it never is: the climax is overheard through a wall, and the bill is paid forward to a brother their father may just have handled well. Because the bowl, the wobbling audit, the sentence she grew round and the curve come first, the hand-off reads as both protecting Kit and collecting from Rob, and she cannot say which.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version: the grown child reads the model's list to her father; he defends himself or breaks; they reconcile or part. Here the audit never yields a bill she believes, he never sees the list, and what she presents instead is his broken rule, in front of him, on someone else's behalf. Nor is it atrophy: his judgment is real; the question is who paid.
Closest published: "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling," Ted Chiang: a father checks his memory of raising his daughter against a lifelog and finds he was the one who wronged her. There the audit is of fact, from the parent's side; here it is counterfactual, from the child's, run on the counsel the father refused, and the question is whether it was his to try.
Beats:
1. Tuesday, 3 a.m., after a night shift, bud in against the rule: the eleventh run, forty-one entries, each beside what a reasonable father would have done. The eleventh father is kinder to Rob than the tenth, and she stops trusting either.
2. The worst entry: Boots in Halifax at thirteen, then the van: "I know why you did it. You wanted me to have to come and get you." The model offers three other reasons; she cannot tell whether his fits because it was true or because she has told her life in his words. Its father for that night says it better, to a girl who no longer exists. The cost moves from what happened to what grew.
3. Wednesday, groundwork for Rob on a beech in Heptonstall: he ignores the client's model's felling plan and lays the limb between a greenhouse and a trampoline; his saw sparks on fence wire deep in the trunk. "Grew round it." She sees how a master is made, nearly tells him, doesn't.
4. Wednesday night: the model finds the curve, his departures worse than counsel early and better later, Kit's years top right. The credits: fetched from a party at fifteen, a silent drive, toast; the model calls it "comparable to typical advice." It was the silence. Its bill is not hers either.
5. Thursday: the Co-op rings; Kit has a tin of peaches in his coat. Rob goes to get him. Through the wall, the bud listening with her, the old sentence starts, stops, becomes a question; a ring-pull; two forks. The model grades him well. Nobody, Rob included, knows if it was right.
6. Kit's room: syrup on his chin. "Why did I do it?" An answer rises in her, in her father's cadence. She does not give it.
7. Downstairs: Rob rinsing two forks; in the bowl, his light, Kit's, and the empty place that was hers. She takes Kit's; he watches and lets her. Upstairs: "It'll give you three reasons. None of them are mine." Kit's fingers close on it. Nothing is ever said to Rob.
8. Saturday morning: the last image.
