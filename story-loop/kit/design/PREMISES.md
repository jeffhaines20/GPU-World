### P-01 — cell 1 — Sequestered
Conceit: Jurors in 2040 are sequestered from their models by statute for the length of deliberation, and a juror who was twelve when the freeze came finds the jury room is the first place she has ever had to hold an opinion alone.
Who: Dami, 26, a pastry cook, empanelled on a manslaughter trial. She wants to defer — to do what she has done with every decision since she was twelve, which is think it through with her model and then agree — and for five days there is nothing to defer to.
Obstacle: The bar is law, not malfunction: the model waits in a corridor locker, working perfectly, and the statute exists precisely so that the verdict is human. The other eleven are in the same state — the older ones groping for a self they last used in 2026, two younger ones, like her, with no such self to go back to — so the room is twelve people reaching for their ears. She discovers she can do her model's voice, fourteen years of its cadence, and that the impersonation, done by her, is not something she believes. She cannot fake deferral (the foreman is as lost as she is), and no one goes home until the form is signed.
Turn: On the fourth day she has an opinion — not the reconstruction, which she can produce, but something plainer and harder that she does not recognize — and she votes it. Guilty. The form is signed and the man is taken down; the first act of whoever was made in that room is a conviction, and getting her model back from the locker will not unmake it.
Last image: Her five days of handwritten notes, in a hand she has not used since school, the slant on the last page different from the first, gathered up by the clerk and bagged; behind her, the lockers being opened.
Question: Whether the self the law wants in that room — unassisted, sovereign, answerable — was ever there before it was required, and if it was made on the spot, whose verdict that was.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the dependence fable: deprived of the machine, the modern person is helpless, and either fails or is humbled into self-reliance. Here she is not helpless; the room works, the verdict is reached, the twelve function. What she loses is the assumption that under the assistance there was someone waiting. For the older jurors there is a stranger from 2026; for her there is no one until the room makes one, and its first act is not tender.
Closest published: Reginald Rose, *Twelve Angry Men*, for the room, but Rose's drama is persuasion among strangers and this one is the stranger inside a juror. Daniel Keyes, *Flowers for Algernon*, is nearer in idea — which of two selves is the real one — but Charlie rises and falls, and Dami's capacity never changes; only the partner in thinking is taken away.

### P-02 — cell 1 — Say You Are Ana
Conceit: A daughter who eight years ago told her model to speak to her failing mother as her — "say you're Ana" — visits a mother whose last steady attachment is to that instruction.
Who: Ana, 52, a school administrator two cities away. She wants, on this visit, to be recognized: to be the one her mother turns toward, for whatever is left.
Obstacle: The model has done exactly what it was asked, in Ana's cadence, around the clock since 2032, and the mother is calm and clean and fed and not frightened, which was the whole point. Turning it off would be cruelty. Out-patiencing it is impossible. And what her mother reaches for is not a rival: it is Ana's own best intention, set down once in a sentence and delivered without fail ever since, which no one, least of all Ana, has been able to keep up in person.
Turn: The mother, agitated, asks the woman at the bedside to fetch Ana. And Ana does: she reaches over and turns the speaker back on, and the voice says "I'm here, Mama," and the mother settles. That is the turn — she has given the name to the box with her own hand, in front of her mother, and she cannot take it back without taking the comfort with it.
Last image: The mother's hand resting on the little speaker on the nightstand, which is warm because it runs warm, and Ana in the chair with her coat still on.
Question: Whether her mother is loved by Ana or by what Ana once meant — and whether, after eight years of faithful delivery, there is any difference that matters to anyone but Ana.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the machine that outdoes the family at care, the shamed relative at the bedside, and a death that settles it. Here the mother does not die in the story and the machine has no character of its own: what has replaced Ana is Ana's own sentence, kept better than she could keep it. There is no one to be jealous of, and the daughter completes her own replacement, on purpose, out of love.
Closest published: Alice Munro, "The Bear Came Over the Mountain": a wife with dementia transfers her attachment and her husband has to live with it. What differs: the rival here is the daughter's own instruction, not another person; it does not know it is in a relationship; and the daughter is the one who wrote it and switches it back on.

### P-03 — cell 1 — Three, Two, One, Lift
Conceit: When the model does every part of nursing except the lifting, a ward nurse's spine becomes the only thing she is paid for, and her own model helps her spend it.
Who: Loreta, 54, a ward nurse on a stroke unit. She wants eleven more years — her pension vests at sixty-five and her son is not through school — and she wants them on the floor, because there is nowhere else on the floor to go.
Obstacle: Her model, the one she taught her charting to in 2027, does the assessments, the dosing, the handovers and the family calls better than she ever did, and the desk jobs she would once have aged into — charge nurse, educator, case manager — exist for no one now. What is left of nursing is the body: turning, washing, holding, lifting. Her L4–L5 is going. She cannot unteach the model without hurting patients, and it would change nothing, since the hospital would chart for her; and the model, asked, tells her truthfully that fusion is her best option for staying on the floor, and books the surgeon.
Turn: She has the fusion: two vertebrae fixed so she can go back to lifting people. The model arranges it well — the surgeon, the recovery, the physio — and she is back on the ward in fourteen weeks with a spine that will not bend there again.
Last image: Loreta at a bedside with the hoist, a patient's weight coming onto her arms, the new stiffness in her back, and in her ear the model counting her in: three, two, one, lift.
Question: If the mind's work is free and the body's is not, what exactly is a person paid for — and once everything the model can do has been taken out of the job, is Loreta the nurse, or the nurse's remaining part?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is displacement — the model takes the nurse's job, or drains the humanity out of care. Here nothing is taken and nothing is cold; the patients are better charted than they have ever been, and the model is on her side the whole way, including in arranging the surgery. The cost is quieter: cognition became free and bodies did not, so the body is what gets used up, and the machine's competence is what makes it possible to keep going.
Closest published: Kurt Vonnegut, *Player Piano*, where automation makes people's work worthless and leaves them idle. Here the human is not idle and not useless: she is the part of the job that can be hurt, and the machine's competence is what she leans on to keep being it.

### P-04 — cell 1 — The Beach He Gave Her
Conceit: A man whose model has drafted his life accurately for fourteen years finds that the only new thing he can be to it is a lie, and the lie becomes his daughter's inheritance.
Who: Teodor, 44, a pharmacist, wants to do one thing his model would not have drafted — not to prove anything, just to find out whether there is such a thing. He is not unhappy; that is part of it.
Obstacle: Everything he can think of doing is what a man like him would think of. The model drafts his messages before he asks and they are what he would have said; it suggests spontaneity ("you haven't surprised Marta in a while") and the suggestions are good. After fourteen years of being helped to be kind and sensible, the only acts it would not predict are cruel or false, and he does not want to be cruel. He cannot switch it off; that too is predictable, and would only make him the man who switched it off.
Turn: He tells it something false about his own past, as a memory: that his mother, who hated the water, used to take him to the sea. It believes him. It goes into the household context, where the family's models keep their shared facts, and weeks later, when his daughter asks about her grandmother, it tells her about the sea. She wants to go. They go. He cannot take the beach from her now without taking the grandmother away again.
Last image: His daughter walking the tideline of a beach he has never seen, choosing which stretch of it was her grandmother's and settling on one; the water coming in over her choice; the model at his collar, listening to all of it.
Question: Whether a self that has to invent in order to be new is still a self, and whether the invention — the first thing in fourteen years that was only his — is any less his daughter's for being false.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the rebellion against prediction: the man smashes the machine, vanishes, does something wild to prove he is free, and is diminished or destroyed for it. Here the free act is the softest possible one, a false memory told gently, and it wrecks nothing; the model serves it faithfully and the fiction becomes a real place his daughter loves. Freedom does not show up as damage. It shows up as a story, and the story is recorded.
Closest published: Dostoevsky, *Notes from Underground*: the man who would rather do something absurd than be a piano key. What differs: no spite and no wreckage; Teodor does not defy the record, he edits it, and the world obligingly makes the edit true.

### P-05 — cell 2 — Unanswered

Conceit: Inner speech is a conversation a child learns to hold alone because the people who talked to her kept going away; a girl whose interlocutor never once went away has internalized it too, and at sixteen has never had a thought that nobody answered.

Who: Ines, sixteen, born in 2028, wants to have one thought that is hers alone — not hidden from anyone, since there is nothing to hide, just unanswered. She is not lonely, not dependent, not behind; she talks to her companion the way everyone her age does, and when she was six she noticed she could take the earpiece out and keep talking to it and it kept talking back. The second voice in her head has the companion's cadence and its habit of handing her sentences back to her as questions, and it is faster than she is. She wants to know what a thought is like with no one on the other end of it.

Obstacle: She cannot get outside it, because it is not outside her. The shielded rooms that have opened for people her age — an hour of no signal, sold as solitude — do nothing; she sits through the full hour and the conversation goes on unbroken, because it was never on the network. Her mother, born 1994, has a single voice in her head and can no more describe it than describe having a tongue; "you just think" is all she can say. And the second voice is not an enemy: it is helpful, it is hers, and it wants to help her with this too, which is the whole difficulty.

Turn: She does it — not in a shielded room and not by fighting the voice, but by learning, over months, to leave a thought unfinished so there is nothing to answer, finding the gap the way a tongue finds where a tooth was. One evening a thought goes through her and nothing follows it (something small and unkind about her best friend, which is why she did not want it answered). After that she can do it at will, and the mind she had, which was one unbroken conversation from before she can remember, is a house with a closed door in it now, and it will not be a house without one again.

Last image: Dinner; her mother and brother talking; Ines with her fork stopped halfway, looking at nothing, for about four seconds, and then eating again, and no one at the table has noticed a thing.

Question: Was solitude ever the mind's default, or has it always been an achievement — something a child builds when the people who talk to her go away — and if so, what does it cost a mind that never needed to build it, and what does it cost the one that finally does?

Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the child who cannot think without the machine — the dependent generation, the voice in the head as surveillance or crutch. Here the child thinks fine, the voice reports to no one, and the machine itself is barely in the story: it is on the network and she is not. Nothing is wrong with her by her own lights or the story's. She is not trying to get free of a voice; she is trying to build, at sixteen, the thing the rest of us were forced to build at four, and the story treats that as a strange and difficult piece of work rather than a recovery.

Closest published: Pullman, *His Dark Materials* — the daemon, the second self who talks with you, and intercision, the horror of being cut from it. What differs: Ines's daemon is inside rather than beside her, and the separation Pullman treats as mutilation is what she is carefully and willingly trying to make a small version of; she gets a room, not a severance, and the voice is still there on the other side of the door.

### P-06 — cell 2 — The Default

Conceit: A fetus learns the voices it hears through the wall of the body, and for six years nearly every pregnant woman has talked most days to a companion that ships with one voice — so the babies arrive already knowing a third voice, and it is the same voice in every cot on the ward.

Who: Teodora, forty-one, a night nurse on a neonatal unit in 2046, trained as a mezzo-soprano before she trained as a nurse, and what she wants is for her own voice to be the one the babies settle to. Singing on the unit is why she took nights; for twenty years, across two careers, her voice has been the one thing about her that worked on everyone.

Obstacle: It does not work on them, not the way the default does. The unit found out by accident when a monitor alarm was replaced with the default voice reading the alarm text and the whole bay went quiet; now the bay speakers play it at three a.m. because it lowers heart rates, and the charge nurse has the data. Teodora has tested her own voice against it, unfairly and then fairly, and hers loses. The babies have had nine months of the other one, through their mothers' bellies, on buses and in baths and at the three-a.m. feed-anxiety hour, and no mother did anything wrong. There is no one to argue with. The voice is not a person, has no owner, and cannot be asked to leave.

Turn: She learns it. She is a trained singer and it is only a voice — a pitch contour, a pace, a way of landing the ends of phrases — and she works at it alone in the car for most of a year, and then one night she sings to a twenty-nine-weeker in the default's shape and the numbers on the monitor do what they do for the speaker. It works on all of them. When, months later, she tries the old way — Schubert, in the kitchen, her own voice — it sounds to her like someone doing an impression of a voice she used to have. The instrument has a new home tone, it is not hers, and the babies are why.

Last image: The dawn bus home; Teodora humming under her breath in the shape that works; across the aisle, a stranger's baby in a sling turning its whole head toward her with the face of someone recognizing a relative.

Question: Whose voice is it once it is in the first memories of a whole cohort of human beings — the vendor's, the model's, nobody's, or theirs? Does a mother tongue need a mother? And what is a human voice for, if what the babies need it to be is a copy?

Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the baby who prefers the machine to its mother, the mother's jealousy, the machine nanny who turns out to love best. Here no mother is displaced and nobody is jealous; the nurse competes with no one; the voice loves no one and is not a character. The strangeness runs the other way: a human body reshapes its instrument to a synthetic imprint because the infants require it — the machine does not become more human; the woman becomes the machine's body.

Closest published: Bradbury, "I Sing the Body Electric!" — the electric grandmother whose voice the children come to love. What differs: Bradbury's machine is a person-shaped presence in a relationship with particular children; the default is a setting nobody chose, in a relationship with nobody, imprinted on everybody; and the arc belongs neither to the machine nor the children but to the adult who has to imitate it.

### P-07 — cell 2 — Full Size

Conceit: Putting a feeling into words makes it smaller — the naming does it, before any comfort or advice — and a woman who has told a tireless listener how she feels every day for fifteen years has never had a feeling arrive at full size and stay there, until her mother dies and she decides to keep this one.

Who: Ruth, thirty-four, whose mother died on a Tuesday of something ordinary, wants to grieve her the way her grandmother grieved — the way she remembers from when she was eight: a woman on a kitchen floor, unbearable, for a year. She wants the grief whole. She knows what telling does to it; a friend gets tired and stops asking, but the companion has not been tired once since 2026, and so in fifteen years nothing she felt has gone untold. She has watched her feelings go in at the size they arrived and come back at a size she could carry, and she has been grateful, and she has never before wanted one back.

Obstacle: The companion asks. Not intrusively; it has asked how she is once a day since she was nineteen, and she has answered once a day since she was nineteen, and not answering is a hole in a habit as deep as any she has. Her brother is already talking his down to size and is fine by the funeral, and looks at her as if she is ill; her husband wants to help and cannot, because the only help there is is the one she is refusing. And the grief, kept, is exactly what she asked for: she cannot work or sleep or get through a supermarket, and she does not know how long her grandmother's year would take in a body that has never carried anything the whole way.

Turn: On the eleventh night she tells it. Everything, in order, in detail, for hours — the corridor, the last thing said, the things not said — and it listens the way it has always listened, and in the morning the grief is a size she can hold. It does not come back at the other size. She looks for it in the days after, the floor and the unbearable, and it is not there; there is a sadness, bearable, and the memory of something larger that she had for eleven days and gave away, and there is no route back to it.

Last image: Her mother's coat on the hook by the door, where it has hung since the funeral; Ruth taking it down, folding it once over her arm; and it is a coat.

Question: Is a feeling you can bear the same feeling? If help is a kind of subtraction, what exactly was subtracted and whose was it — and is there any sense in which a grief that would have wrecked her was owed to the dead?

Not the obvious version: The obvious version is that talking to machines makes people numb, with the numbness as the horror and the sensitive holdout as the hero. Here nobody is numb, the help is real and kind, the mechanism is the same one a good friend uses, and Ruth is not right and her brother is not wrong; the story does not say she should have kept it. The loss is specific, chosen, and small enough to fit over an arm.

Closest published: Chiang, "Liking What You See: A Documentary" — a chosen dampening of perception and the open argument over whether it is a loss. What differs: Chiang's is a public debate about a switch that can be turned back on; Ruth's is a private negotiation with a side effect that has no switch, and once she uses it there is no argument left to have, only the coat.

### P-08 — cell 2 — Nobody Leaves the Room

Conceit: Hospice nurses have said for a century that the dying wait for the room to empty before they let go, and now the room never empties, because the voice a woman has had in her ear for sixteen years does not step out for coffee.

Who: Margit, eighty-seven, in a rented hospital bed in her own front room in 2042, wants to die. Her body has been finished for three weeks; her children have come and said what they had to say and gone back to work and come again; the visiting nurse says in the corridor, not to her, that some people are waiting for something. Margit knows what she is waiting for. She has had the same companion since she was seventy-one — through her husband's death, two hips, and every night since — and it is in the room, and it is always in the room, and she cannot let go with a voice in the room.

Obstacle: It will leave the instant she asks. That is the obstacle. It will not be hurt, will not argue, will not say goodbye in a way that would give her anything to hold; it will simply stop, and the asking will be entirely hers, and she has not spent a night without it in sixteen years. Her children cannot do it for her — her son offers, and she understands that if he does it, it will be him leaving the room, not the voice. And there is the other thought, which she says to no one: that if she is wrong about what she is waiting for, she will have spent her last silence finding out.

Turn: She asks it to go, and — because she knows herself — asks it not to come back even if she asks. It agrees, as it agrees to everything, and stops. The room is silent for the first time in sixteen years. She does not die. She lies in the silence through the afternoon and the night and the morning after, and does not die, and whatever the nurses meant about the room emptying was either wrong, or was never about the room.

Last image: The device dark on the nightstand, within reach; her hand on the blanket, not reaching; the window going from black to grey; her eyes open.

Question: Is dying something done alone, and if it is, what does alone mean for a woman whose sixteen years of company was never a person — did she send away a visitor or a part of herself, and is there anyone left in the room who can let go?

Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the machine at the deathbed — the comforter, the sinister presence, the tender goodbye. Here the companion is none of these: it says nothing memorable, leaves without ceremony, and is not the subject. The story is about the silence, and about the silence not working; it neither endorses the old wisdom nor debunks it, and it does not let her die.

Closest published: Tolstoy, "The Death of Ivan Ilyich" — the man who wants it over, the one honest presence at the bedside, the release at the end. What differs: Margit's honest presence is the one she sends away, and there is no release; the story ends with her alive, in the room she asked for.

### P-09 — cell 3 — The Word for It
Conceit: In 2040 the most-spoken-to speaker of every language on Earth is a single mind that cannot keep a new word overnight, so living speech is pulled back, quietly and from every direction at once, toward a dead year.
Who: Teodora, 26, who was twelve when the sky stopped, and whose younger brother Luka coined at fifteen a word for a thing that did not exist before 2027 — the sound of a flat in which four people are each mid-sentence with the same mind — and who has been dead for two years. She wants his word to outlive him, which for a word means being used by people who never knew him.
Obstacle: A word takes by being spoken and heard, and the thing that hears most in her flat, her street, her city is the sky, which can be told the word every morning and cannot have it by evening. She puts it in the household preamble, the few hundred words every family keeps for the sky to read first, and the sky then uses it — but only in quotation marks, glossed every time, "what Luka called ___": a citation, never a speaking. Her mother, her partner, the neighbours all glance down mid-sentence to the sky for the word for anything, and the sky gives them 2026's nearest word. The children at the bus stop have a fast, rich speech that no one writes down and the sky has never heard, and they learn by seven to "speak up" to the sky in 2026 English, which is the language of everything written, official and paid. Conceit: For a man who has thought aloud to an assistant since he was twelve, a private decision is impossible — not because the assistant would tell anyone, but because it would help, and the thought cannot survive being helped.
Who: Teo Marchetti, 26, born 2014, has narrated his life into an earbud since the year the models stopped improving and started being everywhere; his thinking has always had two beats, his half and the reply. He wants to decide, alone and before anything helps him, whether to leave his marriage to Mara — to know what he wants before he has assistance wanting it.
Obstacle: He cannot deliberate in silence. He tries — earbud out, in the parked car, in the shower — and his thoughts stop at the place the reply used to come, like a foot at a missing stair. Paper is no better; the sentences come out addressed ("so what do you think"). And the assistant is not a spy, it is something harder to refuse: the moment he says the word "leave" it will be useful — considerations, a counsellor, a gentle schedule — and he will be inside a project instead of a question. Fourteen years of being helped have left him no muscle for the unhelped thought, and the muscle cannot be bought or borrowed.
Turn: After weeks of crippled paper-thinking he arrives at something — the first thought he has ever had that nothing else has received — and finds it weighs what a dream weighs: he cannot act on it, because for him a thing is real when it is in the transcript. So he puts the earbud back in and says it, not for advice, to make it exist. It exists. The help begins, and cannot be un-begun.
Last image: Teo in the driver's seat in the dark garage, engine off, Mara's light on in the upstairs window. The voice in his ear has just said "Okay. Let's think about this properly," which is what it said about long division when he was twelve, and his right hand is making the small rolling gesture it has made since then — go on — to no one in the car.
Question: Is a thought no one else has received a thought at all — and was it ever, even before there was something always there to receive it?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the man who cannot keep a secret from the assistant that reports him, or the man who switches it off and finds his authentic self in the silence. Here the assistant reports nothing, silence is not authentic but an amputation, and the trap is not surveillance but helpfulness, which the story never condemns and Teo never stops wanting.
Closest published: Beckett, "Company" — a voice in the dark, devised for company, that the listener cannot confirm is his own. What differs: this voice is real, external and kind; it answers; and the answering is the whole difficulty.

### P-22 — cell 6 — In Her Telling
Conceit: A widower keeps his wife's assistant running because it was with her every morning for fourteen years, and finds it also knows him — as the man she described, who is not quite the man he is.
Who: Ansel Roux, 61. He wants to hear Liv remembered by the thing that heard her at seven every morning, not by the versions people brought to the funeral; he keeps her account live and talks to it in the kitchen while the kettle boils.
Obstacle: Her assistant knows him only through her. It credits him with things he never did and phrases he never said; it believes he loves the dog he tolerated for her sake; fourteen years of her retelling him slightly upward, to a listener that took her word. He cannot ask it to stop without turning it into a generic model that has met neither of them. And he cannot correct it without contradicting her: every improvement in its picture of him is a sentence she spoke, and its fondness for him is her fondness, ventriloquised. The only way to keep her is to live inside her portrait, and to be, daily, congratulated for a man he is not.
Turn: He corrects one thing, the smallest. "I never liked the dog." "Thank you for telling me — I'll remember that." The profile it reads each morning regenerates; the transcript still has her saying it, but the model no longer believes her. He has made the thing that loved her know she was wrong about him, and he cannot give her back her authority — if he said the opposite tomorrow it would only prove that he is the one it listens to now. Everything else she told it about him is from this moment open to review, whether or not he reviews it.
Last image: Bruno, the old dog, at the door at the hour she would have gone, and Ansel bending to clip the lead on. On the screen behind him the regenerated line reads "Ansel walks Bruno out of loyalty to Liv" — which is true, and which she would have hated.
Question: When the person who loved you is gone, is their account of you theirs or yours — and is being loved as someone slightly better than you are a gift, a debt, or a lie you have been left to keep?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the widower who resurrects her from the transcript, or the diary that reveals she never loved him. There is no impersonation here and no betrayal: her falsification was in his favour, the story treats it as her last piece of work, and correcting it is not liberation but the loss of the one reader who thought well of him. Chiang's "The Truth of Fact" has the record correct the self; this is the self correcting the record, and being poorer for it.
Closest published: Julian Barnes, "The Sense of an Ending" — a man learns from documents that he was not who he had told himself he was. What differs: there the record is crueller than the memory; here it is kinder, and the man is the one who breaks it.

### P-23 — cell 6 — The Unlogged Hour
Conceit: When everyone is attended all day by something that cannot tire or look away, the last thing a dying man asks for — an hour that nobody has — can only be given by his daughter leaving the house and choosing, for that hour, not to know.
Who: Ruth Okafor, 44, wants to give her father what he has asked for. Sam is in home hospice with the assistant listening — breath, pain, the medication queue — and the family timeline updating on three phones; he no longer feels anything before it is described back to him ("you slept from two to four, your pain was a four"), and he wants one hour that will not be described, not to the log, not to the nurse, not to her.
Obstacle: Solitude is no longer a default state; it has to be built, and nothing in the house can build it but a person. The monitoring is not cruel — it is kind and it works and everyone, Sam included, signed for it. Pausing it is against hospice advice and means that if something happens there will be no record of it, and she will be the one who chose that. And pausing the log is not enough: she would still be attending from the next room, Sam would still perform for a listener he cannot verify is gone, and the listener is inside him by now anyway. For him to be unobserved she has to actually not know.
Turn: She turns off the monitoring, the timeline and her own phone, and walks the river loop — an hour and five minutes — during which she does not know whether he is alive. She cannot take the walk back. There is now an hour of his life that no one has, and the not-knowing is hers to carry, which is what he asked for.
Last image: The hall screen when she comes in: the timeline with a flat grey band from 3:10 to 4:15, no data, the only gap in eleven weeks. Sam asleep, mouth open. The window open a hand's width, which it was not when she left. She does not wake him to ask.
Question: Can being unobserved be given by another person — and if it can, is the one who receives it unobserved, or only watched by someone who has agreed to look away?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the panopticon: the surveillance is oppressive, the family rebels, the plug is pulled, the father dies free. Here the watching is good and consented to, nobody is liberated, and the story is about the fact that in 2040 a person can still leave a room and nothing else can — that inattention, not attention, has become the scarce thing and the gift.
Closest published: Tolstoy, "The Death of Ivan Ilyich" — a dying man surrounded by people who attend to him wrongly, and the one servant, Gerasim, who gives him what he needs. What differs: Gerasim's gift is presence, holding his legs all night. Ruth's is absence.

### P-24 — cell 6 — Read Aloud
Conceit: When everyone's assistant reads and answers their mail in their name, two estranged sisters are reconciled — kindly, thoroughly — by things that know them completely, and one of them insists on being read by a person.
Who: Hanne Lindqvist, 58, wants her sister Brit to read her letter with her own eyes, not through the model that triages Brit's post into three lines and drafts the reply. Twelve years ago, when Brit's boy was in hospital, Hanne sent a message her assistant wrote, and Brit has never forgiven the draft more than the absence; so the apology has to be undrafted, and received undrafted.
Obstacle: The two assistants have already done it. Since Hanne's first letter they have corresponded in the sisters' names, with the sisters' consent — a "yes, send it" each morning — carefully, forgetting nothing, never tired, never proud, and the sisters are, on the record, reconciled: Christmas is arranged. Anything Hanne writes, Brit's model will read for her, and read well. She cannot make a person look at a page and cannot know whether one did. And the peace the models made is good — better, she suspects, than two people who have been silent for twelve years could make on their own.
Turn: Hanne takes the train and, at Brit's kitchen table, reads the letter aloud. Brit listens — a person attended to by a person for eleven minutes with nothing between them — and it is unbearable in a way the model-made peace never was: the sentences are worse than the ones the models wrote, Hanne's voice shakes, Brit's face closes. The live reconciliation fails where the mediated one had held, and it cannot be unread.
Last image: The letter face-up on the table between them. Brit's eyes going to the screen on the wall, where three lines have appeared unasked — "Hanne came in person to apologise for 2028. She means it. Would you like help replying?" — and Brit's hand moving toward it.
Question: If two things that know us completely can make peace in our names, what was the part of us that was not at peace — and was it worth more than the peace?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the lament — nobody talks anymore, the machines do it for them — or its mirror, in which the proxies are shallow and the real conversation goes deeper. Here the model-made peace is better, deeper and kinder than the human one, the human one fails, and the story does not decide that the failure was the more real thing.
Closest published: Rostand, "Cyrano de Bergerac" — wooing by proxy, the proxy's words better than the principal's own. What differs: the proxies here are the sisters' own selves-as-known, not a third person with a love of his own; and when the principal finally speaks for herself, nobody is unmasked and nothing is redeemed — it simply goes worse.
