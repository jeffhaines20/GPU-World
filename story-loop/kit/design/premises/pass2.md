### P-25 — cell 7 — The Sunset
Conceit: Ireland has voted, by referendum, that every personal model must permanently delete whatever it witnessed more than seven years ago, the only exemption being a record already lodged with the State, and a woman who voted for it has six days before the one night she cannot let go of turns seven.
Who: Nuala Gormley, 55, farms half of a split holding outside Drumshanbo, Co. Leitrim; her brother Diarmuid farms the other half, across a ditch they both walk. She wants the record of one night in November 2033 kept — the night, over the division of their father's land, he put her against the wall of her own kitchen and told her, at length, what he would do — and she wants never to have to use it. She has never once played it. She wants it to exist the way a letter exists in a drawer: so that what happened does not depend on her.
Obstacle: The Sunset Amendment makes no provision for private keeping. There is no private copy, no export, no exemption for consent — the drafters had seen what a consent exemption does to a woman with a brother, and closed it, to protect her. Her model will delete the night at midnight on the seventh anniversary, and the only thing that stops it is a record lodged with the State — a Garda statement, a complaint, a court file — which is the one thing she has refused for seven years to make, because he is her brother and the ditch is still there and the whole parish drinks in the same two pubs. The week before, Diarmuid rings. He is not threatening; he is relieved, and he makes her an offer: after Thursday it is only your word and mine, and I will never contradict you; say what you like about that night and I will say yes, that is how it was. He is offering her the story in exchange for the proof. The obstacle cannot be removed because she believes in the law. She voted for it three years after that night, on the argument — still, she thinks, a good one — that a people has to be able to stop knowing things about each other. She is not against the Sunset. She is its exception.
Turn: On the sixth day she drives to the Garda station in Carrick-on-Shannon and lodges it. She lets her model play the night to a sergeant who watches it without a word (the record is never played for the reader), and it becomes the State's — the one item in her record that will never expire — and Diarmuid, who was a day from being a man who had never done it, is now named in a file in Dublin that will outlast them both. Under the amendment a lodged record cannot be withdrawn; that was the point, so that nobody could be leant on to un-lodge. She wanted to keep it and never use it; the only way to keep it was to use it.
Last image: Her kitchen that night, the model's memory view open on the table: the timeline going grey at the seven-year edge the way it does every night, and on the far side of the edge, alone in the grey where her mother's last Christmas and the calf and the whole of that year have already gone, one night still lit, with the small harp of the State on it, and the edge moving away from it at the rate of a day a day.
Question: Should a society mandate forgetting? Is the statute of limitations a moral principle — a people must be able to stop knowing about each other, a man must be able to become someone who never did it — or a concession to the old cost of remembering, obsolete now that the witness is perfect; and when the only exempt memory is the State's, has private memory been abolished or merely made honest? This turns the contest's panopticon question over: not whether the State will watch, but whether it should be the only one allowed to remember. A reader can hold that Diarmuid's relief is the law working, and that the last image shows what her remembering cost — the whole year gone grey to keep the wound; or that his relief is exactly what the law was always going to be for. Underneath: whether she wanted the proof or the story, and which of the two of them was right to want it gone.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the race against the deadline — the deletion as villain, the abuser wiping the tape, the courtroom scene where the record is played. Here nobody fights the law, the victim voted for it and would again, the brother is not a villain but a man who has waited seven years to be forgiven by a clock, and the story's cruelty is that in this world the only way to keep a private thing is to make it public.
Closest published: Ishiguro, *The Buried Giant* — a country's forgetting as mercy or crime. There the mist is imposed and the old couple wants to remember; here the forgetting was voted for, rolls forward a day at a time, and the woman who wants to remember must hand her memory to the State to keep it. It is near Chiang's "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" in furniture only: the record here is never in doubt and never shown.

### P-26 — cell 7 — Three Minutes Eleven Seconds
Conceit: In Denmark a child's model is on the child's side from birth and hands the whole record to the child at fifteen; a parent may redact anything before the handover, but every redaction is permanently marked — the content destroyed, the gap labelled with its length and its timestamp.
Who: Signe Aalund, 44, a dental hygienist in Aarhus. She wants her son Villum — born in October 2026, the month after the models stopped getting better; handover in five weeks — to have all fifteen years of her except three minutes and eleven seconds: a night in March 2027 when he was five months old and she, alone with him at ten past three, feeding him, told him at length and in detail that she did not want him, that she had thought about leaving him at the hospital, that she was going to give him to her sister. She remembers every word. She has never needed to play it; she knows it is there because it happened, and because his model — set up by her, at the hospital, on his side — was in the room.
Obstacle: A redaction is a black bar. A three-minute-eleven-second bar in his fifth month, labelled *redacted by parent*, will be the first thing he goes looking for and the only thing in fifteen years he cannot open; she knows him; he will ask. Whatever she says the bar holds, her own model will hear her say it and his will too, so a lie about the gap is on the record forever. The alternative is to leave it in, and let him hear his mother, in a voice he will know, in his own first year, in his own record, on his side, saying it. The marking rule cannot be got around, and she does not want it got around: it was written after parents quietly laundered their children's infancies, she supported it, it is a good rule. Her own model holds the same three minutes; she could delete her copy any time; she never has.
Turn: She redacts it, and then — the night before the handover, at the kitchen table, in two sentences — she tells him what it holds. Not the recording: the fact. That she said, when he was five months old, that she did not want him; that she will not let him hear it; that it is gone. The audio is destroyed and the sentence is said, and the two now exist apart: he knows what she said, and he will never hear her say it. It cannot change back in either direction.
Last image: The morning of the handover, the model's light changing from the household white to the blue he picked. He goes straight to the fifth month. He puts his headphones on over the black bar and plays it — three minutes and eleven seconds of nothing, the counter running — and she watches his face while he listens to what she took out.
Question: Whose is the record of a shared life? Does a child's right to the whole of his witnessed infancy outweigh a parent's need to have said the unsayable to someone who could not yet hear it, and is the family a unit that holds its privacy jointly or a set of individuals each with a witness of their own? This is the contest's panopticon question with the watcher reversed — the watcher is the child, invited by the mother, and on the child's side by law. A reader can hold that the household has always been the site of unaccountable power and that the marked bar — a parent's power to destroy, made visible — is the least the law can do; or that a mother must be able to speak to a baby at three in the morning and have it belong to no one, and that a law which makes every nursery a deposition room will have parents performing for an audience of one. Underneath: whether what she said that night was said to *him* at all — whether the witness made an addressee out of someone who did not yet exist — and whether a boy listening to three minutes of silence is hearing it anyway.
Not the obvious version: The teenager who wants privacy from surveilling parents; the tape that reveals abuse; the mother found out. Here the surveillance runs the other way, from cot to parent; nothing is found out, since she remembers it exactly and he is told; and her act is neither concealment nor confession but the separation of the two — the record destroyed, the fact spoken.
Closest published: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" — the father, the daughter, the recorded quarrel. There the record contradicts memory and the story turns on the father learning he was wrong. Here record and memory agree to the word, nothing is discovered, and the story turns on the difference between a thing known and a thing heard. Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun* has the watcher in the nursery, but Klara is the mind of that book; here the watcher is an instrument and the question is the law's.

### P-27 — cell 7 — The Hour
Conceit: In Nairobi, where a caregiver's unbroken model record is the first reference that has ever counted for anything, a woman with eleven years of it has one hour missing, because a dying man asked her to turn it off.

Obstacle: Two months before he died the old man asked her to switch her model off, switched his own off, and talked to her for an hour; she promised him no one would ever hear it. But in a record of eleven years the hour is the only place anything *could* have happened, so it is where Achieng looks, and the agency, and Doha. Nobody accuses her. They ask, reasonably, what the hour was. Her word — all she has left to explain it with — is exactly the thing the record was invented to replace. The gap cannot be filled, because there is no record of it; the promise cannot be shared, because the man it was made to is dead; and every other hour of eleven years was given freely, so the one she kept reads as the one she chose to hide.
Turn: She spends it. On a call to Leeds she tells Achieng the whole hour — Kisumu, the woman, what he said about his daughter — so that Achieng, who never had an unwatched hour with her father, now has his last one secondhand, and is grateful, and annotates Wairimu's record as a former employer: *gap explained; private conversation with the deceased at his request; confirmed by family*. The gap is closed. The hour that nobody in Nairobi recorded is now in Achieng's model, from Wairimu's mouth, for a reference. The reader gets the hour only this way, as it is being spent.
Last image: Doha, the first morning, an old woman in a bath chair, the new household's model on the shelf. Wairimu's hands are under the woman's arm and her voice is saying, in English, what her hands are doing — *I am lifting your arm now; I am washing under your arm* — which is what a good caregiver says to a patient, and also what a woman says who will never again leave an hour unaccounted for.
Question: When proof of innocence is free, does the presumption of innocence survive as a principle, or was it only ever a concession to the cost of knowing? This bears on the contest's developing-world question and its panopticon at once, because the record that reads her hour as guilt is the same record that made a Nairobi caregiver employable in Doha on her own eleven years and no one's word. A reader can hold that the presumption was a moral relation between a person and whoever judges her, and that a world which reads a gap as guilt has made everyone a suspect without accusing anyone; or that the presumption was only what we did when we could not know, that asking a woman to account for an hour is no more tyrannical than asking for a missing receipt, and that for someone with no other collateral the record is the first thing that ever made her word unnecessary — which is why she wants it. Underneath: whether the hour was hers to spend.
Not the obvious version: The innocent worker framed by the gap and vindicated; the gap that hides a crime; surveillance grinding down the poor. Here nothing happened, nobody accuses, the record is the best thing that has happened to her economically, and she spends the one unrecorded hour of her adult life to keep it that way.

There two accounts dispute; here there is no account at all, and the question is what an unrecorded hour is worth in an economy where every other hour is free. The last image knows it is near Stevens in Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*, who performs dignity for a class; Wairimu performs it for a record, and the record pays.

### P-28 — cell 7 — The Roof Fund
Conceit: In a Bucharest block where every resident's record has been open for a decade — because here, where people remember what a closed file meant, closing yours is what informers do — a neighbour threatens, over the vote on the roof repair fund, to have her model read a man's ten years and summarise for the building anything it ought to know.

Obstacle: The record is open by his own setting, and closing it the week the threat is made is the confession. Reading an open record is not against any law; it is what open means. He cannot argue the summary would be unfair, because it will be true — his own model would produce the same one. The block's norm, which he founded, rested on an assumption nobody said aloud: that no one would ever actually *read*. A life is ten thousand hours of nothing; who would bother. Dobre's model will read his ten years in the time it takes her kettle to boil. (Nicu, at thirty-one, read his father's Securitate file at the CNSAS and learned that the informer was the man then still living on the third floor. He knows what reading is.)
Turn: He calls a general meeting on the tenth-floor landing, under the leak, so they can see it, and puts a bylaw to the vote before the roof: no resident shall direct a model to read another resident's record, open or closed. He has to propose it in front of forty people who all know why. He cannot make the argument — he says he has been administrator nine years, and that everyone here has a kitchen, and stops; the story must not let him say more than that — and the hands make it for him: it passes, not because they love him but because every hand that goes up is a hand that has a kitchen, and all forty models record which hands. He has bought his obscurity with his innocence, on the record, with his name on the motion; the block has formally admitted it has things in its kitchens and agreed not to look. Then the roof vote, which he loses.
Last image: Forty hands up on the tenth-floor landing, Doamna Dobre's last, after she has looked around; above them the brown stain on the ceiling that the roof fund was for, which her nephew will now fix.

This sharpens the contest's "indefatigable surveillance": the tireless thing is not the camera but the reader, and there are now eight billion of them. A reader can hold that the information was public by his own choice, that no one has a right against another person's attention, that a right not to be read is a right to be a hypocrite at scale and the bylaw is forty people agreeing to be lied to; or that practical privacy was always obscurity — never that no one could know, only that no one would — and that a world of tireless readers has abolished it without touching a single secret, so that a polity's agreement not to look is the only privacy left. Underneath: whether the man in the kitchen is the man, and whether nine years of service or ten of contempt is the true record.

Here there is no state, no secret and no dissident; the panopticon is a neighbour with a kettle; what is exposed is not a crime but the ordinary unkindness of a marriage; and the remedy is not resistance but a vote to become a polity of the unread — a country that opened its files closing them again, one stairwell at a time.

There the state reads and a conscience answers; here anyone can read, no one's conscience is involved, and the answer is a bylaw. Kadare's *The Palace of Dreams* is the other neighbour — the reading of the private as a ministry — but it is still the state doing it.

### P-32 — cell 8 — The Attested Route
