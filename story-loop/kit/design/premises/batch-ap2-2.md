### P-41 — The Count of 1954
Conceit: In a canal town whose water has been divided since 1954 by a count everyone knows is false, the model gives every household the same true number in private, and the water-clerk who has kept the ledger must decide whether to set the sluice by it.
Who: Sadiq, sixty, water-clerk of a canal town in a dry country, the third of his family to hold the ledger. He wants to hand the office to his son and to die without a water war. The office, his quarter's majority on the council and the shrine's share all rest on a count made by a colonial officer in 1954: lower quarter 1,000 households, upper 1,100. The lower quarter has emptied since; the upper, on the road, has trebled with people down from the hills. The ledger has never been re-counted, because to re-count is to lose.
Obstacle: Nobody has petitioned; nobody needs to. For fourteen years every household in both quarters has asked the model, in private, how many of us are there, and it has answered each alike — from rooftops in the satellite pictures, from the school rolls, from what they have all told it: about 3,100 households up the canal, about 900 down. Before, everyone suspected. Now everyone knows that everyone asked. A suspicion can be lived beside; common knowledge cannot, and nobody made it. The model will not lie and cannot be un-asked. Asked what the law says, it says the shares are custom — whatever the town agrees — and the ministry has never heard of the canal. Sadiq cannot shift the shares a little, because a small change is now a statement about the size of the lie; and he cannot hold the old number, because his son has asked the model what happens if he does, and told him the answer at dinner.
Turn: The spring division. The upper quarter's young men stand at the sluice and ask him nothing. Sadiq has cut a new notch in the board that morning, his son holding the lamp, at a place the board has never been set. He sets it there and says nothing. The water goes where it has never gone; the lower orchards take less than they have ever taken; the ledger of 1954 is dead within the hour, and with it the office, which goes with the majority. His son will not be water-clerk.
Last image: The sluice board at the new notch — raw wood inside the cut, the old notches dark with decades of hands — and the water already through.
Question: Does a community have a right to its load-bearing fictions — a right not to count itself — or is every such fiction a settled majority's disenfranchisement with the word "peace" on it? And is a technology that answers everyone in private, identically, more corrosive to such fictions than a state that counts in public, because it turns suspicion into common knowledge without anyone deciding to? Bears on the panopticon question, inverted: no one is watched; everyone is answered. Underneath: whether a decent man ends a lie by saying it or by doing it, and whether he owed his son the lie.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI census that exposes a minority to the state, or the liberal fable in which the truth gives the majority its due. Here no one counts anyone and no one is exposed; the model tells each person only what they asked; the fiction dies not of revelation but of everyone knowing that everyone knows; and the man who ends it is the one it protected. The story does not say he was right.
Closest published: Achebe, Arrow of God — Ezeulu, keeper of the calendar, whose authority over the yam harvest breaks against a rival power. Differs: there is no colonizer and no refusal; Sadiq yields; and what he breaks against is not a power but a number everyone already had.

### P-30 — The Uncorrected
Conceit: The one thing a human teacher can still do that a tutor cannot is decline to catch a mistake, and an old mathematician does it, on purpose, to a child who has never once been wrong in public.
Who: Ágnes Bakos, 71, retired from the university in Debrecen, was a working mathematician in the 2020s and has spent her retirement with her tutor on one problem it cannot help her past. She wants to finish it; she reckons she has two years of good mind left. Then a letter comes from Zsófi, fourteen, in a village in Hajdú-Bihar, whose tutor, asked who could read her work, gave a name off a 2023 paper. The girl is past what the tutor can teach her and has never been wrong in a way that lasted. Ágnes wants her to become a mathematician — someone who can work where no one can check — and not the thing she is now, which is a solver.
Obstacle: The girl has been caught all her life. Every error since she was four was found by the tutor within the sentence, kindly; she has never submitted anything unchecked, never sat with a wrong thing for a week, never found out for herself that she was wrong. Ágnes cannot teach her to be uncaught by catching her. She can only ask her to work without the tutor — which the girl agrees to, because it is the first time anyone has asked her to be alone with a problem — and the girl agrees on the bargain that Ágnes will check instead. That bargain is the obstacle: the girl has traded a machine's net for a human one, and the only lesson left requires that there be no net. Ágnes's flat is the one room on earth where Zsófi can be wrong, and Ágnes is the only person who can allow it. The price on her side is the problem; there is not time for both.
Turn: The girl's first paper — a real result, small, hers. Ágnes reads it, finds the error on page nine, rings it in soft pencil on her printout, and says nothing. The girl posts the preprint. Someone's tutor, somewhere, finds the error within the day. The girl is now, permanently and in public, a person who has been wrong; the net she trusted was withheld by the hand she trusted; and the two years are spent.
Last image: The printout on Ágnes's side of the desk, the error ringed in pencil, the pencil laid beside it. Across the desk, the girl's chair.
Question: Whether a society that has automated the middle of the ladder — where correction is free, instant and total — can still produce the people who work at the top of it, where being wrong is the medium; whether expertise is possible at all for someone who has never been uncorrected; and whether anyone has the right to decide, for another person, that they must now be allowed to fail. (Bears on: the relation between knowing and being able; who reaches the frontier when the frontier is the last human job in knowledge; what teaching is once instruction is solved.) Underneath: whether that was teaching, or an old woman using a child to prove something before she died.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the harsh mentor vindicated — the girl becomes great and understands. This story does not vindicate. The error is only an error, the girl may not come back, and whether Ágnes was right is exactly what the reader is left holding. The other obvious version is the prodigy who outgrows a jealous machine; this machine is not jealous, it sent the girl, and it cannot do the one thing Ágnes does, which is nothing.
Closest published: Stoppard, *Arcadia* — a girl ahead of her tutor, and mathematics as the thing between them. What differs: Septimus is a human tutor who is behind her; here the human is the second tutor, arrived after the first has done all the teaching, and her one lesson is a silence. And *Arcadia* mourns the girl; this does not.

### P-52 — The Hand Count
Conceit: In a county where every voter has asked the same mind who to vote for, a candidate forecast to lose is given by that same mind the one change of position that would make her the advised choice, and must decide whether to be elected by computation or defeated in person.
Who: Dee Marchetti, 58, who runs the hardware store in a river town in eastern Washington and is running for county commissioner. She wants the seat, and she wants the seat for one thing: the diversion dam at Miller's Bend out, and the run of fish her grandfather saw back in the river before she is too old to stand in it.
Obstacle: Every voter in the county who cares to has asked the same mind who, given what they care about, they should vote for. The mind is honest and it is the same mind, so its answers add up to a forecast anyone's model can give: she loses by 340 out of about nine thousand, with a small margin because the only unadvised voters are the ones who never asked. Her model tells her, because she asks, that knocking on doors will move roughly nothing — no one is uninformed, and no one's priorities are waiting to be set. She knocks anyway. People are kind. They already know why they are voting the way they are, and it is the reason her own model gave for their side. The obstacle is not opposition; it is that there is no one left to persuade.
Turn: In a low week she asks her model what she would have to say to win, and it tells her — keep the dam, fund a fish passage, commission a phased study — and names the two hundred or so voters whose stated priorities she would then match. She says it. She wins by sixty. It cannot be undone: she is the commissioner, elected on a position she does not hold, by voters who never had to meet her — and the one man who did meet her, who told her on his porch that he would vote for her because she came and he had never asked the thing, voted for the woman who was going to take the dam out.
Last image: Election night in the high-school gym, the hand count going on at the long tables because the county charter still requires it, everyone in the room already knowing the number, and Dee across the floor watching the old man in the Carhartt jacket who is watching the count with real interest, because he is the only one who does not.
Question: Bears on what a vote is and what persuasion is for — whether an election in which every voter is honestly advised by the same mind is the completion of democracy or its end. A reader can hold that it is a census of interests with no demagogue, no ignorance and no spin, that persuasion was mostly manipulation and good riddance, and that a candidate who moves her position to meet the electorate is representation working as designed; or that a vote is an act of choosing a person and not a query, that the one unadvised vote in the county was the only real one, and that a commissioner elected by a mind she talked into electing her has been elected by nobody. Underneath: what does Dee owe the man on the porch, and is the dam still in her?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI that rigs the election, or the AI that predicts it and the human spirit that beats the prediction with one heroic upset. Here nothing is rigged, the forecast is honest and correct, and the human who beats it does so by giving the mind what it needs; the story is about what winning costs when losing was the only outcome that would have been hers.
Closest published: Isaac Asimov, "Franchise" — Multivac knows the electorate so well that one man's answers stand in for the whole vote. There the machine picks a single citizen to make the election real; here every citizen is advised and the single unadvised voter is what is left over, and it is the candidate, not the voter, who has to live with the result.

### P-47 — Pithead Baths
Conceit: A valley town's co-operative compute hall, built in the old pithead baths, is worthless as a mind and valuable as a boiler, and the man who wanted his town to own its own thinking must decide whether to let it own the warmth instead.
Who: Emlyn Vaughan, 52, the electrician who wired the hall in 2031 when the Rhondda Fach co-op spent the wind-farm money on four thousand second-hand GPUs and named the thing after the pit. He wants the hall to stay the valley's: for the town to be the one place in Wales that owns the machine it talks to, and for that to have been worth something.
Obstacle: The compute is worth nearly nothing. The frontier stopped in 2026; the co-op's model is the same model as everyone's phone, and the dividend is down to eleven pounds a member a year. The AGM will vote to sell the hall to a broker who will fill the racks with strangers' jobs, and the members, who are not idle but are tired, want the money. He cannot argue the hall is special, because it is not. Twelve years of the valley's conversations live on it and could be moved in an afternoon to an identical model in Reykjavik, and everyone knows it.
Turn: He reads the meters instead of the model and finds what the hall has actually been producing: forty per cent of the town's heat. The baths' pool, the greenhouses, the sheltered flats on Brook Street, all warmed off the racks since 2033 and never counted, because it had never been billed. At the AGM he proposes not the sale but a lease: the broker fills the racks with anyone's work and the valley keeps the heat, in perpetuity, written into the deed. It passes 340 to 12 in about four minutes. The valley's questions move to a cloud. The hall thinks for nobody they know and warms everyone they do. The deed is registered, the jobs are running, and the town's own words are gone from the machine it built to keep them.
Last image: Emlyn in the plant room at two in the morning with the job queue on the left of the screen, batch-render-ulaanbaatar-7, pension-scenario-4q, and the pool gauge on the right climbing toward twenty-nine degrees, and through the glass, in the steam, the over-seventies swimming club doing their lengths in water heated by a stranger's spreadsheet.
Question: A question of my own, bearing on ownership, rent and the tax base: when cognition is free, where does value pool, in the mind or in the physics under it, and if a valley ends up better off as the landlord of a boiler than as the owner of a brain, is that Henry George vindicated or two centuries of the Rhondda's history repeating with a smaller shovel? A reader can hold that the lease is a working social contract, the substrate held in common and global capital paying its rent in warmth, or a defeat with good plumbing. Underneath: what a man owes a machine he built to be talked to and now only maintains.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the town that loses its hall to capital, or keeps it and is redeemed. Here the town keeps the hall, loses the point of it, prospers, and wrongs no one; the strangeness is that the waste turned out to be the product, and that nobody had used the product well.
Closest published: I know no published story with this mechanism, heat as the true product of thought. Nearest in feeling is Richard Llewellyn's How Green Was My Valley, a valley's elegy for the thing it dug. What differs: there is no elegy. The valley is not ruined but reheated, and what is lost is a thing almost nobody in it used.

### P-34 — The Moderator
Conceit: A Vermont town's first floor meeting with attested proxies — residents' models attending in their place and voting their recorded preferences — sets the forty-one people who came against the nineteen hundred who told their models the truth and stayed home, and the Moderator must rule which of them is the town.
Who: Ruth Cotter, 71, Moderator for thirty years and, until she retired, the fourth-grade teacher at the school. She wants the floor meeting to survive: not the Australian ballot the state keeps pressing, but the hall, the articles read aloud, the ragged rising of hands. She backed the proxy pilot because turnout had fallen under five percent and the pilot was sold as the way to keep the hall open.
Obstacle: Article 7 is the school budget, voted down six years running by the hall, and this year the school closes if it fails. The town's private mind, spoken to its models over years of evenings, is for the school by a wide margin, because people told their models what they would never say in front of their neighbours. The proxies vote yes, 1,840 to 60. The forty-one in the room vote no, 30 to 11, and rise on a point of order: the statute says a floor vote is a vote of those present, and the pilot was a town article, not state law. The point is good. She cannot count the proxies and keep the hall, because the day the absent count is the day no one has a reason to come; she cannot rule them out and keep the school. Thirty years of neutrality have left her nothing to hide behind.
Turn: She rules the proxies advisory. Article 7 fails 30 to 11. The school is consolidated into the next town in August, and the ruling stands as precedent.
Last image: The next March. The hall is full for the first time since she was a girl — six hundred, standing at the back, coats on — because now, to count, you have to come. Article 1, election of Moderator. She calls the vote and six hundred hands go up in the same second: not the slow, looking-around rise of a real vote but one motion, every phone still warm from the same question. She stands there counting them.
Question: Democracy has always chosen between the will of those present and the will of all, and the choice stayed academic because the will of all could not be known. A model that has listened to a citizen for ten years can represent her considered view more faithfully than her attendance ever did, and for the first time the will of all is cheap and exact. Is presence — bodies, an evening given up, a hand raised in front of neighbours — the substance of self-government or its ceremony; and if the hall can be refilled but not un-minded, what was it she was keeping? (Bears on the cell's question of elections and of being a public; the question of my own is whether representation, made perfect, abolishes the thing it represents.) Underneath: what does she owe the forty-one who came, and what does she owe the nineteen hundred who told the truth to a machine and stayed home?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is AI proxies captured by a bad actor, or proxy democracy as the end of democracy, or as its perfection. Here the proxies are faithful and more generous than the room; the humans present are the obstacle; she kills the proxies to save the room; and the room comes back the next year as a proxy anyway.
Closest published: Asimov's "Franchise," in which Multivac chooses one citizen whose answers stand for the whole electorate. That story shrinks the electorate to a single representative man; this one represents everyone exactly and asks what the hall of bodies is for. Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" for the New England assembly, though its question is another.

### P-36 — Hakot
Conceit: In Manila, where a perfectly written petition costs nothing and so counts for nothing, the only political speech that registers is a body, and bodies are for hire — not to be seen by a public, since no one is watching, but to be photographed for the record that every model consults.
Who: Rowena, 34, of a barangay along an estero off the Pasig, three years in the hakot crowds — the hauled bodies: rallies, welcomes, prayer vigils, the yellow shirt, the blue shirt, a day's pay and a packed lunch, for anyone. She wants the fifty-thousand-peso reservation deposit on a unit at the relocation site in Bulacan, a door of her own with a lock, which the flood-control project that will clear her street offers to those who can pay it.
Obstacle: Her street sent the city four hundred petitions against the clearance, each written by a resident's model, each faultless; the city's model read them, found them faultless, and filed them. Nothing registers but bodies. The developer's last event before the demolition order is a residents' welcome on her own street — front row for real residents — to be photographed and put into the record, so that anyone who asks whether the community supported the project will be told that some two hundred residents gathered to welcome it. It pays what she still needs, and the deposit closes on Friday. Refusing changes nothing about the record, since the crowd is hired regardless, and everything about her door. The only public she has left is the neighbours who will watch from the doorways; they cannot afford the deposit, and for them the project is only the loss.
Turn: She stands in the front row in the project shirt, holds up the placard they hand her, and is photographed with her mouth open. Aling Nena and the others come out to the doorways to see. The photograph enters the record that afternoon.
Last image: Months on, in the unit in Bulacan, the lock on the door. She asks her model whether the residents of her old barangay supported the clearance, and it shows her the photograph and reads the caption: she is in front with the placard, and behind her, in the doorways, arms folded, are the neighbours who came out to watch — counted.
Question: When anyone can write the perfect petition, petitions are free and worthless, and the body is the last costly signal; and a crowd, in a world where no crowd is watching, exists to be cited. Is a demonstration assembled for the archive a corruption of political voice, or the honest form of what a demonstration always was — presence, purchased with buses and lunches — now that presence is the only currency left? And can the record, which is the only public, ever tell a witness from a participant? (Bears on the contest's questions about the developing world and the end of social media; the question of my own is what political speech costs when speech is free.) Underneath: what does a door of your own cost, and who is allowed to have seen you pay?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the poor protester crushed by the developer, or the hired body who finds her conscience and refuses. Here she stands on the developer's side, is paid, does not refuse, gets her door, and the rally's only audience is a photograph in which her neighbours' watching counts as consent.
Closest published: I cannot name a story about a hired crowd. The nearest in spirit is Nathanael West's *The Day of the Locust*, whose crowd comes to see and becomes the spectacle; here the crowd comes to be photographed and no one comes to see.
