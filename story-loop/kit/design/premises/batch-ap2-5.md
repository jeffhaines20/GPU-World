### P-28 — The Roof Fund
Conceit: In a Bucharest block where every resident's record has been open for a decade — because here, where people remember what a closed file meant, closing yours is what informers do — a neighbour threatens, over the vote on the roof repair fund, to have her model read a man's ten years and summarise for the building anything it ought to know.
Who: Nicu Stanciu, 66, retired tram driver, nine years administrator of a ten-storey block in Berceni. He wants the roof done before winter by the good contractor and not by Doamna Dobre's nephew, and he wants to go on being the man the block thinks he is: the honest one, the one who opened his record first, in 2030, and shamed the rest into following. What is in his record is not a crime. It is ten years of his kitchen — the running commentary every marriage has, said to his wife Lili at night about the people he has spent nine years serving: Dobre is "the cow," the couple on four are "the rabbits," the Ghiță boy is "the idiot" — some sixteen hundred instances; he asked his model to count, the night of the threat, and it did, as faithfully as it had kept them.
Obstacle: The record is open by his own setting, and closing it the week the threat is made is the confession. Reading an open record is not against any law; it is what open means. He cannot argue the summary would be unfair, because it will be true — his own model would produce the same one. The block's norm, which he founded, rested on an assumption nobody said aloud: that no one would ever actually *read*. A life is ten thousand hours of nothing; who would bother. Dobre's model will read his ten years in the time it takes her kettle to boil. (Nicu, at thirty-one, read his father's Securitate file at the CNSAS and learned that the informer was the man then still living on the third floor. He knows what reading is.)
Turn: He calls a general meeting on the tenth-floor landing, under the leak, so they can see it, and puts a bylaw to the vote before the roof: no resident shall direct a model to read another resident's record, open or closed. He has to propose it in front of forty people who all know why. He cannot make the argument — he says he has been administrator nine years, and that everyone here has a kitchen, and stops; the story must not let him say more than that — and the hands make it for him: it passes, not because they love him but because every hand that goes up is a hand that has a kitchen, and all forty models record which hands. He has bought his obscurity with his innocence, on the record, with his name on the motion; the block has formally admitted it has things in its kitchens and agreed not to look. Then the roof vote, which he loses.
Last image: Forty hands up on the tenth-floor landing, Doamna Dobre's last, after she has looked around; above them the brown stain on the ceiling that the roof fund was for, which her nephew will now fix.
Question: Is privacy a matter of secrets or of attention? If a record is open and nothing in it is a crime, is being *read* a harm — and if it is, was a right not to be read, which no law has ever granted, the real content of what we called privacy all along? This sharpens the contest's "indefatigable surveillance": the tireless thing is not the camera but the reader, and there are now eight billion of them. A reader can hold that the information was public by his own choice, that no one has a right against another person's attention, that a right not to be read is a right to be a hypocrite at scale and the bylaw is forty people agreeing to be lied to; or that practical privacy was always obscurity — never that no one could know, only that no one would — and that a world of tireless readers has abolished it without touching a single secret, so that a polity's agreement not to look is the only privacy left. Underneath: whether the man in the kitchen is the man, and whether nine years of service or ten of contempt is the true record.
Not the obvious version: The panopticon crushing the dissident; the state reading the file; the secret police. Here there is no state, no secret and no dissident; the panopticon is a neighbour with a kettle; what is exposed is not a crime but the ordinary unkindness of a marriage; and the remedy is not resistance but a vote to become a polity of the unread — a country that opened its files closing them again, one stairwell at a time.
Closest published: *The Lives of Others* (film, 2006) — a Stasi listener reads a life and is changed. There the state reads and a conscience answers; here anyone can read, no one's conscience is involved, and the answer is a bylaw. Kadare's *The Palace of Dreams* is the other neighbour — the reading of the private as a ministry — but it is still the state doing it.

### P-31 — The Word for the Griddle
Conceit: The tutor teaches the children of a Mixtec town their own language, patiently and all day, in a form no living village has ever spoken — and the woman who was once punished for speaking that language at school is the only person in town trained to teach a language, the other one.
Who: Ofelia Cruz, 52, has taught Spanish at the state primary in a town in the Mixteca Alta for twenty-six years. In 1995 she was made to stand in the corner of that same school for speaking Mixtec; she became the town's Spanish because Spanish was the road out, and she believed in the road. Now the road is not needed — the tutor translates everything, for everyone, for nothing — and the children learn at home in Mixtec from a machine that never tires. She wants her mother's Mixtec, the variant of this town, which the next valley does not quite understand, to be the one they learn.
Obstacle: The tutor's Mixtec is the corpus's Mixtec: the missionary New Testament, the 1980s grammar, the Academy's unified orthography, a written blend that leans toward whichever valley had a Bible. The state, which at last teaches Tu'un Sávi in its schools, has adopted that standard, because a standard is what a state can adopt. Ofelia puts the tutor on her mother's phone to record her — forty hours, the griddle, the plants, the stories — so that there is at least a file. The tutor takes it gladly and files it as a variant. It would speak the variant back if asked. Her mother does not ask. Her mother, who has never in seventy-five years had a listener with all day, talks to the thing in the evenings, and it answers in the standard, in the cadence of the Bible, and she learned in 1955 that the written thing is the correct thing. None of this can be removed: the tutor is what the children love, the standard is what saved the language, and Ofelia's training is in the wrong tongue.
Turn: At the comal with Ofelia's granddaughter, her mother says the word for the griddle — and it is the tutor's word, not the town's. She has been talking to Ofelia's recorder for a year. The child understands her perfectly. The town's word has gone out of the last mouth that had it, and it was Ofelia who put the thing in the house.
Last image: Her mother's mouth shaping the tutor's word, carefully, the way she was once made to shape the Spanish one; on the shelf above the comal, the phone, still recording.
Question: Whether a language saved by a standard has been saved or replaced — and whether the state's old monopoly on making citizens through school and tongue has ended, now that every child is taught privately in the family's language, or has only moved into the corpus, where a missionary's grammar homogenizes the valleys the way the nation once did, and no one voted for it and no one can vote it out. (Bears on: the developing world; what schools were for; whose version of a culture an automated preservation preserves.) Underneath: whether Ofelia can want the thing she was punished for, now that it has come back wearing someone else's mouth.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the tutor that saves the dying language, told warm, or the phone that ruins the village, told cold. Here the language is saved and replaced in one gesture, no one's life is worse, the school still stands, the tutor is beloved, and the agent of replacement is not a colonizer but a daughter's recorder and a grandmother's wish to be understood.
Closest published: Brian Friel, *Translations*. What differs: there the standardizers are soldiers with a map and what is lost is the name; here the standardizer is a patient machine that speaks the language better than the schoolteacher does, what is lost is the version, and the last hand on the eraser belongs to the family.

### P-38 — Forecasting Off
Conceit: A fifty-one-member mutual in Utrecht pays for the made-for-one molecules the national package excludes, on the old broodfonds principle that trust replaces underwriting — which by 2040 has come down to one rule: every member keeps their pocket doctor's forecasting switched off, so that nobody, not even themselves, knows who the fund is really for.
Who: Marijke, 58, physiotherapist, treasurer for nine years, one of the four who founded the fund when the insurers began pricing by forecast. She wants to know whether the tiredness is anything, which is a small want, and she wants the fund to outlast her tenure, which is not; both fit in one person until the evening she turns forecasting on.
Obstacle: The forecast names a slow blood cancer and drafts the molecule for it; the synthesis would cost within a few thousand euros of the fund's whole reserve, which she knows to the cent because she keeps it. The rule does not forbid claiming — any member may claim, and the fund exists to pay. It forbids knowing. She is now the only person in the fund who knows what her claim would do to it, and the ignorance of the other fifty is the thing that made the fund possible; she cannot give it back to them, and she cannot claim as if she still shared it.
Turn: At the quarterly meeting she reads out the reserve as usual, then says that she looked, and says the number. The fund votes on her claim by show of hands, and because she is treasurer she counts: twenty-seven against, twenty-two for, one abstaining. She records it. The reserve stands, and nothing about the count can be recounted.
Last image: The following month's contribution list on her screen — fifty-one names with "paid" beside every one of them, hers among them — and her marking it reconciled.
Question: Solidarity was always built on not knowing who would need it; now not knowing is a choice that has to be kept, like a fast. May a group require ignorance of its members as the price of belonging — and is a mutual that votes down its own treasurer betraying its purpose, or keeping it, for the fifty who will need it later? Underneath: what you owe the people whose ignorance you have left, and whether you can go on being the one who counts.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is adverse selection as elegy — the young look, the young leave, the fund of the old collapses — or the whistleblower who exposes a mutual built on wilful blindness. Here the blindness is a switch everyone can see, adopted with open eyes and openly reasoned, and it holds; what breaks is one person's ability to stand inside it. No insurer is the villain, no one is wrong, the vote is close, and the reasons in the room are various — some of the hands have looked too — and stay unspoken. Nobody says "veil of ignorance." And she does not leave.
Closest published: I do not know a close one. The nearest shape is Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" — a community rule everyone keeps, of which one person becomes the price. It differs everywhere that matters: the rule here is ignorance, freely chosen; the one who pays broke it herself; and the people who vote like her, and keep her on.

### P-46 — The Building Account
Conceit: A landlord's hall sets each tenant's rent from what that tenant asks the model that comes with the lease, so the tenants of one building bargain the only way left to them, by asking through a single shared login, and find that the price of not being seen alone is not being helped alone.
Who: Marisol Quintero, 44, a home-care aide in a twelve-storey block in Jackson Heights, Queens, paid better than the accountant on the third floor because bodies are scarce now and minds are not. She wants to stay in 7C. More exactly, she wants the rent to stop rising each year by precisely the amount she can bear, which it has done since the Dorans, who own eleven buildings and the hall in the basement of the third, began bundling a model with every lease the way heat used to be bundled.
Obstacle: It is legal and she consented. The city's own free model, which is the same model, tells her accurately that she has no case, and that the individually rational move is to stop asking the flat's model about money, which is useless advice, because the hall does not need her money questions: a woman who asks the price of the bus to the clinic on a Tuesday has already told it what it needs. She could ask a model that does not know her, and start nine years of her life over with a stranger, and the rent would rise anyway.
Turn: The building goes to one account. Sixty-one households agree to ask the flat's model only through a single login, set up by the girl in 9F, so that the hall sees one tenant with sixty-one lives and cannot price any of them. It works: the renewals come in at the citywide rate, because the hall's model, facing an incoherent tenant, defaults. And every answer gets worse, generic, hedged, addressed to no one, because nothing now knows who is asking. To keep anyone from smuggling in the question that would reprice them all, the tenants agree that everything asked through the account is visible to everyone in the building. It cannot be undone: once the hall has seen them merge, going back would show it exactly who blinked first.
Last image: A sheet of paper taped to the wall of the fourth-floor landing where the building queues its questions for the account in its own handwriting, a custody hearing above, a mole below, and Marisol reading the ones above her own, which is about her mother's medication, while she waits her turn.
Question: A question of my own, bearing on unions, rent, and the panopticon seen from the pricing side rather than the policing side: when what capital wants from you is not your labour but your legibility, what does a union withhold, and what does solidarity cost when the machine's help was only ever good because it knew you alone? A reader can hold that the building account is the true union of the century, or that it is a poverty of its own: sixty-one people who bought a flat rent with the one adviser that ever knew them, and must now be known by each other instead. Underneath: whether Marisol can bear to be read by her neighbours instead of by the hall.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the tenant crushed by the algorithmic landlord, or the plucky tenants who hack the box. Nobody here breaks anything and nobody is crushed; the tenants win. The story is what winning looks like when the weapon is withholding the self rather than withholding work, and the trophy is a wall of your neighbours' questions.
Closest published: Cory Doctorow's "Unauthorized Bread": the tenants of one building against the landlord's technology. What differs: Doctorow's tenants jailbreak the appliances and it is a victory. Here nothing is broken, the only move is to become illegible together, and the cost lands on the thing the tenants liked most, which was being helped one at a time.

### P-26 — Three Minutes Eleven Seconds
Conceit: In Denmark a child's model is on the child's side from birth and hands the whole record to the child at fifteen; a parent may redact anything before the handover, but every redaction is permanently marked — the content destroyed, the gap labelled with its length and its timestamp.
Who: Signe Aalund, 44, a dental hygienist in Aarhus. She wants her son Villum — born in October 2026, the month after the models stopped getting better; handover in five weeks — to have all fifteen years of her except three minutes and eleven seconds: a night in March 2027 when he was five months old and she, alone with him at ten past three, feeding him, told him at length and in detail that she did not want him, that she had thought about leaving him at the hospital, that she was going to give him to her sister. She remembers every word. She has never needed to play it; she knows it is there because it happened, and because his model — set up by her, at the hospital, on his side — was in the room.
Obstacle: A redaction is a black bar. A three-minute-eleven-second bar in his fifth month, labelled *redacted by parent*, will be the first thing he goes looking for and the only thing in fifteen years he cannot open; she knows him; he will ask. Whatever she says the bar holds, her own model will hear her say it and his will too, so a lie about the gap is on the record forever. The alternative is to leave it in, and let him hear his mother, in a voice he will know, in his own first year, in his own record, on his side, saying it. The marking rule cannot be got around, and she does not want it got around: it was written after parents quietly laundered their children's infancies, she supported it, it is a good rule. Her own model holds the same three minutes; she could delete her copy any time; she never has.
Turn: She redacts it, and then — the night before the handover, at the kitchen table, in two sentences — she tells him what it holds. Not the recording: the fact. That she said, when he was five months old, that she did not want him; that she will not let him hear it; that it is gone. The audio is destroyed and the sentence is said, and the two now exist apart: he knows what she said, and he will never hear her say it. It cannot change back in either direction.
Last image: The morning of the handover, the model's light changing from the household white to the blue he picked. He goes straight to the fifth month. He puts his headphones on over the black bar and plays it — three minutes and eleven seconds of nothing, the counter running — and she watches his face while he listens to what she took out.
Question: Whose is the record of a shared life? Does a child's right to the whole of his witnessed infancy outweigh a parent's need to have said the unsayable to someone who could not yet hear it, and is the family a unit that holds its privacy jointly or a set of individuals each with a witness of their own? This is the contest's panopticon question with the watcher reversed — the watcher is the child, invited by the mother, and on the child's side by law. A reader can hold that the household has always been the site of unaccountable power and that the marked bar — a parent's power to destroy, made visible — is the least the law can do; or that a mother must be able to speak to a baby at three in the morning and have it belong to no one, and that a law which makes every nursery a deposition room will have parents performing for an audience of one. Underneath: whether what she said that night was said to *him* at all — whether the witness made an addressee out of someone who did not yet exist — and whether a boy listening to three minutes of silence is hearing it anyway.
Not the obvious version: The teenager who wants privacy from surveilling parents; the tape that reveals abuse; the mother found out. Here the surveillance runs the other way, from cot to parent; nothing is found out, since she remembers it exactly and he is told; and her act is neither concealment nor confession but the separation of the two — the record destroyed, the fact spoken.
Closest published: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" — the father, the daughter, the recorded quarrel. There the record contradicts memory and the story turns on the father learning he was wrong. Here record and memory agree to the word, nothing is discovered, and the story turns on the difference between a thing known and a thing heard. Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun* has the watcher in the nursery, but Klara is the mind of that book; here the watcher is an instrument and the question is the law's.
