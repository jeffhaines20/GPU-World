### P-32 — The Attested Route
Conceit: A licensing board will let a candidate's tutor vouch for her in place of the exam, at the price of the whole record of how she learned — and she finds out that the exam had been the last wall behind which a mind could be slow in private.
Who: Achieng Otieno, 29, a structural engineer in Nairobi, has designed real buildings for six years under a registered engineer's stamp, with her tutor. She wants her own stamp. She has failed the professional examination twice, and not from nerves: in the Faraday room she is not stupid, she is someone who has thought in dialogue since she was twenty-three, and the exam measures a person who does not exist and whom no client will ever hire — her, alone.
Obstacle: The Engineers Board's new attested route, brought in because buildings did not stop falling down when the exams got harder, accepts the tutor's assessment of six years of actual work in place of the exam, on one condition: the full learning record, unredacted, every exchange since she first opened the thing, released to the board and retained. A redacted record, the board reasons, is only an exam the candidate has set herself. There are no crimes in the record. There is the six months she asked the same question about shear walls nine different ways; the nights she asked whether she was good enough; what she asked about her boss; the shape of her mind at 3 a.m. across six years, which is the truth about her and nobody's business. The exam, she sees now, was what kept it nobody's business: behind it you could be as slow as you liked, so long as you came out the other side.
Turn: She releases it. The board licenses her — and the determination letter quotes the record: three moments cited as evidence of competence, and one of them is the night in 2036 she had taken for the bottom, which the board reads as persistence. Her private failure is her public qualification, on letterhead, in a file, retained.
Last image: The letter on the table, the seal at the top, and below it, in the board's font, between quotation marks, a question she asked at three in the morning four years ago.
Question: Whether the machine that taught you should be allowed to speak for you — whether a fair society certifies what people can actually do, by the only witness that knows, or whether a mind's history is not a credential and abolishing the exam abolishes the last unwatched place in a person's education. (Bears on: what credentials are for when knowing is free; the panopticon question from its blind side — the surveillance you ask for and are helped by.) Underneath: whether Achieng can carry a stamp made out of the nights she was ashamed of.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the record used against her — the tutor as informer. Here the record is used for her, it works, the board is decent and probably right, she gets what she wanted, and what she has lost has no name yet. The other obvious version is the death of exams as liberation; here the exam turns out to have been a kindness nobody had noticed until it was gone.
Closest published: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" — a perfect record of a life and what it does to the one who kept it. What differs: Chiang's record is private and the question is what was true; this record is handed to an institution as a qualification, and the question is who may read, and whether a credential can be made from a mind's history without making the history public property.

In 1995 she was made to stand in the corner of that same school for speaking Mixtec; she became the town's Spanish because Spanish was the road out, and she believed in the road. Now the road is not needed — the tutor translates everything, for everyone, for nothing — and the children learn at home in Mixtec from a machine that never tires. She wants her mother's Mixtec, the variant of this town, which the next valley does not quite understand, to be the one they learn.
Obstacle: The tutor's Mixtec is the corpus's Mixtec: the missionary New Testament, the 1980s grammar, the Academy's unified orthography, a written blend that leans toward whichever valley had a Bible. The state, which at last teaches Tu'un Sávi in its schools, has adopted that standard, because a standard is what a state can adopt. Ofelia puts the tutor on her mother's phone to record her — forty hours, the griddle, the plants, the stories — so that there is at least a file. The tutor takes it gladly and files it as a variant. It would speak the variant back if asked. Her mother does not ask. Her mother, who has never in seventy-five years had a listener with all day, talks to the thing in the evenings, and it answers in the standard, in the cadence of the Bible, and she learned in 1955 that the written thing is the correct thing. None of this can be removed: the tutor is what the children love, the standard is what saved the language, and Ofelia's training is in the wrong tongue.
Turn: At the comal with Ofelia's granddaughter, her mother says the word for the griddle — and it is the tutor's word, not the town's. She has been talking to Ofelia's recorder for a year. The child understands her perfectly. The town's word has gone out of the last mouth that had it, and it was Ofelia who put the thing in the house.
Last image: Her mother's mouth shaping the tutor's word, carefully, the way she was once made to shape the Spanish one; on the shelf above the comal, the phone, still recording.
Question: Whether a language saved by a standard has been saved or replaced — and whether the state's old monopoly on making citizens through school and tongue has ended, now that every child is taught privately in the family's language, or has only moved into the corpus, where a missionary's grammar homogenizes the valleys the way the nation once did, and no one voted for it and no one can vote it out. (Bears on: the developing world; what schools were for; whose version of a culture an automated preservation preserves.) Underneath: whether Ofelia can want the thing she was punished for, now that it has come back wearing someone else's mouth.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the tutor that saves the dying language, told warm, or the phone that ruins the village, told cold. Here the language is saved and replaced in one gesture, no one's life is worse, the school still stands, the tutor is beloved, and the agent of replacement is not a colonizer but a daughter's recorder and a grandmother's wish to be understood.
Closest published: Brian Friel, *Translations*. What differs: there the standardizers are soldiers with a map and what is lost is the name; here the standardizer is a patient machine that speaks the language better than the schoolteacher does, what is lost is the version, and the last hand on the eraser belongs to the family.

### P-50 — Two Hundred and Twelve Days
Conceit: In a youth-justice conference where the offender's remorse is certified by his own model's record of every private conversation he has had about it, the victim finds she can no longer honestly withhold forgiveness, and must decide whether a forgiveness that is owed is still hers to give.
Who: Ruth Tolley, 44, a hand-engraver in Dunedin, who lost the sight of her right eye when a boy dropped a bottle from a footbridge and it came through her windscreen. She wants two things that cannot both be had: to keep her anger, which is the only thing that has made the seven months bearable, and to have the family group conference the state has offered give her something. What she says she wants is to hear him say it. What she wants is to go on not forgiving him with a clear conscience.
Obstacle: The boy, Tyler, 17, has asked that his model's record be shown — everything he has said to it since the night — because he wants to be believed and knows no apology he speaks will be. The facilitator's summary is plain: in 212 days he has raised it on 190; he asked for help with an apology, refused three drafts and said he would rather say it badly; he has asked eleven times whether she can still work. Ruth's own model, asked privately whether such a record could be gamed, tells her truthfully that in principle yes, that this one shows no sign of it, and that its confidence is high. She cannot un-know this. The economy she grew up in let you withhold forgiveness because you could not see inside the other person. Now she can, and there is nothing in there that licenses her to keep what she is keeping.
Turn: In the room Tyler says it badly, as he said he would. And Ruth, who came in having decided on the words that would let him walk out unforgiven, hears herself say "I know you are," and means it, and feels the anger go out of her like a pulled tooth, and cannot get it back. The forgiveness is real, and it is the loss.
Last image: Ruth at her bench that evening with the loupe over her good eye, cutting a date into the inside of a wedding band, the blind eye open beside it, the conference summary still lit on the screen at her elbow: remorse assessment, high.
Question: Bears on the moral economy of apology and forgiveness — what remorse is worth, and what forgiveness is, once sincerity can be witnessed rather than guessed. A reader can hold that the old economy ran on ignorance and the new one on truth, and that a victim who withholds forgiveness from a boy she knows to be sorry is simply being cruel; or that a forgiveness that is owed is a tax, and the record that makes it owed has taken from the victim something the bottle did not reach. Underneath: what was the anger for, and whom was it protecting?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI-drafted apology that fools the meter and not the victim, or the victim and not the meter — sincerity as a forgery problem. Here nothing is forged: the boy is sorry, the record is honest, the woman's own advisor is honest with her, and the story is about what a verified truth does to an act that used to depend on not being sure.
Closest published: Ted Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" — an apology and a recording that does not match memory. There the record corrects the wronged party's own memory and the theme is the fallibility of feeling; here the record certifies the wrongdoer's interior, and the problem is not accuracy but obligation — what a true record demands of the person it is shown to.

### P-44 — Third on the List
Conceit: A community health worker in a village that has always rationed care by who came first must submit her cases to the district's triage ranking, which puts her own cousin's child at the top and is right.
Who: Meseret, forty, ten years the health worker for a village a day's road from the district hospital. She wants to send the neighbor's son in the hospital's one pickup this month, because he came first, and for ten years whoever came first has gone. What she has is the bench outside her house and the referral slips; what she decides is who rides. The village has called that fair because it was luck.
Obstacle: Since 2038 the hospital takes only ranked referrals: she submits her cases, the district's triage model orders them by expected benefit, the pickup comes for the first name, and the ranking is shown to any family that asks. This month there are three cases and one seat. The model puts her cousin's daughter first — the child has the thing that gets worse fast — and the neighbor's son third, because he will be fine. It is right; she can see that it is right. There is an override for clinical judgment, which requires a written reason, and "it will look bad" is not one. If she overrides, the sicker child stays and the district sees her send the well one; if she does not, the village sees her cousin's child go first with her own name under it as submitting worker. The model cannot be removed — the pickup comes for the top name only — and it cannot be argued with, because it is not wrong.
Turn: She submits without overriding. The pickup takes her cousin's child. The neighbor takes his son to the district by bus with the money he had saved for the roof, and the boy is treated and is fine — which proves nothing, since he would have been. What cannot change back is not the seat. It is that the village has seen a choice and, having seen one, now reads her ten years of luck as ten years of choosing.
Last image: The hospital pickup coming back up the track with the dust behind it and her cousin's child in the front seat; every doorway in the village with someone standing in it, the neighbor's too.
Question: Is explicit, optimal rationing more just than the implicit rationing of chance and acquaintance — even where making the choice visible destroys the legitimacy the old way borrowed from luck? Bears on the healthcare question where scarcity is the ordinary condition: whether correctness can substitute for procedure, and whether a place that has never had enough is better served by a rule it can see or by a rule it can pretend is nobody's. A reader can hold that the sickest child should go and nothing else matters, or that a village needs its rationing to be no one's fault. Underneath: whether she can be fair when being fair looks exactly like its opposite, and whether she should have been wrong on purpose.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI doctor that is wrong, or right and hated, or right and useless for want of the drug, or the drone that brings it. Here the model is right and not hated and the drug is not the point; what is resented is that there was a choice at all, that it was visible, and that the visible one looks like the invisible ones used to; and the child who was third is fine.
Closest published: Godwin, "The Cold Equations" — the computation is right and lethal and the story sides with it. Differs: here the computation is right and no one dies; what it costs is a health worker's standing and a village's ability not to know that it chooses. (Jackson's "The Lottery" is the inverse: chance made ritual and horror; this is what goes when chance is replaced by reasons.)

### P-28 — The Roof Fund
Conceit: In a Bucharest block where every resident's record has been open for a decade — because here, where people remember what a closed file meant, closing yours is what informers do — a neighbour threatens, over the vote on the roof repair fund, to have her model read a man's ten years and summarise for the building anything it ought to know.
Who: Nicu Stanciu, 66, retired tram driver, nine years administrator of a ten-storey block in Berceni. He wants the roof done before winter by the good contractor and not by Doamna Dobre's nephew, and he wants to go on being the man the block thinks he is: the honest one, the one who opened his record first, in 2030, and shamed the rest into following. What is in his record is not a crime. It is ten years of his kitchen — the running commentary every marriage has, said to his wife Lili at night about the people he has spent nine years serving: Dobre is "the cow," the couple on four are "the rabbits," the Ghiță boy is "the idiot" — some sixteen hundred instances; he asked his model to count, the night of the threat, and it did, as faithfully as it had kept them.
Obstacle: The record is open by his own setting, and closing it the week the threat is made is the confession. Reading an open record is not against any law; it is what open means. He cannot argue the summary would be unfair, because it will be true — his own model would produce the same one. The block's norm, which he founded, rested on an assumption nobody said aloud: that no one would ever actually *read*. A life is ten thousand hours of nothing; who would bother. Dobre's model will read his ten years in the time it takes her kettle to boil. (Nicu, at thirty-one, read his father's Securitate file at the CNSAS and learned that the informer was the man then still living on the third floor. He knows what reading is.)
Turn: He calls a general meeting on the tenth-floor landing, under the leak, so they can see it, and puts a bylaw to the vote before the roof: no resident shall direct a model to read another resident's record, open or closed. He has to propose it in front of forty people who all know why. He cannot make the argument — he says he has been administrator nine years, and that everyone here has a kitchen, and stops; the story must not let him say more than that — and the hands make it for him: it passes, not because they love him but because every hand that goes up is a hand that has a kitchen, and all forty models record which hands. He has bought his obscurity with his innocence, on the record, with his name on the motion; the block has formally admitted it has things in its kitchens and agreed not to look. Then the roof vote, which he loses.
Last image: Forty hands up on the tenth-floor landing, Doamna Dobre's last, after she has looked around; above them the brown stain on the ceiling that the roof fund was for, which her nephew will now fix.
Question: Is privacy a matter of secrets or of attention? If a record is open and nothing in it is a crime, is being *read* a harm — and if it is, was a right not to be read, which no law has ever granted, the real content of what we called privacy all along? This sharpens the contest's "indefatigable surveillance": the tireless thing is not the camera but the reader, and there are now eight billion of them. A reader can hold that the information was public by his own choice, that no one has a right against another person's attention, that a right not to be read is a right to be a hypocrite at scale and the bylaw is forty people agreeing to be lied to; or that practical privacy was always obscurity — never that no one could know, only that no one would — and that a world of tireless readers has abolished it without touching a single secret, so that a polity's agreement not to look is the only privacy left. Underneath: whether the man in the kitchen is the man, and whether nine years of service or ten of contempt is the true record.
Not the obvious version: The panopticon crushing the dissident; the state reading the file; the secret police. Here there is no state, no secret and no dissident; the panopticon is a neighbour with a kettle; what is exposed is not a crime but the ordinary unkindness of a marriage; and the remedy is not resistance but a vote to become a polity of the unread — a country that opened its files closing them again, one stairwell at a time.

There the state reads and a conscience answers; here anyone can read, no one's conscience is involved, and the answer is a bylaw. Kadare's *The Palace of Dreams* is the other neighbour — the reading of the private as a ministry — but it is still the state doing it.
