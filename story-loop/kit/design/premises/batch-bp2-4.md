### P-26 — Three Minutes Eleven Seconds
Conceit: In Denmark a child's model is on the child's side from birth and hands the whole record to the child at fifteen; a parent may redact anything before the handover, but every redaction is permanently marked — the content destroyed, the gap labelled with its length and its timestamp.
Who: Signe Aalund, 44, a dental hygienist in Aarhus. She wants her son Villum — born in October 2026, the month after the models stopped getting better; handover in five weeks — to have all fifteen years of her except three minutes and eleven seconds: a night in March 2027 when he was five months old and she, alone with him at ten past three, feeding him, told him at length and in detail that she did not want him, that she had thought about leaving him at the hospital, that she was going to give him to her sister. She remembers every word. She has never needed to play it; she knows it is there because it happened, and because his model — set up by her, at the hospital, on his side — was in the room.
Obstacle: A redaction is a black bar. A three-minute-eleven-second bar in his fifth month, labelled *redacted by parent*, will be the first thing he goes looking for and the only thing in fifteen years he cannot open; she knows him; he will ask. Whatever she says the bar holds, her own model will hear her say it and his will too, so a lie about the gap is on the record forever. The alternative is to leave it in, and let him hear his mother, in a voice he will know, in his own first year, in his own record, on his side, saying it. The marking rule cannot be got around, and she does not want it got around: it was written after parents quietly laundered their children's infancies, she supported it, it is a good rule. Her own model holds the same three minutes; she could delete her copy any time; she never has.
Turn: She redacts it, and then — the night before the handover, at the kitchen table, in two sentences — she tells him what it holds. Not the recording: the fact. That she said, when he was five months old, that she did not want him; that she will not let him hear it; that it is gone. The audio is destroyed and the sentence is said, and the two now exist apart: he knows what she said, and he will never hear her say it. It cannot change back in either direction.
Last image: The morning of the handover, the model's light changing from the household white to the blue he picked. He goes straight to the fifth month. He puts his headphones on over the black bar and plays it — three minutes and eleven seconds of nothing, the counter running — and she watches his face while he listens to what she took out.
Question: Whose is the record of a shared life? Does a child's right to the whole of his witnessed infancy outweigh a parent's need to have said the unsayable to someone who could not yet hear it, and is the family a unit that holds its privacy jointly or a set of individuals each with a witness of their own? This is the contest's panopticon question with the watcher reversed — the watcher is the child, invited by the mother, and on the child's side by law. A reader can hold that the household has always been the site of unaccountable power and that the marked bar — a parent's power to destroy, made visible — is the least the law can do; or that a mother must be able to speak to a baby at three in the morning and have it belong to no one, and that a law which makes every nursery a deposition room will have parents performing for an audience of one. Underneath: whether what she said that night was said to *him* at all — whether the witness made an addressee out of someone who did not yet exist — and whether a boy listening to three minutes of silence is hearing it anyway.
Not the obvious version: The teenager who wants privacy from surveilling parents; the tape that reveals abuse; the mother found out. Here the surveillance runs the other way, from cot to parent; nothing is found out, since she remembers it exactly and he is told; and her act is neither concealment nor confession but the separation of the two — the record destroyed, the fact spoken.
Closest published: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" — the father, the daughter, the recorded quarrel. There the record contradicts memory and the story turns on the father learning he was wrong. Here record and memory agree to the word, nothing is discovered, and the story turns on the difference between a thing known and a thing heard. Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun* has the watcher in the nursery, but Klara is the mind of that book; here the watcher is an instrument and the question is the law's.

### P-35 — Five O'Clock
Conceit: In a depopulating Japanese town where the loudspeaker poles of the disaster radio are the last thing that reaches everyone whether they asked or not, the man who runs them has one week before a merger vote and one channel that must never say anything.
Who: Ōmori, 46, of the crisis-management section, fifteen years running the town's bōsai musen: the landslide warnings, the wandering-grandmother notices, and at five every afternoon the chime, "Yūyake Koyake," which tells eight thousand people at once that it is five. He wants the town not to be dissolved on the 14th into the city across the river, its name gone from the poles and the badges and the chime.
Obstacle: The merger is in the record, fairly summarised by any model to anyone who asks, and nobody asks, because nothing in 2040 arrives unbidden: the neighbourhood associations are dormant, the sound trucks are gone, the notices sit in the record like water in a well. Turnout will be the council, the developers, and the old. He does not know the town's mind; he knows only that it has not been made to think. His one channel is trusted because it speaks only for disasters, and its whole worth is that silence: a political word from the poles would make them a sound truck, and the next landslide warning one more thing to ask about later. And there are the regulations, and his job, and the fact that he agrees with the regulations.
Turn: At five on the 8th he does not play "Yūyake Koyake." He plays the chime of the city across the river. He says nothing — he cannot — and eight thousand people hear the wrong tune at once, and at once ask the same mind why, and it tells each of them: no change is recorded; that tune is the one used across the river; the town votes on the 14th on a merger under which the two systems would be consolidated. The town asks. Four hundred people come to the community centre on the 11th, the first meeting there in years. He is suspended on the 9th.
Last image: Some weeks after the vote, whichever way it went. Five o'clock, his balcony, the poles. The chime is wrong again — a tune he did not choose and does not know, played by someone he cannot name — and on the street below, people stop and look up at the speakers, and he takes out his phone to ask.
Question: The 2040 settlement has abolished the unbidden — no feed, no headline, no truck, no siren but the real one — and calls this freedom, and it is one. Can a public exist without any channel that reaches everyone whether they like it or not; and if one such channel remains, is it a commons to be kept silent or a weapon waiting for its first use? (Bears on the contest's social-media question; the question of my own is whether a society of askers can ever learn what it did not know to ask.) Underneath: what does a man owe a place that has not asked him to save it, and what does he owe the silence he was paid to keep?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the state commandeering the loudspeaker, or the officer's stirring broadcast that saves the town. Here he cannot speak and does not; he changes a tune; the town does the asking and the deciding; and the channel, once spent, is spent for whoever comes next.
Closest published: Forster's "The Machine Stops," the nearest world I know: a society that receives only what it summons. Forster's people have stopped wanting the unbidden; these have been given a better thing to ask, and the story is about the last siren and the man who keeps its silence. Bradbury's "The Murderer" is the mirror image, a man smashing a world of nothing but push.

### P-38 — Forecasting Off
Conceit: A fifty-one-member mutual in Utrecht pays for the made-for-one molecules the national package excludes, on the old broodfonds principle that trust replaces underwriting — which by 2040 has come down to one rule: every member keeps their pocket doctor's forecasting switched off, so that nobody, not even themselves, knows who the fund is really for.
Who: Marijke, 58, physiotherapist, treasurer for nine years, one of the four who founded the fund when the insurers began pricing by forecast. She wants to know whether the tiredness is anything, which is a small want, and she wants the fund to outlast her tenure, which is not; both fit in one person until the evening she turns forecasting on.
Obstacle: The forecast names a slow blood cancer and drafts the molecule for it; the synthesis would cost within a few thousand euros of the fund's whole reserve, which she knows to the cent because she keeps it. The rule does not forbid claiming — any member may claim, and the fund exists to pay. It forbids knowing. She is now the only person in the fund who knows what her claim would do to it, and the ignorance of the other fifty is the thing that made the fund possible; she cannot give it back to them, and she cannot claim as if she still shared it.
Turn: At the quarterly meeting she reads out the reserve as usual, then says that she looked, and says the number. The fund votes on her claim by show of hands, and because she is treasurer she counts: twenty-seven against, twenty-two for, one abstaining. She records it. The reserve stands, and nothing about the count can be recounted.
Last image: The following month's contribution list on her screen — fifty-one names with "paid" beside every one of them, hers among them — and her marking it reconciled.
Question: Solidarity was always built on not knowing who would need it; now not knowing is a choice that has to be kept, like a fast. May a group require ignorance of its members as the price of belonging — and is a mutual that votes down its own treasurer betraying its purpose, or keeping it, for the fifty who will need it later? Underneath: what you owe the people whose ignorance you have left, and whether you can go on being the one who counts.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is adverse selection as elegy — the young look, the young leave, the fund of the old collapses — or the whistleblower who exposes a mutual built on wilful blindness. Here the blindness is a switch everyone can see, adopted with open eyes and openly reasoned, and it holds; what breaks is one person's ability to stand inside it. No insurer is the villain, no one is wrong, the vote is close, and the reasons in the room are various — some of the hands have looked too — and stay unspoken. Nobody says "veil of ignorance." And she does not leave.
Closest published: I do not know a close one. The nearest shape is Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" — a community rule everyone keeps, of which one person becomes the price. It differs everywhere that matters: the rule here is ignorance, freely chosen; the one who pays broke it herself; and the people who vote like her, and keep her on.

### P-37 — Physician of Record
Conceit: By 2040 the last thing a human doctor does that the pocket cannot is sign, and the signing has been offshored: on a night shift in Cebu, a physician licensed under an Ohio compact is physician of record for four thousand patients she will never meet, and the refusal she has never made is the safeguard the law requires.
Who: Dr. Rowena Dizon, 41, in a converted call centre in Cebu IT Park, six years and roughly two hundred thousand approvals in. She wants to press the other button once — not because she disagrees with the recommendation in front of her, but because she has never pressed it and, six years in, no longer knows whether it is connected to anything.
Obstacle: The loop is honest, which is the obstacle. Under the compact a refusal by the physician of record is not rerouted to the next signer; it halts the order and schedules a review by an in-state clinician within seventy-two hours. The company does not punish refusals; it only counts them. Nothing stands between her and the button except that the only way to learn whether it works is to use it on someone.
Turn: At 3:40 a.m. she declines a moderate-confidence recommendation to start an anticoagulant for a sixty-one-year-old man in Zanesville, Ohio, with a newly detected arrhythmia, and leaves the reason box empty. The review is set for day three. On day two he has the stroke the drug was meant to prevent — or one it might not have prevented; the record cannot say, and no one ever will be able to. The notification comes to her because she is physician of record; her name is on it. No one reprimands her. The safeguard did exactly what it was written to do. He is dead, she knows the button is real, and she signs the next one.
Last image: She looks up his street. A one-storey house with a ramp to the front door and a car in the drive, on a road she has never walked, in a country she has never been to.
Question: When a machine's decisions require a human signature, is the human a safeguard or a place to put the blame — and if a veto can only be proven real by someone dying of it, is it worth having? Behind that, an economic fact nobody in the story names: responsibility has become the developing world's export, as nursing and the night-shift phone were before it. Underneath, personally: what a person will spend, and whose, to find out that she exists.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the exposé — she refuses, the system routes around her in ninety seconds, the human in the loop is revealed as a fig leaf, she quits or blows the whistle. Here nothing is revealed; the loop was always honest. Her refusal holds, the review is scheduled, and the only thing the system teaches her is that she was real all along, which is the one thing she cannot use. ### P-36 — Hakot
Conceit: In Manila, where a perfectly written petition costs nothing and so counts for nothing, the only political speech that registers is a body, and bodies are for hire — not to be seen by a public, since no one is watching, but to be photographed for the record that every model consults.
Who: Rowena, 34, of a barangay along an estero off the Pasig, three years in the hakot crowds — the hauled bodies: rallies, welcomes, prayer vigils, the yellow shirt, the blue shirt, a day's pay and a packed lunch, for anyone. She wants the fifty-thousand-peso reservation deposit on a unit at the relocation site in Bulacan, a door of her own with a lock, which the flood-control project that will clear her street offers to those who can pay it.
Obstacle: Her street sent the city four hundred petitions against the clearance, each written by a resident's model, each faultless; the city's model read them, found them faultless, and filed them. Nothing registers but bodies. The developer's last event before the demolition order is a residents' welcome on her own street — front row for real residents — to be photographed and put into the record, so that anyone who asks whether the community supported the project will be told that some two hundred residents gathered to welcome it. It pays what she still needs, and the deposit closes on Friday. Refusing changes nothing about the record, since the crowd is hired regardless, and everything about her door. The only public she has left is the neighbours who will watch from the doorways; they cannot afford the deposit, and for them the project is only the loss.
Turn: She stands in the front row in the project shirt, holds up the placard they hand her, and is photographed with her mouth open. Aling Nena and the others come out to the doorways to see. The photograph enters the record that afternoon.
Last image: Months on, in the unit in Bulacan, the lock on the door. She asks her model whether the residents of her old barangay supported the clearance, and it shows her the photograph and reads the caption: she is in front with the placard, and behind her, in the doorways, arms folded, are the neighbours who came out to watch — counted.
Question: When anyone can write the perfect petition, petitions are free and worthless, and the body is the last costly signal; and a crowd, in a world where no crowd is watching, exists to be cited. Is a demonstration assembled for the archive a corruption of political voice, or the honest form of what a demonstration always was — presence, purchased with buses and lunches — now that presence is the only currency left? Here she stands on the developer's side, is paid, does not refuse, gets her door, and the rally's only audience is a photograph in which her neighbours' watching counts as consent.
Closest published: I cannot name a story about a hired crowd. The nearest in spirit is Nathanael West's *The Day of the Locust*, whose crowd comes to see and becomes the spectacle; here the crowd comes to be photographed and no one comes to see.
