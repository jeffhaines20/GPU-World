### P-43 — The Talker's Language

Dolma married in from the next valley, three hours' walk: same language, a different word for fifty things, and on each side a word for the people over there. Her father-in-law, Tashi, dead two years, was the valley's talker — its self-appointed historian, its bore, the man who knew what everyone's grandfather had done and said so. He spent his last six years telling the model everything, every day, correcting its grammar, singing to it, and giving it his opinion of Dolma's village.
Obstacle: The model's version of the language is mostly him — the linguists' recordings were thin and the other speakers were quiet — and the model's version is now the language. The district's mother-tongue hour uses it because it is the only version written down; the university has it; the school has it. Her own valley's words are in it as "the other way of saying," and marked, faintly, with his view of that. The other speakers are old, few and, increasingly, also talking to the model. The only teacher with the hours is the model, and Yangchen prefers it — it is patient and it is always there — while Dolma has evenings and is tired. She cannot take the old man out of the language; he is what it is made of. She could refuse the model and teach the child herself, in the hours she does not have, in a dialect the school will mark wrong.
Turn: At table, Yangchen uses Tashi's word for her mother's village — a word that means, roughly, "the ones who talk wrong" — not in malice; in her language that is simply the place's name. To correct her, Dolma has to use the model's word to be understood by her own daughter. It cannot be untaught. The child's first language is his.
Last image: Evening. Yangchen on the floor with the device, telling it about her day in the language, and it answering. Dolma in the doorway with the tea things, stopped.

Underneath: whether she can give her daughter a thing she can only give in the version of a man she disliked.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the app that saves the dying language, or the children who drop it for the model's English, or the grief-bot that speaks in a dead man's voice. Here the language lives and that is the problem; no one grieves Tashi, least of all Dolma; and the model is not him — it is the language, which is now him.

### P-45 — The Name
Conceit: Every load-bearing drawing in 2040 must carry a licensed human's stamp, the stamps are priced on an insurers' market by the signer's record of refusal, and so judgment is paid for only in the form of its evidence.
Who: Adaeze Nwosu, 61, a structural engineer in Yaba, Lagos, trained to size a beam with a pencil and now stamping about nine hundred drawings a day for buildings in Surabaya, Fort Worth and Kigali, because a Lagos licence is the cheapest body the global insurers will accept as liable. She wants to read one. One drawing, properly, the way she was taught, before she retires.
Obstacle: Her income is the insurers' rate times her throughput. The rate is set by the model's error rate, which has not changed since 2026 and is known the way the yield strength of steel is known, and by her refusal profile, which is zero. A signer who reads is slow; a slow signer is undercut by a cheaper name in a cheaper jurisdiction. And reading one makes the eight hundred and ninety-nine she did not read, in law and in her own head, unread. Nothing forbids her to look; the system makes looking an admission.
Turn: She reads one. It is wrong: a transfer beam whose load path the model inherited from a superseded revision, exactly the error it is documented to make once in a few hundred thousand drawings. She refuses it; the refusal is logged and the error confirmed. Within a week the insurers reprice her. A name with a true refusal on record is worth six times a name with none, not because it will catch the next one, but because it can be shown to a jury to have caught one. Her queue goes from nine hundred a day to eleven thousand. Her own model now applies the stamp; she is required to be alive and licensed. The single act of engineering she has done in a decade is what makes engineering impossible for her for the rest of her life, and her wage is the proof.
Last image: A counter on the wall of her flat ticking through the day's stamps, six thousand and something by lunch, and pinned beneath it the one drawing she caught, with her hand calculation in pencil in the margin, which she reads again while her name goes onto eleven thousand things she will never see.
Question: A question of my own, bearing on work, professions and liability, and on the developing world as the place where a liable body is cheapest: when the law insists on a human to blame for work no human did, does it buy accountability or a market in its likeness, and is the likeness, at scale, perhaps the same thing? A reader can hold that Adaeze's system is the only honest check a civilisation can afford at eleven thousand drawings a day (a body that can refuse, priced by proof that it has), or that it launders machine decisions through a woman's name. Underneath: what it is to have been good at something exactly once.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the rubber-stamp engineer whose building falls and who takes the fall. Here nothing falls, she is right, and being right is what finishes her, because the market pays so well for demonstrated judgment that it prices judgment out. The system is not corrupt. It is efficient.
Closest published: Melville's "Bartleby, the Scrivener": a man paid to copy documents he did not write, whose one act is refusal. What differs: Bartleby's refusal is total, unexplained, unpaid, and ends him. Adaeze's is singular, correct and priced, and ends only the part of her that could do it again. Bartleby's employer does not know what to do with a refusal; Adaeze's insurers know exactly.

### P-25 — The Sunset
Conceit: Ireland has voted, by referendum, that every personal model must permanently delete whatever it witnessed more than seven years ago, the only exemption being a record already lodged with the State, and a woman who voted for it has six days before the one night she cannot let go of turns seven.
Who: Nuala Gormley, 55, farms half of a split holding outside Drumshanbo, Co. Leitrim; her brother Diarmuid farms the other half, across a ditch they both walk. She wants the record of one night in November 2033 kept — the night, over the division of their father's land, he put her against the wall of her own kitchen and told her, at length, what he would do — and she wants never to have to use it. She has never once played it. She wants it to exist the way a letter exists in a drawer: so that what happened does not depend on her.
Obstacle: The Sunset Amendment makes no provision for private keeping. There is no private copy, no export, no exemption for consent — the drafters had seen what a consent exemption does to a woman with a brother, and closed it, to protect her. Her model will delete the night at midnight on the seventh anniversary, and the only thing that stops it is a record lodged with the State — a Garda statement, a complaint, a court file — which is the one thing she has refused for seven years to make, because he is her brother and the ditch is still there and the whole parish drinks in the same two pubs. The week before, Diarmuid rings. He is not threatening; he is relieved, and he makes her an offer: after Thursday it is only your word and mine, and I will never contradict you; say what you like about that night and I will say yes, that is how it was. He is offering her the story in exchange for the proof. The obstacle cannot be removed because she believes in the law. She voted for it three years after that night, on the argument — still, she thinks, a good one — that a people has to be able to stop knowing things about each other. She is not against the Sunset. She is its exception.
Turn: On the sixth day she drives to the Garda station in Carrick-on-Shannon and lodges it. She lets her model play the night to a sergeant who watches it without a word (the record is never played for the reader), and it becomes the State's — the one item in her record that will never expire — and Diarmuid, who was a day from being a man who had never done it, is now named in a file in Dublin that will outlast them both. Under the amendment a lodged record cannot be withdrawn; that was the point, so that nobody could be leant on to un-lodge. She wanted to keep it and never use it; the only way to keep it was to use it.
Last image: Her kitchen that night, the model's memory view open on the table: the timeline going grey at the seven-year edge the way it does every night, and on the far side of the edge, alone in the grey where her mother's last Christmas and the calf and the whole of that year have already gone, one night still lit, with the small harp of the State on it, and the edge moving away from it at the rate of a day a day.
Question: Should a society mandate forgetting? Is the statute of limitations a moral principle — a people must be able to stop knowing about each other, a man must be able to become someone who never did it — or a concession to the old cost of remembering, obsolete now that the witness is perfect; and when the only exempt memory is the State's, has private memory been abolished or merely made honest? This turns the contest's panopticon question over: not whether the State will watch, but whether it should be the only one allowed to remember. A reader can hold that Diarmuid's relief is the law working, and that the last image shows what her remembering cost — the whole year gone grey to keep the wound; or that his relief is exactly what the law was always going to be for. Underneath: whether she wanted the proof or the story, and which of the two of them was right to want it gone.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the race against the deadline — the deletion as villain, the abuser wiping the tape, the courtroom scene where the record is played. Here nobody fights the law, the victim voted for it and would again, the brother is not a villain but a man who has waited seven years to be forgiven by a clock, and the story's cruelty is that in this world the only way to keep a private thing is to make it public.

### P-41 — The Count of 1954
Conceit: In a canal town whose water has been divided since 1954 by a count everyone knows is false, the model gives every household the same true number in private, and the water-clerk who has kept the ledger must decide whether to set the sluice by it.
Who: Sadiq, sixty, water-clerk of a canal town in a dry country, the third of his family to hold the ledger. He wants to hand the office to his son and to die without a water war. The office, his quarter's majority on the council and the shrine's share all rest on a count made by a colonial officer in 1954: lower quarter 1,000 households, upper 1,100. The lower quarter has emptied since; the upper, on the road, has trebled with people down from the hills. The ledger has never been re-counted, because to re-count is to lose.
Obstacle: Nobody has petitioned; nobody needs to. For fourteen years every household in both quarters has asked the model, in private, how many of us are there, and it has answered each alike — from rooftops in the satellite pictures, from the school rolls, from what they have all told it: about 3,100 households up the canal, about 900 down. Before, everyone suspected. Now everyone knows that everyone asked. A suspicion can be lived beside; common knowledge cannot, and nobody made it. The model will not lie and cannot be un-asked. Asked what the law says, it says the shares are custom — whatever the town agrees — and the ministry has never heard of the canal. Sadiq cannot shift the shares a little, because a small change is now a statement about the size of the lie; and he cannot hold the old number, because his son has asked the model what happens if he does, and told him the answer at dinner.
Turn: The spring division. The upper quarter's young men stand at the sluice and ask him nothing. Sadiq has cut a new notch in the board that morning, his son holding the lamp, at a place the board has never been set. He sets it there and says nothing. The water goes where it has never gone; the lower orchards take less than they have ever taken; the ledger of 1954 is dead within the hour, and with it the office, which goes with the majority. His son will not be water-clerk.
Last image: The sluice board at the new notch — raw wood inside the cut, the old notches dark with decades of hands — and the water already through.
Question: Does a community have a right to its load-bearing fictions — a right not to count itself — or is every such fiction a settled majority's disenfranchisement with the word "peace" on it? Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI census that exposes a minority to the state, or the liberal fable in which the truth gives the majority its due. Here no one counts anyone and no one is exposed; the model tells each person only what they asked; the fiction dies not of revelation but of everyone knowing that everyone knows; and the man who ends it is the one it protected. The story does not say he was right.
