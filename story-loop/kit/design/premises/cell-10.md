### P-37 — cell 10 — Physician of Record
Conceit: By 2040 the last thing a human doctor does that the pocket cannot is sign, and the signing has been offshored: on a night shift in Cebu, a physician licensed under an Ohio compact is physician of record for four thousand patients she will never meet, and the refusal she has never made is the safeguard the law requires.
Who: Dr. Rowena Dizon, 41, in a converted call centre in Cebu IT Park, six years and roughly two hundred thousand approvals in. She wants to press the other button once — not because she disagrees with the recommendation in front of her, but because she has never pressed it and, six years in, no longer knows whether it is connected to anything.
Obstacle: The loop is honest, which is the obstacle. Under the compact a refusal by the physician of record is not rerouted to the next signer; it halts the order and schedules a review by an in-state clinician within seventy-two hours. The company does not punish refusals; it only counts them. Nothing stands between her and the button except that the only way to learn whether it works is to use it on someone.
Turn: At 3:40 a.m. she declines a moderate-confidence recommendation to start an anticoagulant for a sixty-one-year-old man in Zanesville, Ohio, with a newly detected arrhythmia, and leaves the reason box empty. The review is set for day three. On day two he has the stroke the drug was meant to prevent — or one it might not have prevented; the record cannot say, and no one ever will be able to. The notification comes to her because she is physician of record; her name is on it. No one reprimands her. The safeguard did exactly what it was written to do. He is dead, she knows the button is real, and she signs the next one.
Last image: She looks up his street. A one-storey house with a ramp to the front door and a car in the drive, on a road she has never walked, in a country she has never been to.
Question: When a machine's decisions require a human signature, is the human a safeguard or a place to put the blame — and if a veto can only be proven real by someone dying of it, is it worth having? Behind that, an economic fact nobody in the story names: responsibility has become the developing world's export, as nursing and the night-shift phone were before it. Underneath, personally: what a person will spend, and whose, to find out that she exists.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the exposé — she refuses, the system routes around her in ninety seconds, the human in the loop is revealed as a fig leaf, she quits or blows the whistle. Here nothing is revealed; the loop was always honest. Her refusal holds, the review is scheduled, and the only thing the system teaches her is that she was real all along, which is the one thing she cannot use. There is no medical disagreement to vindicate or refute her: the recommendation is never shown to have been right or wrong, and nobody says "human in the loop."
Closest published: Melville, "Bartleby, the Scrivener." Bartleby's refusal is total, unexplained and costs only himself, and the narrator cannot get rid of him. Hers is single, unexplained, costs a stranger in another hemisphere, and the next night she is back at the desk, approving.

### P-38 — cell 10 — Forecasting Off
Conceit: A fifty-one-member mutual in Utrecht pays for the made-for-one molecules the national package excludes, on the old broodfonds principle that trust replaces underwriting — which by 2040 has come down to one rule: every member keeps their pocket doctor's forecasting switched off, so that nobody, not even themselves, knows who the fund is really for.
Who: Marijke, 58, physiotherapist, treasurer for nine years, one of the four who founded the fund when the insurers began pricing by forecast. She wants to know whether the tiredness is anything, which is a small want, and she wants the fund to outlast her tenure, which is not; both fit in one person until the evening she turns forecasting on.
Obstacle: The forecast names a slow blood cancer and drafts the molecule for it; the synthesis would cost within a few thousand euros of the fund's whole reserve, which she knows to the cent because she keeps it. The rule does not forbid claiming — any member may claim, and the fund exists to pay. It forbids knowing. She is now the only person in the fund who knows what her claim would do to it, and the ignorance of the other fifty is the thing that made the fund possible; she cannot give it back to them, and she cannot claim as if she still shared it.
Turn: At the quarterly meeting she reads out the reserve as usual, then says that she looked, and says the number. The fund votes on her claim by show of hands, and because she is treasurer she counts: twenty-seven against, twenty-two for, one abstaining. She records it. The reserve stands, and nothing about the count can be recounted.
Last image: The following month's contribution list on her screen — fifty-one names with "paid" beside every one of them, hers among them — and her marking it reconciled.
Question: Solidarity was always built on not knowing who would need it; now not knowing is a choice that has to be kept, like a fast. May a group require ignorance of its members as the price of belonging — and is a mutual that votes down its own treasurer betraying its purpose, or keeping it, for the fifty who will need it later? Underneath: what you owe the people whose ignorance you have left, and whether you can go on being the one who counts.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is adverse selection as elegy — the young look, the young leave, the fund of the old collapses — or the whistleblower who exposes a mutual built on wilful blindness. Here the blindness is a switch everyone can see, adopted with open eyes and openly reasoned, and it holds; what breaks is one person's ability to stand inside it. No insurer is the villain, no one is wrong, the vote is close, and the reasons in the room are various — some of the hands have looked too — and stay unspoken. Nobody says "veil of ignorance." And she does not leave.
Closest published: I do not know a close one. The nearest shape is Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" — a community rule everyone keeps, of which one person becomes the price. It differs everywhere that matters: the rule here is ignorance, freely chosen; the one who pays broke it herself; and the people who vote like her, and keep her on.

### P-39 — cell 10 — The Quarter Slot
Conceit: Everyone in the catchment of a health centre under Mulanje mountain already knows, from the phone in their hand, the name and stage and price of what is wrong with them and the exact molecule that would fix it; the Ministry, through a donor programme, sends each centre the authorisation for one made-for-one synthesis a quarter, and the nurse in charge has to give it to somebody.
Who: Chikondi, 44, nurse-midwife technician in charge, whose authority for twenty years was that she knew things, and who now holds the key to a cupboard of forty generics for people who arrive already knowing the names of what she cannot give them. She wants the slot out of her hands: to give it, by the Ministry's ranking, to the man at the top of the list, and have it be over until the rains.
Obstacle: The ranking is public — every pocket runs the same model, so who is first and who is ninth is known in every village before she opens the list. The man at the top, a tea picker of thirty-four with a leukaemia the design would very likely cure, will not take it. Not for anyone else, and not for any reason he will give her beyond that everyone would know it was him. The programme allows her to reallocate once within the window; second on the list is a girl of nine.
Turn: On the last day of the window she has the girl's name on the screen and does not put her thumb to the reader. She lets the authorisation lapse at midnight and go back to the national pool — the first centre in the programme's history to return one, as the district officer's message says. The girl's molecule is not made; the quarter is over; and the centre is now a place that has once refused a rescue, which every phone in the catchment knows by morning.
Last image: The morning line under the eaves of the centre, thirty people each holding a phone that knows the name of what would save them, and him among them, waiting his turn for what the cupboard has.
Question: When everyone can see exactly what they lack and there is enough for one, is a rescue performed in the sight of the others a good or a wound — better that one is saved while all watch, or that none is — and who has the standing to decide that for a whole line: the one at the top, or the one with the key? This is the developing world's actual 2040 question, which is not access to diagnosis but the new precision of poverty: the poor now know the name of what they are not getting. Underneath: whether to be the one.
Not the obvious version: The village saved or ruined by a phone is forbidden and is not this; nor the nurse who must choose and chooses her own child, nor the QALY-maximising algorithm overruled by a warm human heart, nor the donor exposed as a fraud. The phones are right, the ranking is fair, the donor is sincere and the molecule would work. What is strange is that the man refuses, and stranger that she agrees with him. Nobody says "levelling down," or "envy," or "witchcraft"; whatever the reasons are, they stay in the line.
Closest published: Le Guin, "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." There the many are kept happy by one who suffers and the conscientious walk away; here one would be saved in the sight of the many, refuses, and nobody walks anywhere — the line re-forms in the morning.

### P-40 — cell 10 — The Ceiling
Conceit: Once molecules made for one had made "nothing more can be done" false for everyone, forever, Taiwan's national insurance began requiring every adult to name, while well, the most it would spend on a drug designed for them alone — printed on the card like a blood type, the premium scaled to it, raisable only after a five-year wait — so that every death would at least be a number somebody had chosen.
Who: Yu-hsuan, 25, a junior engineer in Tainan, in the year she is required to set her own number. She wants her mother to live, and she has the money: the gap between her mother's ceiling and the cost of the design is almost exactly the deposit she has saved for a flat.
Obstacle: The scheme lets a family member pay the gap, with the patient's consent, which cannot be given on anyone's behalf; and a made-for-one design can only be ordered through the scheme that holds the record it was made from. Her mother, 58, who did alterations out of the front room for thirty years and set her number low in 2029 so the premium would leave more for her daughter, will not sign the consent. Not from despair; she says the number was the point, and that she chose it. The design's order window is thirty days, because the tumour drifts and the molecule with it.
Turn: On day nineteen her mother asks to be driven to the district office and, at the counter, locks her ceiling — the scheme's permanent lock, which rebates a little premium and which no gap payment, from anyone, can ever again pass. It is the thing the poor do for the rebate. She does it so that her daughter will have nothing left to decide. The clerk reads the lock back to her twice, as the procedure requires, and she confirms it twice, and the daughter, sitting beside her, has the deposit still, and will always have it.
Last image: The same counter, the same clerk, who has noticed from the ID that the daughter is due this year and asks if she would like to do hers now, since she is here. The form on the counter screen, the field for the number, and what she has typed in it, which is her mother's number.
Question: Rationing by self-declaration — is a limit you set on your own body while well the most honest form of rationing a society could have, or the way a society gets its poor to consent to dying cheaply and calls it choice? Under it, the question every such scheme has to answer and cannot: whether the well person who chose the number and the sick person who must live under it are the same person, and who decides when they disagree — the earlier self, the later one, or the daughter who loves them both. Underneath, personally: whether honouring your mother means honouring her number or her life.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the mother who dies because the molecule costs more than the family has, or the daughter who sells everything to buy it. Here the money is there and is not the obstacle; the mother's own earlier choice is, and a scheme that honours it, and then the mother's second choice, which is the same choice made harder. Nobody speaks of autonomy or dignity; the scheme is not villainous, it is the least bad answer anyone has found to a real problem, and the story does not find a better one. The daughter neither rebels against the number nor argues with it. She takes it.
Closest published: Tolstoy, "The Death of Ivan Ilyich," for a household arranging itself around a death and the lie everyone tells. Here nobody lies — the number is on the card, the lock is on the record, the day count is on the app — and the trouble is entirely the truth. I do not know a nearer one and would rather say so than invent it.
