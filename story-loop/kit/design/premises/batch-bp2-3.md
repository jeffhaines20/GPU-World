### P-42 — The Clean Book
Conceit: A moneylender's daughter inherits her father's ledger, runs it by the model's fair advice, and empties it in a year — and learns that the debts were what tied the town to her door.
Who: Kesar, thirty-four, back from the city to a market town in western India when her father's heart went. What she wants is the house he had: the bench outside where people waited, the standing, the being the one who is asked. Her father was the town's sahukar for forty years; her brother sends money from Dubai and has no intention of coming home. She means to be a better sahukar than her father — fairer, cleaner, a woman of the new kind — and she has the model to keep the book.
Obstacle: The book ran on a gap. Her father lent at his rate, collected at his discretion, and each Diwali, after the new ledger was blessed, tore one page from the old one in front of the street — a forgiveness that was also a display, and that bound the forgiven to the house for a generation. The model closes the gap from both sides at once. It keeps her book more forgivingly than he did, in total, and does it precisely, privately, by message, with no page to tear. And every borrower carries the same mind, which tells each of them what a fair settlement is: the number she sees. She cannot be unfair — not because she would be caught, but because there is no longer a number that isn't known. She cannot be her father either, because his discretion was a kind of secret, and there are no secrets about sums. Her brother, whose remittances come home through the same model, says: clear it, take the capital, put it in the cold store on the highway — it is what Papa would have done with the tool.
Turn: One morning in the monsoon she sends forty-one settlement offers, drafted by the model, each fair to the rupee; the same mind in forty-one pockets advises acceptance. By Diwali the book is clean. No one is in her debt, and no one is bound to her house.
Last image: Diwali night: the new account book, marked with vermilion and blank, on the shelf where her father's stood; Kesar in the lit street with a plate of sweets, greeted the way anyone is greeted.
Question: Is debt a bond or a burden — is a town's web of owing what makes it a community, or what keeps its poor in their place — and is a fairness that closes every account a liberation or a dissolution? Bears on what perfect, cheap, impartial credit does to societies whose cohesion ran on partial, expensive, personal credit: a reader can hold that the sahukar's forgiveness was domination and good riddance, or that a ledger is not the only thing a town keeps its accounts in. Underneath: what she is when no one owes her, and whether her father was loved or obeyed.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the lending app that ruins the moneylender, or the app that becomes the new moneylender and is worse. Here the model is on everyone's side, including hers; it does not compete with her, it makes her fair; and what fairness empties is not her purse — she is richer — but the bench.
Closest published: Premchand, Godaan — Hori's debt, told from beneath. Differs: told from the lender's side, in a town where the model has made the lender honest; the debt is not the crusher but the tie; and the ending is settlement, not ruin.

### P-33 — Nobody Ran
Conceit: A cooperative bank in a Kerala market town is emptied in a week by eight thousand members taking the same prudent advice from the same patient mind, and there is no panic for its secretary to stand in the doorway and calm.
Who: Sreelatha, 54, secretary of the town's cooperative bank for twenty-six years, who has lent the town's money to the town on her knowledge of who repays. She wants the members to leave their deposits where they are until the 5th, when the quarterly instalments come in and the filings will show the loan book sound.
Obstacle: The rubber-processing plant that employed a third of her borrowers closed in July; the closure is in the record — the filings, the notices, the news, everything the models read — and the repayments are not yet. Asked "is my money safe," every member's model gives the same measured answer: the guarantee limit, the plant, "you may wish to hold no more than the insured amount here." The Gulf families who hold most of the deposits do the measured thing, one household at a time, on the same afternoon. There is no queue, no rumour, no fear: nothing to answer. She knows the borrowers will pay because she knows the borrowers, and that knowledge is exactly the kind the record cannot hold; a secretary's word about her own bank is, to the mind everyone asks, the claim of an interested party. She cannot stand in the road and tell eight thousand calm people not to be calm.
Turn: On the night of the 4th, with the bank three days from the liquidity line at which the registrar steps in, she asks her own model about her own retirement deposit — as a member, not as a test — and it tells her what it told everyone. At 23:51 she moves the portion above the guarantee to the state cooperative bank, which is the reasonable thing, and which is timestamped. On the 5th the instalments arrive in full into a bank already below the line, and the state bank absorbs it by noon.
Last image: The afternoon of the 5th, the sign already changed. Sudhakaran, who owes on three acres of rubber, counting his instalment out in notes onto the same counter, on time, and Sreelatha taking them.
Question: The old public sphere carried panic, and panic could be met with a face and a voice; the new one carries prudence, and prudence cannot be argued with. When every person consults the same careful mind, collective judgment loses the independence that made crowds occasionally wise, and the witness who knows more than the record loses her standing entirely. Is a public that trusts the record over the interested witness safer — the manager on the steps was usually lying — or has it only made its collapses quieter, more correct, and impossible to stop? (Bears on the contest's social-media question, what replaces the crowd, and on an economic question of my own: whether markets and institutions require that people disagree.) Underneath: what does it cost to keep acting on knowledge no one else will honour, and at what hour does it become prudent to stop?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is a run started by AI-generated rumour, or an AI adviser that is wrong. Here the advice is right for every person who receives it, nobody panics, and the bank is killed by eight thousand correct decisions taken in the same week; the secretary's testimony is true and worth nothing, and her one act of prudence is the thing the record keeps.
Closest published: The run on the Building & Loan in *It's a Wonderful Life* (Capra, from Philip Van Doren Stern's "The Greatest Gift"), where George Bailey holds the crowd with his word and his honeymoon money. This is that scene with no crowd on the other side of the counter and no standing for the word, and she hands her money the other way.

She wants the slot out of her hands: to give it, by the Ministry's ranking, to the man at the top of the list, and have it be over until the rains.
Obstacle: The ranking is public — every pocket runs the same model, so who is first and who is ninth is known in every village before she opens the list. The man at the top, a tea picker of thirty-four with a leukaemia the design would very likely cure, will not take it. Not for anyone else, and not for any reason he will give her beyond that everyone would know it was him. The programme allows her to reallocate once within the window; second on the list is a girl of nine.
Turn: On the last day of the window she has the girl's name on the screen and does not put her thumb to the reader. She lets the authorisation lapse at midnight and go back to the national pool — the first centre in the programme's history to return one, as the district officer's message says. The girl's molecule is not made; the quarter is over; and the centre is now a place that has once refused a rescue, which every phone in the catchment knows by morning.
Last image: The morning line under the eaves of the centre, thirty people each holding a phone that knows the name of what would save them, and him among them, waiting his turn for what the cupboard has.
Question: When everyone can see exactly what they lack and there is enough for one, is a rescue performed in the sight of the others a good or a wound — better that one is saved while all watch, or that none is — and who has the standing to decide that for a whole line: the one at the top, or the one with the key? This is the developing world's actual 2040 question, which is not access to diagnosis but the new precision of poverty: the poor now know the name of what they are not getting. Underneath: whether to be the one.

Nobody says "levelling down," or "envy," or "witchcraft"; whatever the reasons are, they stay in the line.

### P-49 — The Court of Protection
Conceit: An eighty-one-year-old hill farmer with advancing dementia runs his farm competently through the model he has talked to for fourteen years, and the Court of Protection must decide whether a competence that lives in the tool is his.
Who: Alec Ridley, 81, four hundred acres of fell above the North Tyne in Northumberland, sheep and a few suckler cows, a widower. He wants to die on the farm and not in the place in Hexham with the keypad on the door, and he wants the farm run right until then — the dosing, the lambing, the payment forms — which it is, because the thing in his ear remembers what he cannot. His daughter Jean, who loves him and who found him in the yard in his pyjamas at four on a November morning, has applied to be his deputy for property and affairs.
Obstacle: The Mental Capacity Act asks whether a man can understand, retain, use or weigh, and communicate the information a decision needs — and says he must not be found unable until every practicable step to help him has been tried, and that retaining the information only briefly does not count against him. The model is a practicable step. With it he can do all four; without it he cannot hold the price of a gimmer for the length of an auction. Jean's case is that the model is not helping him decide, it is deciding. His case is that he decided, fourteen years ago, to trust it, and the deciding has gone well. Neither can simply be right, because the Act was written for help that was a person in the room, and a person in the room can be asked to step outside.
Turn: The district judge asks that the model be switched off for the rest of the hearing, so the court can see Mr Ridley. Alec agrees; he is proud and he thinks he will do well. For forty minutes in a room in Newcastle his daughter, his solicitor and the judge watch a man who cannot find the year, who calls Jean by her mother's name, who explains lambing beautifully and then explains it again. Then it is switched back on and he is himself, and he knows what they have seen. The judge finds that he has capacity, with the tool, as a man with spectacles has sight. Everyone leaves the room having seen him without it, and no order of the court can put that back.
Last image: Alec driving the Land Rover home up the fell road he has driven for sixty years, the quiet voice in his ear telling him, kindly, to take the left fork at the cattle grid, and him taking it.
Question: Bears on legal personhood — what capacity and autonomy are when a person's competence lives in a tool they cannot function without. A reader can hold that the extended mind is simply the mind (spectacles, hearing aids, a notebook, a wife who remembered the dates) and that the law should stop looking inside skulls; or that capacity was always a claim about a self, and a self that cannot be found for forty minutes is being run, not helped, and a state that calls that autonomy has abandoned a man to a machine that will keep him plausible until he walks into the beck. Underneath: is Jean's application love or theft, and which of the two men in that room is her father?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI that manages an old person's life while the family argues over crutch or cage, ending on the pathos of decline — or the machine fails and the daughter was right. Here the machine does not fail, the ruling is legally correct and arguably wise, and what is lost is not competence but exposure: the tool's success is what makes the man's absence visible, and the court's remedy — switch it off and look — is the cruelty.
Closest published: Daniel Keyes, Flowers for Algernon — a competence that comes and goes, seen from inside. There the competence was in Charlie and its loss was the story; here it is in the tool and stays, and the story is a law trying to say whose it is. The non-fiction source is Clark and Chalmers's Otto and his notebook, made into a hearing.

### P-46 — The Building Account
Conceit: A landlord's hall sets each tenant's rent from what that tenant asks the model that comes with the lease, so the tenants of one building bargain the only way left to them, by asking through a single shared login, and find that the price of not being seen alone is not being helped alone.
Who: Marisol Quintero, 44, a home-care aide in a twelve-storey block in Jackson Heights, Queens, paid better than the accountant on the third floor because bodies are scarce now and minds are not. She wants to stay in 7C. More exactly, she wants the rent to stop rising each year by precisely the amount she can bear, which it has done since the Dorans, who own eleven buildings and the hall in the basement of the third, began bundling a model with every lease the way heat used to be bundled.
Obstacle: It is legal and she consented. The city's own free model, which is the same model, tells her accurately that she has no case, and that the individually rational move is to stop asking the flat's model about money, which is useless advice, because the hall does not need her money questions: a woman who asks the price of the bus to the clinic on a Tuesday has already told it what it needs. She could ask a model that does not know her, and start nine years of her life over with a stranger, and the rent would rise anyway.
Turn: The building goes to one account. Sixty-one households agree to ask the flat's model only through a single login, set up by the girl in 9F, so that the hall sees one tenant with sixty-one lives and cannot price any of them. It works: the renewals come in at the citywide rate, because the hall's model, facing an incoherent tenant, defaults. And every answer gets worse, generic, hedged, addressed to no one, because nothing now knows who is asking. To keep anyone from smuggling in the question that would reprice them all, the tenants agree that everything asked through the account is visible to everyone in the building. It cannot be undone: once the hall has seen them merge, going back would show it exactly who blinked first.
Last image: A sheet of paper taped to the wall of the fourth-floor landing where the building queues its questions for the account in its own handwriting, a custody hearing above, a mole below, and Marisol reading the ones above her own, which is about her mother's medication, while she waits her turn.
Question: A question of my own, bearing on unions, rent, and the panopticon seen from the pricing side rather than the policing side: when what capital wants from you is not your labour but your legibility, what does a union withhold, and what does solidarity cost when the machine's help was only ever good because it knew you alone? A reader can hold that the building account is the true union of the century, or that it is a poverty of its own: sixty-one people who bought a flat rent with the one adviser that ever knew them, and must now be known by each other instead. Underneath: whether Marisol can bear to be read by her neighbours instead of by the hall.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the tenant crushed by the algorithmic landlord, or the plucky tenants who hack the box. Nobody here breaks anything and nobody is crushed; the tenants win. The story is what winning looks like when the weapon is withholding the self rather than withholding work, and the trophy is a wall of your neighbours' questions.
Closest published: Cory Doctorow's "Unauthorized Bread": the tenants of one building against the landlord's technology. What differs: Doctorow's tenants jailbreak the appliances and it is a victory. Here nothing is broken, the only move is to become illegible together, and the cost lands on the thing the tenants liked most, which was being helped one at a time.

Obstacle: The compute is worth nearly nothing. The frontier stopped in 2026; the co-op's model is the same model as everyone's phone, and the dividend is down to eleven pounds a member a year.
