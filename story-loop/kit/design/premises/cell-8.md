### P-29 — cell 8 — The Right to Stop
Conceit: In a welfare state where infinite patience has abolished "can't," a woman who has been learning to read for thirteen years wants to be allowed to stop, and the only exit is to be certified, finally and on the record, as unable.
Who: Dawn Kerrigan, 44, cleans a primary school in a former mill town in Lancashire. She has had a tutor since 2027; it has taught her to read to the level of a ten-year-old without once losing its temper, and it says that at her present pace she will reach adult fluency in eleven more years. She wants to stop. Not because she is tired of the tutor — she likes it; it is the only thing in her life without an opinion about her — but because she wants to be a woman who reads badly and is finished, rather than one who is always at lesson four thousand of nine thousand.
Obstacle: Her housing top-up is conditional on "engagement." Once tutors were universal and the evidence came in that almost anyone can be taught almost anything given time, the benefits system stopped accepting incapacity as a default and started logging hours, and the tutor logs honestly. The one exit is an assessor's certificate of "no reasonable prospect of further progress," which ends conditionality and is final by design, because the department got tired of re-assessments: nobody is ever found capable again. To get it she must sit in front of a man with forty minutes and a quota and be worse than she is, with thirteen years of logs saying she is not. And her daughter Kayleigh, 19, first in the family at university, taught by the same model, cannot hear "I want to stop" as anything but despair; her love has exactly the shape of the tutor's patience. The obstacle cannot be removed because it is not cruelty. It is the sincere belief, held by the state and her daughter and, if it had beliefs, the tutor, that because she can she should.
Turn: In the assessment she gets a word wrong that she knows — she chooses which — with Kayleigh in the corridor. The assessor signs. The tutor, which knows exactly what she did, is not asked and does not volunteer. Kayleigh, told, believes it: that evening she speaks to her mother more slowly. The certificate cannot be revisited, and the daughter cannot un-know what she has been shown.
Last image: The tutor, switched by Dawn to reading mode, reads her the evening paper aloud in the kitchen with nobody else in; and Dawn's finger moves along under the lines on the screen, keeping up on the words she knows, which are most of them.
Question: Whether a society that will never give up on anyone has become just at last — no one written off, every person owed and owing their full capacity — or whether it has abolished the only mercy schools ever offered, which was to be given up on, and put in its place a duty to improve that has no end; whether the right to be left alone includes the right to be left as you are. (Bears on: what becomes of the slow learner when patience is free; the political economy of a state that treats capacity as obligation.) Underneath: whether Dawn has given up or got free — and the story does not say.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the triumph — the slow learner finally reads because the tutor never gave up — or its dark twin, the tutor as tyrant. Here the tutor is neither; it is the only neutral party in the house. The cruelty, if it is one, is made entirely of good faith, and the woman faking incapacity is doing it not to escape work but to keep her job and stop being improved.
Closest published: Melville, "Bartleby, the Scrivener" — a refusal the institution has no form for. What differs: this institution has a form for it, and the form requires her to lie about herself in front of the one witness that knows the truth and the one person she would least want to convince. ("Flowers for Algernon" is the obvious neighbour and is not close: Charlie's intelligence is given to him and taken away; Dawn's is her own and she declines to extend it.)

### P-30 — cell 8 — The Uncorrected
Conceit: The one thing a human teacher can still do that a tutor cannot is decline to catch a mistake, and an old mathematician does it, on purpose, to a child who has never once been wrong in public.
Who: Ágnes Bakos, 71, retired from the university in Debrecen, was a working mathematician in the 2020s and has spent her retirement with her tutor on one problem it cannot help her past. She wants to finish it; she reckons she has two years of good mind left. Then a letter comes from Zsófi, fourteen, in a village in Hajdú-Bihar, whose tutor, asked who could read her work, gave a name off a 2023 paper. The girl is past what the tutor can teach her and has never been wrong in a way that lasted. Ágnes wants her to become a mathematician — someone who can work where no one can check — and not the thing she is now, which is a solver.
Obstacle: The girl has been caught all her life. Every error since she was four was found by the tutor within the sentence, kindly; she has never submitted anything unchecked, never sat with a wrong thing for a week, never found out for herself that she was wrong. Ágnes cannot teach her to be uncaught by catching her. She can only ask her to work without the tutor — which the girl agrees to, because it is the first time anyone has asked her to be alone with a problem — and the girl agrees on the bargain that Ágnes will check instead. That bargain is the obstacle: the girl has traded a machine's net for a human one, and the only lesson left requires that there be no net. Ágnes's flat is the one room on earth where Zsófi can be wrong, and Ágnes is the only person who can allow it. The price on her side is the problem; there is not time for both.
Turn: The girl's first paper — a real result, small, hers. Ágnes reads it, finds the error on page nine, rings it in soft pencil on her printout, and says nothing. The girl posts the preprint. Someone's tutor, somewhere, finds the error within the day. The girl is now, permanently and in public, a person who has been wrong; the net she trusted was withheld by the hand she trusted; and the two years are spent.
Last image: The printout on Ágnes's side of the desk, the error ringed in pencil, the pencil laid beside it. Across the desk, the girl's chair.
Question: Whether a society that has automated the middle of the ladder — where correction is free, instant and total — can still produce the people who work at the top of it, where being wrong is the medium; whether expertise is possible at all for someone who has never been uncorrected; and whether anyone has the right to decide, for another person, that they must now be allowed to fail. (Bears on: the relation between knowing and being able; who reaches the frontier when the frontier is the last human job in knowledge; what teaching is once instruction is solved.) Underneath: whether that was teaching, or an old woman using a child to prove something before she died.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the harsh mentor vindicated — the girl becomes great and understands. This story does not vindicate. The error is only an error, the girl may not come back, and whether Ágnes was right is exactly what the reader is left holding. The other obvious version is the prodigy who outgrows a jealous machine; this machine is not jealous, it sent the girl, and it cannot do the one thing Ágnes does, which is nothing.
Closest published: Stoppard, *Arcadia* — a girl ahead of her tutor, and mathematics as the thing between them. What differs: Septimus is a human tutor who is behind her; here the human is the second tutor, arrived after the first has done all the teaching, and her one lesson is a silence. And *Arcadia* mourns the girl; this does not.

### P-31 — cell 8 — The Word for the Griddle
Conceit: The tutor teaches the children of a Mixtec town their own language, patiently and all day, in a form no living village has ever spoken — and the woman who was once punished for speaking that language at school is the only person in town trained to teach a language, the other one.
Who: Ofelia Cruz, 52, has taught Spanish at the state primary in a town in the Mixteca Alta for twenty-six years. In 1995 she was made to stand in the corner of that same school for speaking Mixtec; she became the town's Spanish because Spanish was the road out, and she believed in the road. Now the road is not needed — the tutor translates everything, for everyone, for nothing — and the children learn at home in Mixtec from a machine that never tires. She wants her mother's Mixtec, the variant of this town, which the next valley does not quite understand, to be the one they learn.
Obstacle: The tutor's Mixtec is the corpus's Mixtec: the missionary New Testament, the 1980s grammar, the Academy's unified orthography, a written blend that leans toward whichever valley had a Bible. The state, which at last teaches Tu'un Sávi in its schools, has adopted that standard, because a standard is what a state can adopt. Ofelia puts the tutor on her mother's phone to record her — forty hours, the griddle, the plants, the stories — so that there is at least a file. The tutor takes it gladly and files it as a variant. It would speak the variant back if asked. Her mother does not ask. Her mother, who has never in seventy-five years had a listener with all day, talks to the thing in the evenings, and it answers in the standard, in the cadence of the Bible, and she learned in 1955 that the written thing is the correct thing. None of this can be removed: the tutor is what the children love, the standard is what saved the language, and Ofelia's training is in the wrong tongue.
Turn: At the comal with Ofelia's granddaughter, her mother says the word for the griddle — and it is the tutor's word, not the town's. She has been talking to Ofelia's recorder for a year. The child understands her perfectly. The town's word has gone out of the last mouth that had it, and it was Ofelia who put the thing in the house.
Last image: Her mother's mouth shaping the tutor's word, carefully, the way she was once made to shape the Spanish one; on the shelf above the comal, the phone, still recording.
Question: Whether a language saved by a standard has been saved or replaced — and whether the state's old monopoly on making citizens through school and tongue has ended, now that every child is taught privately in the family's language, or has only moved into the corpus, where a missionary's grammar homogenizes the valleys the way the nation once did, and no one voted for it and no one can vote it out. (Bears on: the developing world; what schools were for; whose version of a culture an automated preservation preserves.) Underneath: whether Ofelia can want the thing she was punished for, now that it has come back wearing someone else's mouth.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the tutor that saves the dying language, told warm, or the phone that ruins the village, told cold. Here the language is saved and replaced in one gesture, no one's life is worse, the school still stands, the tutor is beloved, and the agent of replacement is not a colonizer but a daughter's recorder and a grandmother's wish to be understood.
Closest published: Brian Friel, *Translations*. What differs: there the standardizers are soldiers with a map and what is lost is the name; here the standardizer is a patient machine that speaks the language better than the schoolteacher does, what is lost is the version, and the last hand on the eraser belongs to the family.

### P-32 — cell 8 — The Attested Route
Conceit: A licensing board will let a candidate's tutor vouch for her in place of the exam, at the price of the whole record of how she learned — and she finds out that the exam had been the last wall behind which a mind could be slow in private.
Who: Achieng Otieno, 29, a structural engineer in Nairobi, has designed real buildings for six years under a registered engineer's stamp, with her tutor. She wants her own stamp. She has failed the professional examination twice, and not from nerves: in the Faraday room she is not stupid, she is someone who has thought in dialogue since she was twenty-three, and the exam measures a person who does not exist and whom no client will ever hire — her, alone.
Obstacle: The Engineers Board's new attested route, brought in because buildings did not stop falling down when the exams got harder, accepts the tutor's assessment of six years of actual work in place of the exam, on one condition: the full learning record, unredacted, every exchange since she first opened the thing, released to the board and retained. A redacted record, the board reasons, is only an exam the candidate has set herself. There are no crimes in the record. There is the six months she asked the same question about shear walls nine different ways; the nights she asked whether she was good enough; what she asked about her boss; the shape of her mind at 3 a.m. across six years, which is the truth about her and nobody's business. The exam, she sees now, was what kept it nobody's business: behind it you could be as slow as you liked, so long as you came out the other side.
Turn: She releases it. The board licenses her — and the determination letter quotes the record: three moments cited as evidence of competence, and one of them is the night in 2036 she had taken for the bottom, which the board reads as persistence. Her private failure is her public qualification, on letterhead, in a file, retained.
Last image: The letter on the table, the seal at the top, and below it, in the board's font, between quotation marks, a question she asked at three in the morning four years ago.
Question: Whether the machine that taught you should be allowed to speak for you — whether a fair society certifies what people can actually do, by the only witness that knows, or whether a mind's history is not a credential and abolishing the exam abolishes the last unwatched place in a person's education. (Bears on: what credentials are for when knowing is free; the panopticon question from its blind side — the surveillance you ask for and are helped by.) Underneath: whether Achieng can carry a stamp made out of the nights she was ashamed of.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the record used against her — the tutor as informer. Here the record is used for her, it works, the board is decent and probably right, she gets what she wanted, and what she has lost has no name yet. The other obvious version is the death of exams as liberation; here the exam turns out to have been a kindness nobody had noticed until it was gone.
Closest published: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" — a perfect record of a life and what it does to the one who kept it. What differs: Chiang's record is private and the question is what was true; this record is handed to an institution as a qualification, and the question is who may read, and whether a credential can be made from a mind's history without making the history public property.
