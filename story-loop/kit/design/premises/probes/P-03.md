The right shoe was on the floor between her feet with its laces open. She had got the left one on by putting her foot up on the bench, and sat now with the other foot in its sock on the lino, looking at the shoe the way she looked at a patient who had decided not to get up today.

Handover was done. Done at seven, her model to the night model, while she stood at the desk and ate a biscuit.

"Tell me plainly," she said. "Not the way you'd tell a family."

"All right." It talked the way she charted; that was where it had learned. "The disc at L4–L5 is gone. The facets are carrying you. Physio and a brace buy a year if you stop lifting, and you can't stop lifting, because there's nothing on this floor that isn't lifting. I've checked every vacancy in the trust each Monday since March. No charge post, no educator, no case manager. Not anywhere. I'm sorry."

"I know."

"Then it's fusion. L4 to L5, posterior, screws and a cage. Mr. Adeyemi at the Royal does the most of them within an hour of your house, with the lowest revision rate. Five nights in. Fourteen weeks off, on full sick, which you have. After that the level doesn't bend, so it can't hurt, and you can hoist and turn and get people up the bed. I've run it against your pension date and Dan's last year. It fits."

She reached for the shoe. Her hand stopped a foot above it, where it stopped now, and she straightened and breathed out through her nose.

"He has a cancellation on the ninth of October. Dan's half term is the week after, so he can drive you to physio. I've held it since this afternoon, when you lifted Mr. Hollis. I heard you. You can give it back until Friday."

"Keep it."

"It's booked."

She pushed her foot into the shoe with the heel crushed down, the way she told patients not to, and walked out to the car like that.
