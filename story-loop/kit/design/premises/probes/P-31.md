Her mother wet her palms in the bowl and took the ball of masa out of Ita's hands and gave it back, and Ita slapped it between her palms the way she had been shown, too hard, so that the edge tore. Her mother said nothing about the tear. She wiped the comal with the rag she kept in the lime water, and the water hissed and went to nothing, and she told the child to lay it down now, there, on the jiyo.

Ofelia was shelling beans at the table and her hands went on shelling.

Jiyo. A breath at the front of it where the town put a hush. The tutor said it that way, and so did the valley over the ridge, whose Bible the tutor had learned it from. The town said it with the hush you make over a cradle, and her mother had said it that way every morning of Ofelia's life, and last spring into the phone, for the file.

Ita laid the tortilla on the comal where she had been told and pressed the torn edge flat with two fingers and took the fingers away fast and put them in her mouth. She had understood every word. She said it back, jiyo, pleased with herself, and her great-grandmother said it once more for her, slowly, the lips first and then the breath, the way her own mouth had once been made to do the Spanish for the teacher from Tlaxiaco, the way you give a child a word she will need somewhere else.

Ofelia had the other word in her mouth. She kept her lips shut on it, the way she had kept them shut in the corner of the second-grade room in 1995 with the teacher's back to her, and she went on shelling, and the beans went into the pot.

Above the comal, between the salt and the matches, the phone lay face up on the shelf. The line on its screen rose when her mother spoke and lay flat when she stopped, and rose again.
