The counter was too low for the clerk, so he bent to the screen with his forearms flat and cuffs pushed back, and her mother looked at the cuffs. Yu-hsuan knew the look. It found the half-centimetre nobody else could see.

"Lin Hsiu-chin," the clerk read. "Made-for-one ceiling, six hundred thousand, set 2029. You are applying for the permanent lock. Once locked, the ceiling cannot be raised by you or by anyone acting on your behalf, and no gap payment, from a family member or anyone else, can ever be applied to it. Your premium falls by two hundred and fourteen dollars a month. Do you confirm?"

"Yes," her mother said.

"I need the words. 'I confirm.'"

"I confirm."

He typed nothing. He turned the screen so all three could see it and read it again at the same speed, the six hundred thousand, the two hundred and fourteen, the ever. At "family member" his eyes came up, not to her mother, to her, and went down. That was the procedure. Yu-hsuan had read it the night before, her model holding the gap-payment consent open beneath it as it had every morning for nineteen days, with the count: eleven days left in the window. Her mother's thumb went over her forefinger, over the hard place the needle had made, the way it did when a hem was pinned and she was deciding whether to trust it.

"I confirm," her mother said, before he asked.

The printer under the counter gave out a slip. He stamped it and slid it across with the card, and her mother put the card back in the wallet behind the photograph of Yu-hsuan in her graduation gown, and folded the slip in with the receipts. In Yu-hsuan's lap her phone showed the account she had not touched since spring, two million two hundred thousand, the ninth-floor flat with the balcony where the washing machine would go. It was hers. It went on being hers.

"Your left cuff," her mother said to the clerk. "Half a centimetre. Bring it to me."
