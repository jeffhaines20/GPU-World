"Why did you order the demolition?"

The traders' advocate had asked it twice and the judge had let him. On the second bench the men from the market sat with their hands on their knees. One of them sold her onions on Fridays. He had not looked at her all morning and she took it for a kindness.

"I would like to refer to the assessment."

"The witness may refresh her memory," the judge said, writing.

She took the phone from her bag and typed: *exhibit four, the reasons.* Her model had them before she had finished typing. On the screen: *The assessment finds that the eastern sheds stand within the statutory fire lane.*

"I found that the eastern sheds stand within the fire lane," she said.

The onion seller looked up. His shed was one of them. The fire lane was where the trucks unloaded at four in the morning. She had stood in it.

"I found that the drainage channel under the fish section has been built over and cannot be cleared. I found that the roof of the north block is tin on timber and the timber is" — the word was compromised, and she said it — "compromised. I found that the site is required for the second phase of the elevated expressway."

Not a lie in it. She had seen the water stand in the fish section in July, a hand deep, the ice melting into it.

"Slower," the judge said. He was dictating to the peshkar, who typed. "The witness states. That she ordered. The demolition. Because she found."

She waited for him and went on, and the peshkar's screen filled with a paragraph in which she was the subject of every sentence.

On the front bench the Federation's lawyer wrote nothing down. He had told her in the corridor not to bother reading it beforehand.

The judge asked whether those were her reasons.

"Those are my reasons."

The peshkar brought the printed deposition to the box and showed her where. She signed it before he had let go of the page.
