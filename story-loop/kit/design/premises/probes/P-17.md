At eleven the return trace lifted and lay down again, the way it did every night when the sleepless opened their models and asked. A quarter of a degree. On an ordinary night, half. The markets had been shut since Friday. The roof sensor read minus thirty-four. Sensor fourteen, in the pumping station at the far end of the loop, read 52.1 and had lost a tenth while she watched it. Her mother's building was the last before the station, forty metres of pipe upstream.

The switch the operator's electrician had fitted in November was a black toggle under a red hinged cover, with a laminated card beside it: LOAD FILL, and the three conditions. Outdoor below minus twenty-five. Far-end return below fifty-five. Hall load under forty percent. She had all three. She lifted the cover with her thumbnail and pushed the toggle down, and it made the small sound a toggle makes. In the log she wrote 23:41, the three numbers, load fill on, and her initials.

The hall took eleven minutes to come up. Through the window the plume thickened and straightened and stood. The duty engineer rang from Dublin to say the fill had taken and wished her a happy Christmas. At half past midnight sensor fourteen crossed fifty-five. At one it read fifty-nine, and the radiator under her mother's window would be too hot to keep a hand on.

From 23:52 the return trace was a ruled line across the screen. She set the scale to a degree, then a tenth, then a hundredth, where the sensor's own noise showed as a fur along the line. There was nothing in the fur. There had never been a night, not the emptiest August Sunday, with nothing in it. At ten past one she opened her own model and asked it what the weather would do, which she knew, and watched the line while it answered. Beyond the wall her question took the place of one that nobody had asked, and the line did not move. Sensor fourteen read fifty-nine. She wrote it down.
