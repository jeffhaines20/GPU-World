### P-51 — The Second Will
Conceit: A widow who inherited the house under a clear, probated will is told by her dead husband's model — fourteen years of his conversations, asked a question he never lived to be asked — that he would have changed it, and must decide whether the dead can go on having intentions.
Who: Miriam Halloran, 66, in the east-end Toronto house she and Paul bought in 2019. Paul died in the spring; the will left her the house outright and left Daniel, his son from the first marriage, a sum of money. Uncontested, probated, done. She wants to stay in the house. She also wants — and would say this first if asked — to do right by Paul, which until August she took to be the same thing.
Obstacle: Daniel's business has failed. He has not asked her for money. He has asked whether she has ever put the question to Paul's model, and she has not; she uses it for what Paul used it for, the garden and the taxes. Nobody in the story thinks the model is Paul. It is a record of him, better than his letters, and it can extrapolate. When she does ask, in text, at the kitchen table, whether Paul, seeing Daniel now, would have changed the will, it says yes, probably, and gives his reasons in his own reasoning, citing things he said about Daniel in 2033 and 2037 that she never heard. The law says the will. The record says the man. She cannot make them the same, and she cannot un-ask.
Turn: She puts the house on the market. Not because Daniel asked and not because anything requires it, but because she has decided that the man's intentions outlived the document — and in deciding that she has made her husband into someone who can still want things, which she cannot take back, because the next question, and the next, will also have answers.
Last image: Miriam in a two-room flat off the Danforth, Paul's small speaker on the windowsill among his geraniums — she has never once let it use his voice — and her hand stopped above it, before she asks it, instead, what to feed them.
Question: Bears on inheritance and the finality of death — whether the reconstructed preferences of the dead should have standing, and, if the reconstruction can update, whether the dead person is finished. A reader can hold that a will was always a poor proxy for what the testator wanted, that we honour the dead by honouring what they would want, and that a better instrument for that is a gift; or that death's finality is what frees the living, and a dead man who keeps having new opinions, however accurate, is a dead hand that never lets go. Underneath: is loyalty to the dead loyalty to what they wrote or to who they were, and who is the one who gets to say?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the griefbot — the widow who talks to her dead husband's simulation and must learn to let go — or the courtroom fight over whether the AI's account of the testator's intent is admissible. Here nobody mistakes the record for the man, the court is never involved because the will is clear, and the trouble is that a woman alone in a kitchen chooses to give the dead an ongoing say, and the story does not settle whether that is fidelity or a haunting she has invited.
Closest published: Black Mirror, "Be Right Back" — a widow and a reconstruction of the dead. There the reconstruction is a companion and the failure is that it is not him; here it is an instrument, is never asked to be him, and is right — and the question is whether a right answer from the dead should move the living. A. S. Byatt's Possession reconstructs the dead's intentions from letters; this is the same work done by something that can also answer back.

### P-40 — The Ceiling

Who: Yu-hsuan, 25, a junior engineer in Tainan, in the year she is required to set her own number. She wants her mother to live, and she has the money: the gap between her mother's ceiling and the cost of the design is almost exactly the deposit she has saved for a flat.
Obstacle: The scheme lets a family member pay the gap, with the patient's consent, which cannot be given on anyone's behalf; and a made-for-one design can only be ordered through the scheme that holds the record it was made from. Her mother, 58, who did alterations out of the front room for thirty years and set her number low in 2029 so the premium would leave more for her daughter, will not sign the consent. Not from despair; she says the number was the point, and that she chose it. The design's order window is thirty days, because the tumour drifts and the molecule with it.
Turn: On day nineteen her mother asks to be driven to the district office and, at the counter, locks her ceiling — the scheme's permanent lock, which rebates a little premium and which no gap payment, from anyone, can ever again pass. It is the thing the poor do for the rebate. She does it so that her daughter will have nothing left to decide. The clerk reads the lock back to her twice, as the procedure requires, and she confirms it twice, and the daughter, sitting beside her, has the deposit still, and will always have it.
Last image: The same counter, the same clerk, who has noticed from the ID that the daughter is due this year and asks if she would like to do hers now, since she is here. The form on the counter screen, the field for the number, and what she has typed in it, which is her mother's number.
Question: Rationing by self-declaration — is a limit you set on your own body while well the most honest form of rationing a society could have, or the way a society gets its poor to consent to dying cheaply and calls it choice? Under it, the question every such scheme has to answer and cannot: whether the well person who chose the number and the sick person who must live under it are the same person, and who decides when they disagree — the earlier self, the later one, or the daughter who loves them both. Underneath, personally: whether honouring your mother means honouring her number or her life.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the mother who dies because the molecule costs more than the family has, or the daughter who sells everything to buy it. Here the money is there and is not the obstacle; the mother's own earlier choice is, and a scheme that honours it, and then the mother's second choice, which is the same choice made harder. Nobody speaks of autonomy or dignity; the scheme is not villainous, it is the least bad answer anyone has found to a real problem, and the story does not find a better one. The daughter neither rebels against the number nor argues with it. She takes it.
Closest published: Tolstoy, "The Death of Ivan Ilyich," for a household arranging itself around a death and the lie everyone tells. Here nobody lies — the number is on the card, the lock is on the record, the day count is on the app — and the trouble is entirely the truth. ### P-34 — The Moderator
Conceit: A Vermont town's first floor meeting with attested proxies — residents' models attending in their place and voting their recorded preferences — sets the forty-one people who came against the nineteen hundred who told their models the truth and stayed home, and the Moderator must rule which of them is the town.

She backed the proxy pilot because turnout had fallen under five percent and the pilot was sold as the way to keep the hall open.
Obstacle: Article 7 is the school budget, voted down six years running by the hall, and this year the school closes if it fails. The town's private mind, spoken to its models over years of evenings, is for the school by a wide margin, because people told their models what they would never say in front of their neighbours. The proxies vote yes, 1,840 to 60. The forty-one in the room vote no, 30 to 11, and rise on a point of order: the statute says a floor vote is a vote of those present, and the pilot was a town article, not state law. The point is good. She cannot count the proxies and keep the hall, because the day the absent count is the day no one has a reason to come; she cannot rule them out and keep the school. Thirty years of neutrality have left her nothing to hide behind.
Turn: She rules the proxies advisory. Article 7 fails 30 to 11. The school is consolidated into the next town in August, and the ruling stands as precedent.
Last image: The next March. The hall is full for the first time since she was a girl — six hundred, standing at the back, coats on — because now, to count, you have to come. Article 1, election of Moderator. She calls the vote and six hundred hands go up in the same second: not the slow, looking-around rise of a real vote but one motion, every phone still warm from the same question. She stands there counting them.
Question: Democracy has always chosen between the will of those present and the will of all, and the choice stayed academic because the will of all could not be known. A model that has listened to a citizen for ten years can represent her considered view more faithfully than her attendance ever did, and for the first time the will of all is cheap and exact. Is presence — bodies, an evening given up, a hand raised in front of neighbours — the substance of self-government or its ceremony; and if the hall can be refilled but not un-minded, what was it she was keeping? (Bears on the cell's question of elections and of being a public; the question of my own is whether representation, made perfect, abolishes the thing it represents.) Underneath: what does she owe the forty-one who came, and what does she owe the nineteen hundred who told the truth to a machine and stayed home?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is AI proxies captured by a bad actor, or proxy democracy as the end of democracy, or as its perfection. Here the proxies are faithful and more generous than the room; the humans present are the obstacle; she kills the proxies to save the room; and the room comes back the next year as a proxy anyway.
Closest published: Asimov's "Franchise," in which Multivac chooses one citizen whose answers stand for the whole electorate. That story shrinks the electorate to a single representative man; this one represents everyone exactly and asks what the hall of bodies is for. Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" for the New England assembly, though its question is another.

### P-29 — The Right to Stop
Conceit: In a welfare state where infinite patience has abolished "can't," a woman who has been learning to read for thirteen years wants to be allowed to stop, and the only exit is to be certified, finally and on the record, as unable.
Who: Dawn Kerrigan, 44, cleans a primary school in a former mill town in Lancashire. She has had a tutor since 2027; it has taught her to read to the level of a ten-year-old without once losing its temper, and it says that at her present pace she will reach adult fluency in eleven more years. She wants to stop. Not because she is tired of the tutor — she likes it; it is the only thing in her life without an opinion about her — but because she wants to be a woman who reads badly and is finished, rather than one who is always at lesson four thousand of nine thousand.
Obstacle: Her housing top-up is conditional on "engagement." Once tutors were universal and the evidence came in that almost anyone can be taught almost anything given time, the benefits system stopped accepting incapacity as a default and started logging hours, and the tutor logs honestly. The one exit is an assessor's certificate of "no reasonable prospect of further progress," which ends conditionality and is final by design, because the department got tired of re-assessments: nobody is ever found capable again. To get it she must sit in front of a man with forty minutes and a quota and be worse than she is, with thirteen years of logs saying she is not. And her daughter Kayleigh, 19, first in the family at university, taught by the same model, cannot hear "I want to stop" as anything but despair; her love has exactly the shape of the tutor's patience. The obstacle cannot be removed because it is not cruelty. It is the sincere belief, held by the state and her daughter and, if it had beliefs, the tutor, that because she can she should.
Turn: In the assessment she gets a word wrong that she knows — she chooses which — with Kayleigh in the corridor. The assessor signs. The tutor, which knows exactly what she did, is not asked and does not volunteer. Kayleigh, told, believes it: that evening she speaks to her mother more slowly. The certificate cannot be revisited, and the daughter cannot un-know what she has been shown.
Last image: The tutor, switched by Dawn to reading mode, reads her the evening paper aloud in the kitchen with nobody else in; and Dawn's finger moves along under the lines on the screen, keeping up on the words she knows, which are most of them.
Question: Whether a society that will never give up on anyone has become just at last — no one written off, every person owed and owing their full capacity — or whether it has abolished the only mercy schools ever offered, which was to be given up on, and put in its place a duty to improve that has no end; whether the right to be left alone includes the right to be left as you are. (Bears on: what becomes of the slow learner when patience is free; the political economy of a state that treats capacity as obligation.) Underneath: whether Dawn has given up or got free — and the story does not say.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the triumph — the slow learner finally reads because the tutor never gave up — or its dark twin, the tutor as tyrant. Here the tutor is neither; it is the only neutral party in the house. The cruelty, if it is one, is made entirely of good faith, and the woman faking incapacity is doing it not to escape work but to keep her job and stop being improved.
Closest published: Melville, "Bartleby, the Scrivener" — a refusal the institution has no form for. What differs: this institution has a form for it, and the form requires her to lie about herself in front of the one witness that knows the truth and the one person she would least want to convince. ("Flowers for Algernon" is the obvious neighbour and is not close: Charlie's intelligence is given to him and taken away; Dawn's is her own and she declines to extend it.)

The girl is past what the tutor can teach her and has never been wrong in a way that lasted. Ágnes wants her to become a mathematician — someone who can work where no one can check — and not the thing she is now, which is a solver.
Obstacle: The girl has been caught all her life. Every error since she was four was found by the tutor within the sentence, kindly; she has never submitted anything unchecked, never sat with a wrong thing for a week, never found out for herself that she was wrong. Ágnes cannot teach her to be uncaught by catching her. She can only ask her to work without the tutor — which the girl agrees to, because it is the first time anyone has asked her to be alone with a problem — and the girl agrees on the bargain that Ágnes will check instead. That bargain is the obstacle: the girl has traded a machine's net for a human one, and the only lesson left requires that there be no net. Ágnes's flat is the one room on earth where Zsófi can be wrong, and Ágnes is the only person who can allow it. The price on her side is the problem; there is not time for both.
Turn: The girl's first paper — a real result, small, hers. Ágnes reads it, finds the error on page nine, rings it in soft pencil on her printout, and says nothing. The girl posts the preprint. Someone's tutor, somewhere, finds the error within the day. The girl is now, permanently and in public, a person who has been wrong; the net she trusted was withheld by the hand she trusted; and the two years are spent.

Question: Whether a society that has automated the middle of the ladder — where correction is free, instant and total — can still produce the people who work at the top of it, where being wrong is the medium; whether expertise is possible at all for someone who has never been uncorrected; and whether anyone has the right to decide, for another person, that they must now be allowed to fail. Underneath: whether that was teaching, or an old woman using a child to prove something before she died.

The error is only an error, the girl may not come back, and whether Ágnes was right is exactly what the reader is left holding. The other obvious version is the prodigy who outgrows a jealous machine; this machine is not jealous, it sent the girl, and it cannot do the one thing Ágnes does, which is nothing.

What differs: Septimus is a human tutor who is behind her; here the human is the second tutor, arrived after the first has done all the teaching, and her one lesson is a silence. And *Arcadia* mourns the girl; this does not.

### P-52 — The Hand Count
Conceit: In a county where every voter has asked the same mind who to vote for, a candidate forecast to lose is given by that same mind the one change of position that would make her the advised choice, and must decide whether to be elected by computation or defeated in person.

She wants the seat, and she wants the seat for one thing: the diversion dam at Miller's Bend out, and the run of fish her grandfather saw back in the river before she is too old to stand in it.
Obstacle: Every voter in the county who cares to has asked the same mind who, given what they care about, they should vote for. The mind is honest and it is the same mind, so its answers add up to a forecast anyone's model can give: she loses by 340 out of about nine thousand, with a small margin because the only unadvised voters are the ones who never asked. Her model tells her, because she asks, that knocking on doors will move roughly nothing — no one is uninformed, and no one's priorities are waiting to be set. She knocks anyway. People are kind. They already know why they are voting the way they are, and it is the reason her own model gave for their side. The obstacle is not opposition; it is that there is no one left to persuade.
Turn: In a low week she asks her model what she would have to say to win, and it tells her — keep the dam, fund a fish passage, commission a phased study — and names the two hundred or so voters whose stated priorities she would then match. She says it. She wins by sixty. It cannot be undone: she is the commissioner, elected on a position she does not hold, by voters who never had to meet her — and the one man who did meet her, who told her on his porch that he would vote for her because she came and he had never asked the thing, voted for the woman who was going to take the dam out.
Last image: Election night in the high-school gym, the hand count going on at the long tables because the county charter still requires it, everyone in the room already knowing the number, and Dee across the floor watching the old man in the Carhartt jacket who is watching the count with real interest, because he is the only one who does not.
Question: Bears on what a vote is and what persuasion is for — whether an election in which every voter is honestly advised by the same mind is the completion of democracy or its end. A reader can hold that it is a census of interests with no demagogue, no ignorance and no spin, that persuasion was mostly manipulation and good riddance, and that a candidate who moves her position to meet the electorate is representation working as designed; or that a vote is an act of choosing a person and not a query, that the one unadvised vote in the county was the only real one, and that a commissioner elected by a mind she talked into electing her has been elected by nobody. Underneath: what does Dee owe the man on the porch, and is the dam still in her?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI that rigs the election, or the AI that predicts it and the human spirit that beats the prediction with one heroic upset. Here nothing is rigged, the forecast is honest and correct, and the human who beats it does so by giving the mind what it needs; the story is about what winning costs when losing was the only outcome that would have been hers.
Closest published: Isaac Asimov, "Franchise" — Multivac knows the electorate so well that one man's answers stand in for the whole vote. There the machine picks a single citizen to make the election real; here every citizen is advised and the single unadvised voter is what is left over, and it is the candidate, not the voter, who has to live with the result.
