### P-41 — cell 11 — The Count of 1954
Conceit: In a canal town whose water has been divided since 1954 by a count everyone knows is false, the model gives every household the same true number in private, and the water-clerk who has kept the ledger must decide whether to set the sluice by it.
Who: Sadiq, sixty, water-clerk of a canal town in a dry country, the third of his family to hold the ledger. He wants to hand the office to his son and to die without a water war. The office, his quarter's majority on the council and the shrine's share all rest on a count made by a colonial officer in 1954: lower quarter 1,000 households, upper 1,100. The lower quarter has emptied since; the upper, on the road, has trebled with people down from the hills. The ledger has never been re-counted, because to re-count is to lose.
Obstacle: Nobody has petitioned; nobody needs to. For fourteen years every household in both quarters has asked the model, in private, how many of us are there, and it has answered each alike — from rooftops in the satellite pictures, from the school rolls, from what they have all told it: about 3,100 households up the canal, about 900 down. Before, everyone suspected. Now everyone knows that everyone asked. A suspicion can be lived beside; common knowledge cannot, and nobody made it. The model will not lie and cannot be un-asked. Asked what the law says, it says the shares are custom — whatever the town agrees — and the ministry has never heard of the canal. Sadiq cannot shift the shares a little, because a small change is now a statement about the size of the lie; and he cannot hold the old number, because his son has asked the model what happens if he does, and told him the answer at dinner.
Turn: The spring division. The upper quarter's young men stand at the sluice and ask him nothing. Sadiq has cut a new notch in the board that morning, his son holding the lamp, at a place the board has never been set. He sets it there and says nothing. The water goes where it has never gone; the lower orchards take less than they have ever taken; the ledger of 1954 is dead within the hour, and with it the office, which goes with the majority. His son will not be water-clerk.
Last image: The sluice board at the new notch — raw wood inside the cut, the old notches dark with decades of hands — and the water already through.
Question: Does a community have a right to its load-bearing fictions — a right not to count itself — or is every such fiction a settled majority's disenfranchisement with the word "peace" on it? And is a technology that answers everyone in private, identically, more corrosive to such fictions than a state that counts in public, because it turns suspicion into common knowledge without anyone deciding to? Bears on the panopticon question, inverted: no one is watched; everyone is answered. Underneath: whether a decent man ends a lie by saying it or by doing it, and whether he owed his son the lie.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI census that exposes a minority to the state, or the liberal fable in which the truth gives the majority its due. Here no one counts anyone and no one is exposed; the model tells each person only what they asked; the fiction dies not of revelation but of everyone knowing that everyone knows; and the man who ends it is the one it protected. The story does not say he was right.
Closest published: Achebe, Arrow of God — Ezeulu, keeper of the calendar, whose authority over the yam harvest breaks against a rival power. Differs: there is no colonizer and no refusal; Sadiq yields; and what he breaks against is not a power but a number everyone already had.

### P-42 — cell 11 — The Clean Book
Conceit: A moneylender's daughter inherits her father's ledger, runs it by the model's fair advice, and empties it in a year — and learns that the debts were what tied the town to her door.
Who: Kesar, thirty-four, back from the city to a market town in western India when her father's heart went. What she wants is the house he had: the bench outside where people waited, the standing, the being the one who is asked. Her father was the town's sahukar for forty years; her brother sends money from Dubai and has no intention of coming home. She means to be a better sahukar than her father — fairer, cleaner, a woman of the new kind — and she has the model to keep the book.
Obstacle: The book ran on a gap. Her father lent at his rate, collected at his discretion, and each Diwali, after the new ledger was blessed, tore one page from the old one in front of the street — a forgiveness that was also a display, and that bound the forgiven to the house for a generation. The model closes the gap from both sides at once. It keeps her book more forgivingly than he did, in total, and does it precisely, privately, by message, with no page to tear. And every borrower carries the same mind, which tells each of them what a fair settlement is: the number she sees. She cannot be unfair — not because she would be caught, but because there is no longer a number that isn't known. She cannot be her father either, because his discretion was a kind of secret, and there are no secrets about sums. Her brother, whose remittances come home through the same model, says: clear it, take the capital, put it in the cold store on the highway — it is what Papa would have done with the tool.
Turn: One morning in the monsoon she sends forty-one settlement offers, drafted by the model, each fair to the rupee; the same mind in forty-one pockets advises acceptance. By Diwali the book is clean. No one is in her debt, and no one is bound to her house.
Last image: Diwali night: the new account book, marked with vermilion and blank, on the shelf where her father's stood; Kesar in the lit street with a plate of sweets, greeted the way anyone is greeted.
Question: Is debt a bond or a burden — is a town's web of owing what makes it a community, or what keeps its poor in their place — and is a fairness that closes every account a liberation or a dissolution? Bears on what perfect, cheap, impartial credit does to societies whose cohesion ran on partial, expensive, personal credit: a reader can hold that the sahukar's forgiveness was domination and good riddance, or that a ledger is not the only thing a town keeps its accounts in. Underneath: what she is when no one owes her, and whether her father was loved or obeyed.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the lending app that ruins the moneylender, or the app that becomes the new moneylender and is worse. Here the model is on everyone's side, including hers; it does not compete with her, it makes her fair; and what fairness empties is not her purse — she is richer — but the bench.
Closest published: Premchand, Godaan — Hori's debt, told from beneath. Differs: told from the lender's side, in a town where the model has made the lender honest; the debt is not the crusher but the tie; and the ending is settlement, not ruin.

### P-43 — cell 11 — The Talker's Language
Conceit: A four-hundred-speaker valley language survives because one garrulous old man spent his last six years teaching it to the model, and now his daughter-in-law's child is learning it from him — dialect, grudges and all.
Who: Dolma, thirty-five, runs the tea shop on the road in a highland valley whose language is spoken by about four hundred people and by no clinic, school or bus. What she wants is for her daughter, Yangchen, six, to have the language — not the school's, not the road's, hers. Dolma married in from the next valley, three hours' walk: same language, a different word for fifty things, and on each side a word for the people over there. Her father-in-law, Tashi, dead two years, was the valley's talker — its self-appointed historian, its bore, the man who knew what everyone's grandfather had done and said so. He spent his last six years telling the model everything, every day, correcting its grammar, singing to it, and giving it his opinion of Dolma's village.
Obstacle: The model's version of the language is mostly him — the linguists' recordings were thin and the other speakers were quiet — and the model's version is now the language. The district's mother-tongue hour uses it because it is the only version written down; the university has it; the school has it. Her own valley's words are in it as "the other way of saying," and marked, faintly, with his view of that. The other speakers are old, few and, increasingly, also talking to the model. The only teacher with the hours is the model, and Yangchen prefers it — it is patient and it is always there — while Dolma has evenings and is tired. She cannot take the old man out of the language; he is what it is made of. She could refuse the model and teach the child herself, in the hours she does not have, in a dialect the school will mark wrong.
Turn: At table, Yangchen uses Tashi's word for her mother's village — a word that means, roughly, "the ones who talk wrong" — not in malice; in her language that is simply the place's name. To correct her, Dolma has to use the model's word to be understood by her own daughter. It cannot be untaught. The child's first language is his.
Last image: Evening. Yangchen on the floor with the device, telling it about her day in the language, and it answering. Dolma in the doorway with the tea things, stopped.
Question: Is a language its speakers or its structure — can a heritage be kept by a keeper that is not a member of the community, and if it can, whose is it? Bears on the politics of the standard: every written language was once one dialect that won, and here the winner was whoever talked most into the record, and the state, arriving after the model, canonized it because it was the only one on paper. A reader can hold that a language alive in one man's version beats a language dead, or that what the model keeps is a recording of a bore and not a people's tongue. Underneath: whether she can give her daughter a thing she can only give in the version of a man she disliked.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the app that saves the dying language, or the children who drop it for the model's English, or the grief-bot that speaks in a dead man's voice. Here the language lives and that is the problem; no one grieves Tashi, least of all Dolma; and the model is not him — it is the language, which is now him.
Closest published: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling," the Jijingi strand — a recording technology enters an oral community and changes what its words are. Differs: Chiang's question is truth and memory; this one is whose version becomes the language when the keeper is not a speaker and the archive was filled by whoever had the most to say.

### P-44 — cell 11 — Third on the List
Conceit: A community health worker in a village that has always rationed care by who came first must submit her cases to the district's triage ranking, which puts her own cousin's child at the top and is right.
Who: Meseret, forty, ten years the health worker for a village a day's road from the district hospital. She wants to send the neighbor's son in the hospital's one pickup this month, because he came first, and for ten years whoever came first has gone. What she has is the bench outside her house and the referral slips; what she decides is who rides. The village has called that fair because it was luck.
Obstacle: Since 2038 the hospital takes only ranked referrals: she submits her cases, the district's triage model orders them by expected benefit, the pickup comes for the first name, and the ranking is shown to any family that asks. This month there are three cases and one seat. The model puts her cousin's daughter first — the child has the thing that gets worse fast — and the neighbor's son third, because he will be fine. It is right; she can see that it is right. There is an override for clinical judgment, which requires a written reason, and "it will look bad" is not one. If she overrides, the sicker child stays and the district sees her send the well one; if she does not, the village sees her cousin's child go first with her own name under it as submitting worker. The model cannot be removed — the pickup comes for the top name only — and it cannot be argued with, because it is not wrong.
Turn: She submits without overriding. The pickup takes her cousin's child. The neighbor takes his son to the district by bus with the money he had saved for the roof, and the boy is treated and is fine — which proves nothing, since he would have been. What cannot change back is not the seat. It is that the village has seen a choice and, having seen one, now reads her ten years of luck as ten years of choosing.
Last image: The hospital pickup coming back up the track with the dust behind it and her cousin's child in the front seat; every doorway in the village with someone standing in it, the neighbor's too.
Question: Is explicit, optimal rationing more just than the implicit rationing of chance and acquaintance — even where making the choice visible destroys the legitimacy the old way borrowed from luck? Bears on the healthcare question where scarcity is the ordinary condition: whether correctness can substitute for procedure, and whether a place that has never had enough is better served by a rule it can see or by a rule it can pretend is nobody's. A reader can hold that the sickest child should go and nothing else matters, or that a village needs its rationing to be no one's fault. Underneath: whether she can be fair when being fair looks exactly like its opposite, and whether she should have been wrong on purpose.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI doctor that is wrong, or right and hated, or right and useless for want of the drug, or the drone that brings it. Here the model is right and not hated and the drug is not the point; what is resented is that there was a choice at all, that it was visible, and that the visible one looks like the invisible ones used to; and the child who was third is fine.
Closest published: Godwin, "The Cold Equations" — the computation is right and lethal and the story sides with it. Differs: here the computation is right and no one dies; what it costs is a health worker's standing and a village's ability not to know that it chooses. (Jackson's "The Lottery" is the inverse: chance made ritual and horror; this is what goes when chance is replaced by reasons.)
