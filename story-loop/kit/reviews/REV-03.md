Inside most calls the tool also ran its own reworded follow-up queries, which I did not choose. I added one page fetch to check a quotation. Each query combined one outline's distinctive elements. For O-1: a father's yearly notebooks of predictions with confidence figures, read by his son before leaving; and a grown child using an AI to audit a parent who never consulted it. For O-2: a midwife overruling an AI at a home birth, with the patient's own AI writing the complaint; and Gawande's "The Learning Curve," to check that comparator. For O-3: a pharmacist who never went against the AI and kept notebooks of what he would have done, graded by his daughter; and "The Whispering Earring," to check it. For O-4: a daughter rerunning her childhood through an AI and getting a different father each time, with earpieces kept in a bowl; and the "grew round it" tree-round-fence-wire image with an overheard father and a shoplifting brother. For O-5: "herring's backbone" with the Deemster's oath and Hall Caine; and a judge departing from an AI recommendation in a custody-relocation case under a law making concordance mandatory. ## Reading

**O-1 Right, Late.** (1) Ted Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" (2013). Same: a father's account of raising his child is checked against a record, and the record goes against him. Different: here two records take sides (the model's audit and Wade's own ledger), the question is his right to learn this way rather than his memory, and the deciding fact lies inside the son. (2) Atul Gawande, "The Learning Curve" (*The New Yorker*, 2002; nonfiction). Same: skill is bought by practising on people who never agreed to it, and the early cases pay for the later ones; that is exactly the "learning curve" Owen sees. Different: Gawande's subjects are strangers in a teaching hospital; Wade's is his own son. (3) Andre Dubus, "A Father's Story" (1983). Same: a father's bet on his own child is paid for by another family's son, as in the Tolliver beat ("Cost: not mine"). Different: Dubus's father hides a death and argues it out with God; here the stranger's cost is one beat, not the story. (4) *The Guilty* (*Den skyldige*, Gustav Möller, 2018), setting only. Same: an emergency dispatcher trusts a hunch over the script. Different: Wade keeps the script at work, bets only at home, and his bets mostly come in. The ledger habit (bets with a stated confidence, scored later) is forecasting practice, known from Tetlock and Gardner's *Superforecasting* (2015, nonfiction). I know no fiction built on it, and it is the element in this outline I can place least. Verdict: in conversation with Chiang, knowingly, since the outline names him; otherwise familiar furniture, new house. Nothing I know makes it derivative of a published work. Its problem is O-3.

**O-2 Working Height.** (1) Gawande, "The Learning Curve" (2002). The conceit is Gawande's argument moved to 2040: a novice can learn only on real patients who never agreed to be the lesson. What differs: Gawande's residents learn to do more; Noor learns to do less, against a machine that is right, and the patient reads the bill. (2) Chris Bohjalian, *Midwives* (1997). Same: a home-birth midwife makes a call nobody can check, and the record of it becomes a formal accusation (in Bohjalian, a manslaughter trial, told years later by the midwife's daughter, by then a doctor). Different: Bohjalian's mother dies and the book is a trial; here mother and baby are well, the departure is toward caution, and the harm is a caesarean that was "probably avoidable, about three in five". (3) Antonioni's *Blow-Up* (1966) and Coppola's *The Conversation* (1974), for the passage only. Same: a trained eye or ear goes back over a recording until what it was sure of drains away. Different: here the recording is handed to the person who paid, and "Again" makes the doubt hers. (4) Chiang's "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling", the outline's own choice, is a weak fit: no birth, no apprentice, no stranger, only a fair record that settles nothing. The alarm that cannot say why ("Something's wrong. I don't know what.") is standard furniture: the fire commander in Gary Klein's *Sources of Power* (1998), retold in Gladwell's *Blink* (2005). Verdict: familiar furniture, new house (MINOR at most). I have not met this arrangement: a departure toward caution, a training simulator written by the counsel itself, and the patient ending up re-hearing the evidence. The "Closest published" line names the wrong neighbour.

**O-3 Undispensed.** (1) Kazuo Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day* (1989). Same: a man who handed his judgment to an authority he trusted asks, late, whether that fidelity was humility or abdication (Stevens says he cannot even claim he made his own mistakes). It is told in a restrained first person that the passage shares ("I waited. I have become very good at waiting."). Different: Stevens's master was wrong and the life he gave up was a love; Magnus's counsel was right, and the life he gave up was written down nightly and is graded by the person it would have happened to. (2) Scott Alexander, "The Whispering Earring" (2012, a blog fable). Same: an adviser that is never wrong, a wearer who lives an exemplary life by it, and the question whether anyone is left inside, which is Magnus's "whether, all along, someone was there". Different: the Earring answers no; the green books are a written shadow-self that answers yes, and Nell's split grade complicates the yes. (3) Henry James, "The Beast in the Jungle" (1903), the outline's own choice. Same: a life defined by what did not happen, seen too late. Different: here the unlived life was written down as it went, and its subject marks it. (4) Tolstoy, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* (1886), which the outline names itself. Same: a correct life audited at its end. Different: no one is dying, and the verdict splits instead of condemning. (5) Neal Stephenson, *The Diamond Age* (1995), ranked lower, but a genre reader will think of it: a girl called Nell raised by a machine teacher speaking in a human voice, and the question whether a real person was behind it. Elizabeth Strout's "Pharmacy" (the first story in *Olive Kitteridge*, 2008) has a decent small-town coastal pharmacist with an unlived inner life; that is a character echo only. Verdict: in conversation with James and Tolstoy, knowingly. But the neighbour an editor would reach for first, *Remains*, is not named. So: familiar furniture, new house (MINOR). Notebooks graded by the person they are about are new to me in published fiction, but they are also O-1's device.

**O-4 Eleven Fathers.** (1) Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" (2013), the outline's own choice. Same: a child's grievance against a father is put to a machine. Different: this audit is counterfactual, run from the child's side, unstable from run to run, and never delivered. (2) Chiang, "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom" (2019). Same: consulting machine-given alternatives to one's own life, nightly envy of the better version ("reads a better childhood than hers"), and finding that the alternatives settle nothing. Different: Chiang's prisms open onto real branches; Tess's eleven fathers are a model's guesses, and the subject is a father's apprenticeship, not free will. (3) Gawande, "The Learning Curve" (2002). "The curve of his apprenticeship, with her at the bottom and her brother at the top" is Gawande's learning curve with the eldest child as the early cases; the difference is that his is nonfiction about surgeons. (4) Alexander, "The Whispering Earring" (2012). The bowl by the door is a household refusing the always-right whisperer, and the outline's "Nor is it atrophy" answers the Earring's premise directly. Verdict: in conversation with Chiang, knowingly; otherwise familiar furniture, new house (MINOR at most). Three things are new to me: the climax overheard through a wall, the list never read to the father, and the debt paid forward to the brother instead of presented. In premise it is O-1's sibling.

**O-5 The Herring's Backbone.** (1) Ian McEwan, *The Children Act* (2014). Same: a woman judge in a family case meets the boy in person before ruling, in an unusual hospital visit, and overrides both his parents and his own stated wish; the cost lands on the boy afterwards. Different: there is no machine, and Fiona rules squarely within the law. Aalin departs from a counsel every litigant can run, under a statute that ends departures, and the cost leaves on the Liverpool boat. (2) Hall Caine, *The Deemster* (1887), the first Manx novel. Same: the Deemster as a figure, the herring's backbone as the image of fair justice (ch. 33, fetched: "as fair as the backbone lies down the middle of a herring", said of rough justice), and justice tangled with the Deemster's own family. Different: it is a Victorian melodrama of murder and outlawry, and nothing in O-5's plot comes from it. (3) Philip K. Dick, "The Minority Report" (1956). Same: a single dissent against a system whose predictions bind. Different: Dick's is a thriller of self-preservation; here the dissent is certified and becomes the last. (4) Kurt Vonnegut, "Harrison Bergeron" (1961), the outline's own choice, is weak; it shares only a law that caps the exceptional. Asimov's "The Evitable Conflict" (1950), with its always-right Machines, is about as loose. The Concordance Act's real-world model is the US Federal Sentencing Guidelines, binding until *United States v. Booker* (2005); "departure", the word the whole loop uses, is the Guidelines' own term. Verdict: familiar furniture (judge against algorithm), new house. The Manx oath, the Act read from Tynwald Hill, and a rule-maker who signs rather than enforces make this the least familiar arrangement of the five. MINOR at most, for the unnamed neighbours.

**Between the outlines.** O-1 and O-3 are one story twice. In both, a father keeps a paper record of his judgments about his child, one book a year, written at night. The grown child (19 in O-1, 23 in O-3) spends the last night before leaving by boat for a working life (deckhand; junior doctor) scoring the books at the kitchen table, as "Right"/"Wrong" or as ticks and crosses. The deciding entry is a teenage episode where the father guessed what was happening and stayed away. The child's answer is that the father's coming was the thing dreaded ("Because you'd have come"; "if Dad comes up those stairs into the gallery I will die"). Both close on the kitchen table after the child has gone, with a line left blank and a pen held over it (O-1) or lying across it (O-3). The only real difference is the father's sign: Wade always departed from the model, Magnus never did. That mirror is interesting, but it does not make two stories. O-4 tells the same premise a third time. Its plot departs furthest from O-1, but it keeps O-1's main elements: the father who never asked the counsel, the 19-year-old leaving home, the learning curve with the child's early years at the bottom, a theft as a key entry, and the question "raised, or practised on?" word for word. O-2 and O-5 are one skeleton twice. In both, a practitioner sits at one end of a concordance scale (Noor's 97.1 is the province's lowest; Aalin's 2,914 judgments all agreed with Counsel). A retiring elder who knows from the inside what a departure costs lets one happen (Marga's five women moved for nothing; Juan's son and grandchildren, spent on another Deemster's education). The one departure falls on a stranger and rests on a perception that cannot be put into words. Then a working woman who reads instruments for a living ("the way she would read a gauge"; "the look she must give a monitor at four in the morning") runs her own model's fair review and asks whether the practitioner knows ("Do you know now?"; "Do you know you're right?"). The two passages are that same scene. All five end on a verdict withheld: a pen held over the line; "Again"; a tick crossed through and a last line left blank; "could not tell"; "No". The passages also share one writer's reflexes. Four cut from the hard line to an ambient sound ("The furnace came on"; "The Rayburn ticked"; "The radiator ticked. Downstairs the fridge came on."; "Rain ticked on the roofs of the cars"). Three have a hard line said flatly "the way" a job reads things (a gauge, a potassium, the wind getting up). Two have someone who "did not remember" a small act of the hand (O-1, O-4). Two have someone look "at the window, where" (O-1, O-2). Three of the five name the same Chiang story as their closest neighbour. That choice is wrong for O-2, and O-3 and O-5 miss nearer neighbours (F5–F7).

## Findings

F1: MAJOR — O-1 and O-3 (each other) are one story twice: the father's yearly books scored overnight by a grown child leaving by boat, the teenage night he stayed away, and the blank line under a pen at the kitchen table; the loop should put them to the human as one option rather than two of five, or rebuild one so it shares neither the scoring night nor the ending.

F2: MINOR — O-4 (with O-1 and O-3) is a third telling of the premise in which a grown child audits the father, sharing O-1's refusing father, learning curve, theft entry and "raised, or practised on?" word for word; the loop should tell the human that the five outlines are two situations and, counting O-1 and O-3 once, four stories.

F3: MINOR — O-2 and O-5 (each other) share one skeleton, and their passages are the same scene (the stranger reads out her own model's fair review and asks whether the practitioner knows); if both go forward, the loop should rebuild one confrontation so the two do not end on the same question.

F4: MINOR — O-1 to O-5 (one another) all end on a withheld verdict and share the same prose reflexes (the cut to an ambient sound, "said it the way", "did not remember", "looked at the window, where"); the loop should tell the human that choosing an outline does not choose an ending, and should have the draft's critics look for these reflexes by name.

F5: MINOR — O-3 names James and Tolstoy but not its nearest neighbours, Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* (1989) and, behind its "was anyone there" question, Scott Alexander's "The Whispering Earring" (2012); the loop should add both to its "Closest published" line and keep the first-person voice out of Stevens's cadence.

F6: MINOR — O-5's nearest neighbours are Ian McEwan's *The Children Act* (2014) and Hall Caine's *The Deemster* (1887), not "Harrison Bergeron"; the loop should name them and make sure Aalin's meetings with Fynlo (chambers, then the kipper yard) do not read as a restaging of Fiona's hospital visit.

F7: MINOR — O-2's nearest neighbours are Atul Gawande's "The Learning Curve" (2002), whose argument its conceit transposes, and Chris Bohjalian's *Midwives* (1997), not Chiang's story; the loop should correct the line and, if O-2 goes forward, make the draft answer Gawande rather than restate him.

F8: NIT — O-3's daughter is named Nell in a story about a child raised by machine counsel in a human voice, which will read as a nod to the Nell of Neal Stephenson's *The Diamond Age* (1995); the loop should rename her unless the nod is meant.

F9: NIT — O-2's passage uses the device of Antonioni's *Blow-Up* (1966) and Coppola's *The Conversation* (1974), going back over a recording until the certainty drains out; that is fine, but the loop should expect a reviewer to name it and make sure the new turn (the woman who paid asks to hear it again) carries the scene.

F10: NIT — O-4's eleven reruns and its nightly reading of "a better childhood than hers" sit close to the envy of other selves in Ted Chiang's "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom" (2019); the loop should name it beside "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling".

Nothing in the five passages reads to me as lifted from a published text. The one quotation is O-5's oath ("as indifferently as the herring's backbone doth lie in the midst of the fish"). It is the historical Deemster's oath and is marked as a quotation. Caine used the same image in 1887, so the image is not new to fiction, but the quotation is honest. O-2's heartbeat as "a horse going hard on sand" is a fresher version of the stock galloping-horse comparison, not a borrowing. O-4's "grew round it" is a common image; the search found no single source. O-3's "I have become very good at waiting" is in Stevens's register, but the words are not his.
