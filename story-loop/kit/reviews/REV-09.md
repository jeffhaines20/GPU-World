# REV-09 — depth-critic, M3, draft-01 of story A (2026-10-29)

## The six questions

1. **What is the story actually about?** In one sentence: whether a child is better off given one sentence about what she is, by someone who loves her and may only be speaking his own wish, or three mild and equally likely ones, by something that cannot love her and cannot choose between them, and which of the two she will hand on to the next child. That is not the plot. The plot is a daughter auditing her father through her earpiece in the six weeks before she leaves home, her brother copying her, and her going.

   The story sets the two answers side by side early. The father, at the red light in Mytholmroyd, "told her the one thing he had ever told her about what she was." The bud, night after night, "offered three other reasons a girl of thirteen might go up a moor, different ones each night, mild and complete, each as likely as his ... and she could not tell which of the four was hers."

   The ending chooses between them twice. First: "It might have been true of Kit. She did not give it to him." Then, a page later: "Ask it. It'll give you three."

2. **Is the question real?** Yes. A thoughtful reader can hold either answer, because the text keeps adding evidence to both sides.

   On the side of the one sentence, and the man who says it:
   - the eye Tess got from him ("She had learned to see that from him, on Saturdays");
   - the whole limb that lands "with a stride to spare either side";
   - "Your sister's goes red," which only a man without a bud would notice;
   - the bud grading him differently from run to run on the one entry that matters.

   On the side of the three, and the thing that gives them:
   - the party, where "a reasonable father turned the van round at the sick," a grade she "had never once argued with";
   - the bud reading the curve more wisely than she does ("Any scoring of a learner draws it");
   - "You could still go in," which is the decent advice, and she shushes it;
   - the turn's finding that the father's sentence may have been "his, at the lights": his wish, not the truth about her.

   The text leans one way in its images. The bud lies "very small, grading him" in her palm. The father doesn't check the train because he is looking at his son. Outside the house, the people who wear buds are drawn as empty (see Findings). But the text never decides for the reader. Tess decides for herself: she keeps the sentence back and hands Kit the three. The last line then puts the cost of her choice on Kit's ear, "which was going red." That is a question, not a slogan.

3. **Does the story earn its idea, or announce it?** It earns it.

   It explains the idea in only two places. The first is the long audit paragraph ("Forty-one entries, each an occasion on which her father had decided something about her ... graded against what a reasonable father would have done"). That is setup, and it is told in the word Tess borrowed from the care home. The second is the last clause of the turn: "the wish of a man who had put every bud in the house in a bowl so that his children would have to come to him." That is the one place the story says what something meant. It is earned, because Tess has just done the same thing herself: she left two places empty in the bowl so that her father would have to go up.

   Everywhere else, the strangeness changes what someone does:
   - The grade on the moor entry keeps changing between runs ("the same father at the party every night and a different man in the van at Mytholmroyd"). She cannot get past that, and it is why she stops: "Not for a twelfth run; she knew what that would give her."
   - The party memory shows what the grade gets right and what it cannot hold: "wiped her face with it, not roughly," and "his hand on her back."
   - The red ear turns the idea into something physical. The device leaves a mark, and it is the man who doesn't wear one who reads it. The one doing the audit learns she was the one being watched.
   - At four on Thursday the bud offers two options, "within what's usually advised." She takes a third, and it turns out to be her father's.
   - She takes the bud out so she can hear him with nothing in between. The bud is the only voice in the story that lectures, and she takes it out mid-sentence ("though it may also be worth—").
   - The two buds look the same and can only be told apart by how they behave. Hers speaks without being asked; Kit's waits to be asked. Hence "Ask it."
   - "Mine never mentioned wire" is the client's first doubt about his bud, and "Her father did not answer that," because he hit the wire too.
   - Everyone's bud is the same ("theirs was this one"), so the audit can have no second opinion.

4. **Do the people behave like people?** Yes, including in the places most at risk.
   - Tess's choice at four a.m. could have been the plot's, but it is hers. After six weeks of grading him, she stages a forty-second entry live ("She went back up to see what her father would do"). Two scenes later she catches her own wish in it.
   - Kit's train times are a twelve-year-old answering, for his sister, the question he answered "Stuff" for his father. She "did not ask him what the times the other way were for."
   - The father's broken-off sentence ("You did it because— ... No. I don't. Why did you?") was prepared by the curve: worse early, better late, someone learning.

   The nearest thing to a person doing what the plot needs is the client at Heptonstall. His own "He'll see what's best" explains why he stays silent when overruled. But every other moment he has serves the scene's argument. His plan goes "from his ear to his mouth," and his look "from the wire to his boy and back" only sets up "Mine never mentioned wire." He is there to speak for the bud and then admit it couldn't see the wire.

   Close to the plot's need, but it holds: the father ends an eleven-year rule with one "Right" on Friday, the day after he took Kit's bud from him. The jacket still on, the turned back and the running tap make that "Right" physical.

   Tess at six ("and she let him wait") reads as her taking on the family's silence even while she refuses its sentence. The text records what it costs Kit ("found at five and come to her door and no further"), so I don't count it against the story here.

5. **What does the ending cost?** Real things, and nothing is given back.
   - She gives up the answer. "The question was in her mouth ... and she did not ask it." Then "That was the whole of it, then or after" closes it for good.
   - She loses her father's door. Her own experiment shows her that he goes to Kit's room and not to hers ("He had not come to her door once since August"). On her last morning his eyes are on Kit's ear, not on her.
   - She spends her last night with "nothing in her ear." She wants hers for "the kind of question she had not put to a person since she was thirteen," and she does not go down.
   - She stops looking after Kit. The boots she has brought in since she was fourteen, she "left ... there."
   - The father gives up the bowl.
   - Kit gets the gift and its price: a boy speaking to his father "from it," his ear going red.

6. **Would a reader be different afterwards?** Yes, in a small way, and it is the kind of change the brief asks for: thinking differently about something assumed. A reader leaves with three things they didn't have before.
   - A machine asked to judge your past holds steady on the entries that don't matter. On the one that made you, it gives "a different man in the van" every night.
   - The sentence a parent gave you about yourself may have been his own wish. Tess can only hear that when it rises in her "in her father's shape."
   - The hedging we hold against these machines ("three ... mild and complete, each as likely as his") might be a kinder thing to hand a child than one loving, possibly wrong sentence. The red ear is its price.

   The third is the assumption the story turns over. Two things keep the change small. The ending gives the reader pointers to its reasons rather than the reasons themselves. And the mother whose leaving started the bowl is kept out of reach (see Findings). Neither is big enough to cancel it.

## Findings

F1: MINOR — The ending settles the story's question through three refusals, and each one reaches the reader as a pointer to a reason rather than the reason itself.
- The sentence that carries the turn: "hers, who had left him in the bowl to be found." "Him" has just meant the father ("You wanted him to have to come up"). And nobody was left in the bowl: Kit's bud was the one that was gone.
- "she did not ask it, for the reason she had given Kit no sentence at six." The reader has to rebuild that reason from the scene before, where it was itself only implied.
- "She did not go down. Going down would make Friday hers."

Read with their reasons, these are Tess choosing neither to give nor to receive a sentence about what someone is. Read without them, they are the family's old silence repeating in her ("nothing said"; "That was the whole of it, then or after"). The story is richer for allowing both readings, but the first has to be reachable on a first reading. If it isn't, the ending shrinks to a portrait of people who don't talk, and the choice between one sentence and three gets lost. A reader would notice the strain at the turn and again at the close. This is a finding about whether the idea is earned, not about the idea.

F2: MINOR — Outside the family, the people the reader meets in the world of buds are props, and they tip a balance the rest of the story keeps level.
- The client's plan comes "from his ear to his mouth with nothing of his own added."
- His boy "looked at her and at nothing, listening."
- The child indoors says "all right, then, to nobody in the room."

The contest itself is fair. "In three" is a sound plan, "Whole" is a better one, and the wire, which neither man saw, levels it. But the only bud-users the reader sees for any length of time have no self. The few moments the client has of his own ("He'll see what's best"; "looked from the wire to his boy and back") all go to proving the father right or to making the wire's point. That puts life with the bud, outside the house, at the "simple nightmare" end of the premise. It makes the father's bowl look right by default rather than by argument. I am not objecting to the view that the bud empties people out. I am saying this scene asserts it instead of earning it, because the client is given nothing of his own to lose. A reader would notice that he is a type.

F3: MINOR — The mother is essential to the story and carries no weight in it.
- The bowl "had started the same week" she went to Manchester.
- Kit's Manchester train times are the only other trace of her.
- The bud's one offer to talk about her ("It may also be worth noting that the earliest entries follow a change in the household") gets "'Go on,' she said, and it went on." The narration does not go on with it.
- Tess was eight when her mother left, and the story gives her no memory of her mother.
- The audit Tess built to find out "what the years in this house had cost her" grades only her father. The story never shows that narrowing as her choice.

The turn reads the bowl as the wish of a man who wanted his children to have to come to him. That is the wish of a man who had been left, and the leaving is the one thing the reader is kept from. The silence could be Tess's own, something she has grown round the way the beech grew round the wire. But the text shows it as the narration's silence, not hers: she asks the bud to go on, and it is the telling that cuts away. This is not a request for a mother subplot. The ending rests on what the bowl means: the father's Friday "Right" and the gift to Kit both depend on it. That meaning is stated at the turn while its root is held back. A reader would notice the gap.

F4: NIT — The curve is the one "figure she could read" that the audit produces, and it plots things the audit doesn't contain. The entries are "each an occasion on which her father had decided something about her," yet the curve has "Kit's up in the top right." The bud also explains its shape partly by "a second child being easier than the first." Either the audit includes Kit, or the curve comes from somewhere the story hasn't said. A related small question: an earpiece "drew" a shape that she "looked at" in a dark room, so where is the figure? It's small, but this is where the workings of the idea show.
