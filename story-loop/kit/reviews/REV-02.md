# REV-02 — originality critic, M1 second pass, cards P-25 to P-52 (2026-09-08)

Web search ran. I searched for each card's distinctive elements in combination; no search returned a published story matching any card's core combination, and the useful hits were non-fiction. The searches that changed my view: Asimov's "Profession" (1957) — confirmed premise and thesis ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profession_(novella))); Damon Knight's "I See You" (1976) — confirmed as the canonical "privacy abolished by universal viewing" story ([ISFDB](http://www.isfdb.org/cgi-bin/title.cgi?50386=)); Malka Older's *Infomocracy* (2016) — confirmed the universal Information service advising every voter ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2016/06/08/480477941/infomocracy-is-a-sci-fi-thriller-with-election-year-chills)); data-centre waste heat warming a town swimming pool — confirmed as a real and widely reported scheme, Deep Green at Exmouth and Trafford, 2023–24 ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/uk-data-center-startup-offers-to-heat-britains-swimming-pools-with-waste-heat/)); AI voting proxies — confirmed as a circulating Schneier/Sanders policy proposal, not only fiction ([AI Frontiers](https://ai-frontiers.org/articles/ai-will-be-your-personal-political-proxy)); "human in the loop" as moral crumple zone / accountability sink ([Doctorow, Pluralistic](https://pluralistic.net/2024/10/30/a-neck-in-a-noose/)). Searches that failed to find anything at all, which I report as failures rather than clearances: fiction about a quiet AI-advised bank run; fiction about a dead person's model contradicting a probated will; fiction about AI-certified remorse in restorative justice; fiction about a tax on model queries; fiction about an AI standard displacing a village dialect; fiction about hired crowds photographed for a machine-read archive. My search is US-indexed and English-only, so absence there is weak evidence.

## Reading

Ranked by how likely a well-read editor is to say "this is X": P-30 (Asimov, "Profession") is the closest call in the pass; then P-52 (*Infomocracy*), P-26 (Black Mirror "Arkangel"), P-34 ("Franchise" plus the live proxy-voting proposal), P-29 (*I, Daniel Blake*), P-40 (*Logan's Run*). Nothing in the twenty-eight is derivative in the strong sense; I raise no MAJOR.

**P-25 The Sunset.** Closest: Ishiguro, *The Buried Giant* (2015) — a polity's forgetting as mercy or crime; there the mist is imposed and the couple wants to remember, here it was voted for by the woman who now needs the exception. Then Black Mirror, "The Entire History of You" (2011) for the apparatus, and Damon Knight, "I See You" (1976) for a society that has to legislate what may be seen. Mandated-deletion law is common furniture; the exemption-only-through-the-State hinge, and a victim who would vote for it again, I have not met. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-26 Three Minutes Eleven Seconds.** Closest: Black Mirror, "Arkangel" (2017) — a parent's power to redact what a child's recorded life contains, and the child later going at the redaction; same furniture and same confrontation, opposite direction (there the parent watches and censors perception; here the child owns the witness and the redaction is legally marked). Then Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" (2013), which the card names. The turn — destroy the audio, speak the fact — is the card's own. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-27 The Hour.** Closest: Black Mirror, "The Entire History of You" — the demanded replay and the gap read as guilt is that episode's engine, and it is what everyone will hear first. Then Ken Liu, "The Perfect Match" (2012) and Eggers, *The Circle* (2013) for the record as the price of standing; Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day* (1989), which the card names for the last image. Different: nobody accuses, nothing happened, and the record is the thing that lifted her. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-28 The Roof Fund.** Closest: Damon Knight, "I See You" — the world where anyone may look at anything and society has to decide what to do about it; Knight abolishes privacy, this card re-founds it by bylaw. Then *The Lives of Others* (2006) and Kadare, *The Palace of Dreams* (1981), both named by the card, both state-run where this is a neighbour with a kettle. The right-not-to-be-read move is the freshest idea in the surveillance cell. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-29 The Right to Stop.** Closest: Ken Loach, *I, Daniel Blake* (2016) — the conditionality assessment, the quota, the claimant made to perform incapacity; same scene, with an honest tutor's log where Loach has a points form. Then Melville, "Bartleby" (1853), named by the card, and Stephenson's Primer as the tutor archetype. Different: the tutor is the neutral party and the coercion is entirely good faith. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-30 The Uncorrected.** Closest: Asimov, "Profession" (1957) — a society whose instruction is automated cannot produce its originators, who must be the ones the machine did not finish; that is this card's Question almost verbatim, in a famous story the card does not name. Different: Asimov's is institutional with a reveal, this is two people and a withheld pencil mark, and it refuses vindication. Then Scott Alexander, "The Whispering Earring" (the card is its inverse: harm from never being uncorrected) and Stoppard, *Arcadia* (1993), which the card names. Familiar furniture, new house — but the furniture is a named thesis, and a judge who knows Asimov will hear it.

**P-31 The Word for the Griddle.** Closest: Brian Friel, *Translations* (1980), which the card names and answers well. Then Liu Cixin, "The Village Teacher" (2001) for the schoolmaster's obsolescence, and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's account of being punished for speaking Gikuyu at school, which is what the opening image will summon. Searches found the standard-displaces-dialect worry documented only as real-world commentary, not as fiction. Familiar furniture, new house — but see the duplication with P-43.

**P-32 The Attested Route.** Closest: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact" (named), then *The Circle* — the credential bought with total disclosure. Different: the institution is decent, the record is used for her, and the loss has no name. I know no story that makes the exam the last unwatched room. Unfamiliar to me in its exact form; familiar in its furniture.

**P-33 Nobody Ran.** Closest: Capra, *It's a Wonderful Life* (1946), which the card names and knowingly inverts — no crowd, no standing for the word, and she hands her money the other way. Then Robert Harris, *The Fear Index* (2011), which is the opposite case (a rogue machine, not correct advice). My search for correlated-advice bank-run fiction found nothing. In conversation with *It's a Wonderful Life*, knowingly; not a finding.

**P-34 The Moderator.** Closest: Asimov, "Franchise" (1955), which the card names and correctly distinguishes. Then the Schneier and Sanders AI-proxy-voting proposal, which is circulating widely enough that judges will read the premise as an illustration of it rather than an invention. Jackson, "The Lottery" (1948) for the assembly. The last image — six hundred hands rising in one motion — is the card's own and is the best thing in it. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-35 Five O'Clock.** Closest: Forster, "The Machine Stops" (1909) and Bradbury, "The Murderer" (1953), both named. The bōsai musen setting, the borrowed chime as the only speech available, and the cost being the channel's silence, I have not seen anywhere. Unfamiliar to me; the freshest of the public-sphere cards.

**P-36 Hakot.** Closest: Nathanael West, *The Day of the Locust* (1939), which the card names; then Bacigalupi, "The Gambler" (2008) for a world where the metric decides what counts as speech. Searches confirm hakot is real practice and turned up no fiction using it. Unfamiliar to me.

**P-37 Physician of Record.** Closest: the moral-crumple-zone / accountability-sink discourse (Elish 2016; Doctorow 2024) — the card knows it and refuses its conclusion, which is the point. Then Alex Rivera, *Sleep Dealer* (2008) for offshored liable bodies, and Melville, "Bartleby," which the card names. Different: nothing is exposed, the loop is honest, and the cost lands on a stranger. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-38 Forecasting Off.** Closest: *Gattaca* (1997) — insurance priced by forecast, and a life organised around refusing to be read; then Jackson, "The Lottery," which the card names for shape only. The broodfonds rule that forbids knowing, and a treasurer who breaks it and stays, I do not know from anywhere. Unfamiliar to me.

**P-39 The Quarter Slot.** Closest: Le Guin, "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973), named and knowingly inverted; then N.K. Jemisin, "The Ones Who Stay and Fight" (2018), the best-known answer to it, and Godwin, "The Cold Equations" (1954). Different: the one who would be saved refuses, and the nurse agrees with him. In conversation with Omelas, knowingly; not a finding.

**P-40 The Ceiling.** Closest: Nolan and Johnson, *Logan's Run* (1967) — a number carried on the body that decides when you die, and a bureaucracy that honours it; different in that the citizen sets her own number while well, which is the card's whole invention. Then Tolstoy, "The Death of Ivan Ilyich" (1886), named, and Project Itoh, *Harmony* (2008) for health as public obligation. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-41 The Count of 1954.** Closest: Achebe, *Arrow of God* (1964), named; then Havel, "The Power of the Powerless" (1978) and Andersen, "The Emperor's New Clothes" — the load-bearing lie that dies of common knowledge rather than of revelation. The mechanism (identical private answers, nobody counting anybody) is genuinely new to me in fiction. Familiar furniture, new house; one of the strongest cards.

**P-42 The Clean Book.** Closest: R.K. Narayan, *The Financial Expert* (1952) — the moneylender as the town's social centre and what he is when the money stops; then Premchand, *Godaan* (1936), named, and Graeber's *Debt* (2011) for the thesis that obligation is the tie. Different: told from the lender's side, and fairness is what empties the bench. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-43 The Talker's Language.** Closest: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact" (named); then the real case of a last-speaker corpus recorded from one informant. The idea that the archive belongs to whoever talked most is a good one and I know no story with it. Unfamiliar to me — but it is P-31's premise again.

**P-44 Third on the List.** Closest: Godwin, "The Cold Equations," named and knowingly inverted (right, and nobody dies); then Calabresi and Bobbitt, *Tragic Choices* (1978), which is this card's thesis in a famous book — that societies hide rationing decisions to protect their legitimacy. Jackson, "The Lottery," named. In conversation with "The Cold Equations"; the thesis is borrowed and should be known to be.

**P-45 The Name.** Closest: Melville, "Bartleby," named; then Rivera, *Sleep Dealer* and Marshall Brain, *Manna* for the economics. The invention — a market that prices a proven refusal so highly it makes refusing again impossible — I have not seen, and it is the best economic idea in the pass. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-46 The Building Account.** Closest: Doctorow, "Unauthorized Bread" (2019), named and answered squarely; then Brunton and Nissenbaum, *Obfuscation* (2015) for the tactic of collective illegibility, which is the card's central move under its real-world name. Different: nothing is broken and the cost is the loss of being known. In conversation with Doctorow, knowingly.

**P-47 Pithead Baths.** Closest, and it is not fiction: the Deep Green scheme heating the Exmouth and Trafford leisure-centre pools with data-centre waste heat, reported everywhere in 2023–24. In fiction: Llewellyn, *How Green Was My Valley* (1939), named, and Kim Stanley Robinson's *The Ministry for the Future* (2020) for the commons-and-rent politics. The card's discovery is a real arrangement, not an invention. Familiar furniture, new house — with a caveat.

**P-48 Ask Again.** Closest: Frederik Pohl, "The Midas Plague" (1954) — a state whose solvency requires its citizens' appetite, and the machinery built to keep them consuming; nearer than the Omelas the card names, which fits badly since there is no child in the cellar. Searches found the query levy only as live tax-policy discourse ("token taxes"), not as fiction. Familiar furniture, new house.

**P-49 The Court of Protection.** Closest: Jordan Harrison, *Marjorie Prime* (2014) — dementia and a machine that holds what the person cannot; different in that this machine is a prosthesis for competence, not a memory of the dead, and the drama is a statutory test. Then Keyes, *Flowers for Algernon* (1959) and Clark and Chalmers's Otto, both named. The forty minutes with the tool switched off is the card's own and I know no precedent. Unfamiliar to me.

**P-50 Two Hundred and Twelve Days.** Closest: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact" (named). Then the certified-interior device: Dick's Voigt-Kampff in *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?* (1968), and Wiesenthal's *The Sunflower* (1969) for whether forgiveness can be owed. Different: nothing is forged and the loss is the victim's anger. My search found no fiction on AI-certified remorse. Unfamiliar to me in its exact form.

**P-51 The Second Will.** Closest: Black Mirror, "Be Right Back" (2013) and *Marjorie Prime*, both refused by the card on the right grounds (nobody mistakes it for him). Then Byatt, *Possession* (1990), named. Searches found no fiction where a reconstruction's answer overrides a probated will. Unfamiliar to me; the estate-law framing is the fresh part.

**P-52 The Hand Count.** Closest: Malka Older, *Infomocracy* (2016) — an election in a world where one honest universal information service advises every voter; the card names only "Franchise" and misses the book that owns this world. Different: Older's plot is a rigging thriller, this one has nothing rigged and turns on the candidate. Then Michael Ritchie's *The Candidate* (1972) for winning by becoming what wins. Familiar furniture, new house.

**Lifted-image check.** I found no card whose central image or line appears taken from a specific text. Three near-misses worth stating: P-31's opening (a child made to stand in the corner for speaking her own language at school) is a real documented practice in Mexico and Wales, but the image is so strongly associated with Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's *Decolonising the Mind* that some readers will hear it as his; P-49's "as a man with spectacles has sight" is a stock analogy from the extended-mind literature and from Mental Capacity Act commentary, so it is a borrowed formulation rather than an invented one; P-33's opening situation is deliberately built on the Bailey Building and Loan run, which the card declares. P-35's "Yūyake Koyake" and the 5 p.m. chime, P-36's hakot, P-40's card-borne number and P-42's Diwali ledger blessing are real-world materials used accurately, not fiction borrowed. The strongest individual lines — "the poor now know the name of what they are not getting," "a suspicion can be lived beside; common knowledge cannot," "what it is to have been good at something exactly once," "the only thing in her life without an opinion about her" — I believe to be invented; none returns a source I can find.

## Findings

F1: P-30 — MINOR. Isaac Asimov, "Profession" (*Astounding*, 1957). The card's stated question — whether a society that has automated instruction can still produce the people who work where nobody can check, and whether expertise is possible for someone never uncorrected — is "Profession"'s thesis, and "Profession" is famous enough that a judge who knows the field will name it. The card names Stoppard and does not name Asimov. The dramatic situation genuinely differs (a withheld pencil mark, no institution, no vindication), which is why this is MINOR and not MAJOR; but if the finished story states the argument on the page rather than leaving it in the reader's hands, it becomes the Asimov story with a Hungarian setting.

F2: P-25, P-26, P-27, P-28, P-32, P-50 — MINOR. Black Mirror, "The Entire History of You" (2011), and Damon Knight, "I See You" (*F&SF*, 1976). Six of twenty-eight cards run on one borrowed apparatus: a perfect personal witness whose record can be replayed, withheld, or read by others, with the gap in it functioning as accusation. That episode owns the gap-as-accusation beat and Knight owns the everyone-can-see-everything settlement. The loop's known-nearby list says "Black Mirror" generically; it should name the episode, because "she has a gap in her lifelog and must account for it" is the single most-used move in this pass.

F3: P-26 — MINOR. Black Mirror, "Arkangel" (2017). A parent's power to redact what her child's recorded life contains, and the child going straight at the redaction, is that episode's premise and its climax. The card reverses the direction (the witness belongs to the child by law, and the redaction is marked rather than hidden), which is a real difference, but the parent-redaction-child-confrontation shape is Arkangel's and the card does not name it.

F4: P-52 — MINOR. Malka Older, *Infomocracy* (2016) and the Centenal Cycle. The card's world — every voter honestly advised by the same universal information service, and politics reorganised around what that service will say — is Older's world, and hers is Hugo-finalist and well known to exactly this audience. The card names only Asimov's "Franchise," which is the further neighbour. Different: Older's plot is about rigging, this card's forecast is honest and the pressure lands on the candidate.

F5: P-34 — MINOR. Asimov, "Franchise" (1955), plus the Schneier and Sanders AI-political-proxy proposal now circulating as policy essays. The card handles "Franchise" correctly, but the proxy mechanism itself is a live and widely read proposal rather than an invention, so a judge will read the premise as its illustration. The card's originality lives in the Moderator's ruling and in the last image; those should carry more weight than the mechanism.

F6: P-29 — MINOR. Ken Loach, *I, Daniel Blake* (2016). The benefits assessment with a quota, and the claimant who must perform incapacity to a stranger with a form, is that film's central scene and a familiar one to British readers. What is new is that the honest tutor's log is what she must lie against and that her daughter is the audience; the card should be careful that the assessment scene does not become the Loach scene with an AI in it.

F7: P-40 — MINOR. William F. Nolan and George Clayton Johnson, *Logan's Run* (1967). A number carried on the body that decides when a life ends, honoured by a bureaucracy that reads it back to you, is that book's furniture. The card's invention is that the citizen chooses her own number while well and can lock it; that is the difference to protect. The card names Tolstoy and does not name the SF precedent.

F8: P-47 — MINOR. Not fiction: the Deep Green data-centre waste-heat scheme heating the Exmouth Leisure Centre pool (2023) and its successors, widely reported. The card says "I know no published story with this mechanism," which is true and misleading — the mechanism is a real arrangement many readers will recognise, so Emlyn's discovery reads as reportage rather than a turn. The story needs the surprise to sit somewhere else: in the deed, the lease, and the town's own words leaving the machine.

F9: P-48 — MINOR. Frederik Pohl, "The Midas Plague" (*Galaxy*, 1954). An institution whose solvency requires its citizens' appetite, and the machinery it builds to keep that appetite up, is Pohl's premise; it is a much closer neighbour than the Omelas the card names, which fits badly since the card itself says there is no child in the cellar. Naming Pohl instead would let the card stop leaning on a comparison it has to disown.

F10: P-37 and P-45 — MINOR. Alex Rivera, *Sleep Dealer* (2008), plus the accountability-sink discourse (Elish's "moral crumple zone," 2016; Doctorow, 2024). Both cards use the same premise — the cheapest liable body is in the global south, and the rich world buys the signature rather than the work — and the discourse around it is common property. Each card's escape is its own (P-37's honest loop, P-45's priced refusal); the shared premise should not be presented as a discovery in both.

F11: P-29, P-37, P-45 — MINOR. Melville, "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (1853). Three cards in one pass name Bartleby as their closest published work, and Bartleby is already on the loop's known-nearby list. Whatever the merits of each, a set in which three of twenty-eight premises are described by their own writers as the Bartleby situation has concentrated its risk; at most one should reach the shortlist on that ground.

F12: P-25, P-26, P-27, P-32, P-43, P-50 — MINOR. Ted Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" (2013). Six cards name it as their nearest neighbour or invoke it, and the loop has already ruled it nearby. The distinctions the cards draw are real and mostly well argued, but a shortlist drawn from this pass could easily be six variations on the one story the contest's readers will have in mind.

F13: P-31 and P-43 — MINOR. Not a resemblance to published work but to each other: both are "a patient machine's standard version of a minority language displaces the community's version, and the family's own hand does it" (Mixtec grandmother adopting the tutor's word for the griddle; the child speaking the dead talker's dialect). The last images are near-twins — an older speaker or a child voicing the machine's version while the protagonist watches from the doorway. One of the two should go, or one should change its question.

F14: P-44 — MINOR. Guido Calabresi and Philip Bobbitt, *Tragic Choices* (1978). The card's argument — that societies conceal rationing decisions because visible allocation destroys the legitimacy the invisible kind borrowed from luck — is that book's thesis, and it is well known. Godwin's "The Cold Equations," which the card names, is the further neighbour. The story is fine; the writer should know the thesis is borrowed and not let a character deliver it.

F15: P-41 — MINOR. Václav Havel, "The Power of the Powerless" (1978), and Andersen, "The Emperor's New Clothes." The load-bearing public lie that everyone privately disbelieves, and what happens when disbelief becomes common knowledge, is old political theory and an old tale. What is genuinely new here is the mechanism — nobody counts, nobody is exposed, and the fiction dies of everyone knowing that everyone asked. Keep the mechanism foregrounded and the thesis unspoken, as the card already intends.

## What I did not check

I read and searched only in English, and my search index is US-facing. I did not check: Irish-language or contemporary Irish literary fiction for P-25; Danish and Nordic SF for P-26; Kenyan, Nigerian and Kenyan-diaspora fiction beyond the best-known names for P-27, P-32, P-39 and P-45; Romanian post-Securitate fiction beyond Kadare, Müller and Manea for P-28; Hungarian fiction for P-30; Mexican and Mixtec-language writing, and Latin American SF generally (Bef, Alberto Chimal, Liliana Colanzi), for P-31; Malayalam and other Indian-language fiction for P-33 and P-42, including anything about cooperative banking; Japanese SF beyond translated Itoh and Tawada for P-35, and no Japanese-language search at all; Philippine literature in English or Filipino for P-36, where a hired-crowd story is quite likely to exist and I could not find one; Dutch fiction for P-38; Malawian and southern African fiction for P-39; Taiwanese and Sinophone SF for P-40, which is the card where my ignorance is largest; Persian, Urdu and Arabic fiction about water rights for P-41; Tibetan, Nepali and Himalayan writing for P-43; Amharic and Ethiopian fiction for P-44; Welsh-language fiction for P-47; Swedish fiction for P-48; New Zealand fiction for P-50; Canadian fiction for P-51.

By decade and venue: I did not systematically check the SF magazines before 1970 (*Astounding*, *Galaxy*, *If*, *New Worlds*), where a query tax, a mandated-forgetting statute or a proxy-voting town meeting could easily be sitting under a title I have never heard; nor the 1970s–80s feminist and New Wave anthologies (*Orbit*, *Dangerous Visions*, *Aurora*); nor Interzone, Omni or Analog runs; nor recent issues of Clarkesworld, Lightspeed, Uncanny, Asimov's, Strange Horizons, Reactor and khōréō from 2024 to 2026, which I sampled by search only and where near-future AI-tutor, AI-triage and AI-village stories are being published continuously — I could not rule out a recent close neighbour to P-30, P-39 or P-44 there. I did not read gwern.net's fiction page in full, so I cannot certify that none of these cards brushes a piece of Gwern's own fiction beyond "It Looks Like You're Trying To Take Over The World," which none of them resembles. I did not check Stephenson beyond *The Diamond Age*, *Fall* and *The Ministry*-adjacent politics; *Anathem* and *Termination Shock* I know only in outline. Finally, I judged all twenty-eight from cards, not prose: several of these are MINOR only so long as the execution keeps the differences the cards claim, and a draft that states its thesis rather than dramatizing it can turn F1, F4 or F14 into something worse.
