# REV-04 — depth-critic, M2, the spine of story A, "Eleven Fathers" (2026-09-25)

## Reading

1. **What the story is about.** In my words: a daughter cannot audit the father who formed her, because the judgment she audits with and the memories she feeds the machine are his work. And the one way she has to spare her brother that forming is a forming of its own, done to him without asking. That is not the plot (a week of nights, a confiscated bud, the 9:14). It is the spine's "What she is wrong about" turned into events, and each of its three parts has a beat:
   - She cannot tell whose reason is hers (Beat 1: "She cannot tell whose is hers").
   - The harm and the gift are one thing. The sentence at the lights is both his knowing of her and her making, and Friday gives Kit the shield while taking the growing-round.
   - She is at the bottom of her own curve. Thursday is her first departure from a plan, and it is run on Kit.

   The idea is worth a reader's attention.

2. **Is the question real?** In its design, yes, and in one place better than that. The world's question assumes that those who paid can audit the departer with the same model. The spine makes that audit the thing that will not hold still: "eleven runs, eleven reasonable fathers, and the gap between runs wider than any entry"; "Every second opinion is the same bud in another ear". That goes at the premise of the question instead of answering it with a slogan. The nearest story I know with this shape is Chiang's "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling", where a lifelog settles what a father really said to his daughter. Here the machine cannot settle it, and that is the idea, not a hedge.

   The second question (may Tess let Kit be the case?) is fully open. What she risks on Thursday is exactly the sentence at the lights: "I know why you did it" is how that sentence began. The reader sees what she was willing to let Kit be given. Thursday's plain sentence convicts her, and Friday may acquit her.

   The first question (were Rob's eleven years his right, or vanity?) is decided by weight, though the text never says so:
   - Every entry the beats stage from the eleven years is either for Rob or undecidable by design. The silence at fifteen is set against the bud's score ("It was the silence"), and the must-not list files it among the bud's errors. The lights are undecidable.
   - The curve is the one piece of evidence against him, and the bud discounts it in the same beat ("cannot tell learning from luck, or from a second child being easier").
   - In the present he is the best person in the house. There is the stopped sentence, which the bud grades as good practice, and the ear he notices.
   - The last image gives the bud a train time and gives Rob the ear.

   The spine names the counterweight ("was once plainly wrong (the party at nine)"), but only in the must-not list. The cost a reader does feel, that he has not come to her door since August, is real, but it is distance, not vanity. So a reader has decided "gift" by Wednesday night (F1).

   Part of the cause is in "The question" itself. There, being right at the lights would mean "he beat the bud and owes nothing". That turns "may he take a risk he cannot price, on someone else?" into "did it pay?" (F7). Both are findings about balance and logic, not disagreement with either answer.

3. **Does the story earn its idea, or announce it?** It earns it. The strangeness changes what she does three times:
   - In Beat 1 she stops the rerun at the lights and reads "the distance between last night and tonight". The machine's variance is the finding.
   - In Beat 3, because no rerun can vary a live case, she refuses both her bud's plan and its other option and lets her father find Kit. The story's deciding act is caused by the machine's failure to hold still.
   - In Beat 5, the property that beat her audit becomes what she hands her brother: "Ask it. It'll give you three."

   The client's bud missing the wire is "the same bud in another ear" used, not said. In the passage, "He's asking rather than telling ... generally considered good practice" turns the audit on itself at the moment she stops wanting it. The idea is explained once, fairly, in the bud's own hedging voice (Beat 2: "any scoring of a learner draws that shape"). It risks being announced in two places: the spine's wording of Thursday's plain sentence (F6), and the Who's "where she will be taught when not to", which must stay in the spine.

4. **Do the people behave like people?** Mostly, and in the passage superbly. "I know why you did it ... No. I don't. Why did you?" is a man catching himself in the act that made his daughter. "Your sister's goes red" is a man who has known since August and let it be. Tess's audit is a real person's obsession, and her Thursday choice is cold in the way people are.

   The moment where a character does what the plot needs is Kit at six. "Why did I do it?" is the story's question in the mouth of a boy whose register is "Dunno", and it comes just after he has heard that his sister does it too (F3).

   Two larger places are held up by the must-not list rather than by the people:
   - Tess never asks Rob the thing she has been asking the machine at night, although the ending counts that as a cost (F2).
   - Rob, who after "No. I don't" still said "Give it here", watches her undo it a day and a half later without a word (F4).

   Behind both, Rob's rule has a date and no cause, so he risks being a position rather than a man (F5).

5. **What does the ending cost?** Something real, in three hands, and nothing is cancelled:
   - Rob loses being the only one who knows his boy. His daughter takes that without a word, and the image is his: not checking, looking at the ear.
   - Tess leaves without the bill, having spent it, and is no longer only the one it was done to.
   - Kit has the shield and a reddening ear.

   It is an image with a cost, not a verdict held back. The weakest of the costs is Kit's "growing-round". The only image the story has for growing round is the wire, which is damage found by a chain, so a reader may count his shield as pure gain (F8). The unasked question is a cost only if asking was ever alive on the page (F2).

6. **Would a reader be different afterwards?** Yes, in a small way of the kind the brief asks for ("makes you think differently about something you had assumed"). The assumption overturned is that the machine in the ear of the one who paid could settle the account. A reader leaves holding some sentence a parent once said about them, unsure whether it fits because it was true or because they grew to fit it, and with nowhere outside themselves to check from. The world adds a newer thought: a second opinion everyone has is not a second opinion, and a machine that gives a child three answers spares him one voice at the price of being shaped by none. For that to land, the reader must be holding both answers, not only Rob's, when Tess reaches the bowl on Friday (F1).

## Findings

F1: MAJOR — The "vanity" half of the question has nothing on the scale: the silence at fifteen counts for Rob, the lights are undecidable by design, the bud discounts the curve in the same beat, the last image gives the bud a train time and Rob the ear, and the one entry the spine names as Rob plainly wrong (the party at nine) is in the must-not list and in no beat, so a reader settles "gift" by Wednesday night and the story slides toward the noble-refuser neighbour the spine itself names. Put the party at nine, or one entry like it, in Beat 1 or 2, with a cost still visible in Tess at nineteen, so the question is still open when she reaches the bowl on Friday (Beats 1–2; Ending image; first bullet of "What this story must not become").
F2: MINOR — The ending counts among its costs that "the one person who could have been asked never was", but no beat gives Tess a moment where the question to Rob rises and she lets it go, so her silence is held by the must-not list ("Nothing from the audit is ever said to Rob") rather than by her. Her reason is to hand (any answer he gave would be one more sentence to grow round, and she has just refused Kit one), and it needs one visible moment, at the bowl on Friday or the door on Saturday (Ending image; Beats 5–6).
F3: MINOR — Kit's "Why did I do it?" is the story's question in the mouth of a boy whose register is "Dunno"; at six, having just heard "Your sister's goes red", what he has to take up with the sister he learned the bowl from is her (did you know he knew; did you see mine was gone?), and letting him ask that would put her Thursday choice in front of the one it was run on (Beat 4).
F4: MINOR — The passage shows Rob, after "No. I don't", still saying "Give it here" and putting Kit's bud back in the bowl, so his watching Tess undo that a day and a half later without a word is the least prepared act in the spine, and it is the Turn. "He had not come to her door once since August" can carry it, but the draft owes the reader one sign of which it is: her he is letting, or the bowl he is giving up (Beat 5; the passage's last lines).
F5: MINOR — The bowl has a date but neither a cause nor a reach: "eleven years" starts it when Tess was eight and Kit one, but the spine never says what happened then (or where the children's mother is), nor whether the buds stayed in the bowl only indoors or also on the moor at thirteen, in Halifax at fifteen and at school. Cause and reach are the weights on the "vanity" side (grief, rivalry or pride; a house rule or a whole childhood), and the Turn's "a man whose words he cannot check" is true only on the larger reach (Who; Turn).
F6: MINOR — The spine's wording of Thursday's plain sentence ("the one it is done on is asleep on the far side of the wall") uses the story's own frame ("done on", "done to") and so states outright the mirror that the curve, the lights and Friday already let the reader find. In her own words the sentence should carry her motive (to see what her father does) and the fact of Kit asleep, and stop there (Beat 3).
F7: MINOR — "The question" makes Rob's entitlement turn on one outcome ("If ... true, he beat the bud and owes nothing"), which quietly turns the world's question (may he take a risk he cannot price, on someone else?) into "did it pay?", and that is why every weight is piled on the lights (F1); being right at the lights would not pay off the years at the bottom of the curve. This is a point about the question's logic, not a disagreement with its answer (The question).
F8: MINOR — Kit's loss of "the growing-round" is counted as a cost, but the story's only image of growing round is the wire, damage found by a chain, so unless Tess's growing round Rob's sentence is felt somewhere as what made her and not only what walled her in, a reader will count Kit's shield as pure gain and his whole cost as a red ear (Beat 2; Ending image).
F9: NIT — "not checking, because he cannot and would not": his own bud is in the bowl (the passage's "beside his own", Friday's "three buds") and in this world nobody has none, so "cannot" is false; "would not" carries the image alone (Ending image).
