# Story bible

Everything that must stay consistent. `check_continuity.py` reads the Names and Invented-terms tables of
this file against the manuscript: it fails on a spelling in one that is one character from the other, and
on an entry here the manuscript never uses. The Rules, Timeline, and Physical-details sections below are
for the writer and the critics; no program parses them, and nothing checks that the story obeys them.

## Word that is not a drift

Words in the story that sit one character from a name or term above and are meant to. `check_continuity.py`
reads one character's difference as a name that drifted across a revision, which is usually right; when it
is not — Mara in a story that mentions Mars — the word that is *not* a bible entry goes here, one row per
word, and the check leaves it alone. Two names that are both in the bible (Mara and Mira, say) need no row
at all: the check knows they are both meant to be there. A plural never needs declaring.

Every row needs its reason. This table switches off the check that catches a character renamed halfway
through a revision, so a row with a blank reason is an off-switch nobody has to justify; the check refuses
one, exactly as it refuses a motif declared with no reason.

| Word that is not a drift | Why both are meant to be there |
|---|---|
| it | The pronoun, one deletion from "Kit", and the story's only name for the bud ("the bud said"; "Ask it"; "What did you ask it?"). Capitalised at the start of a sentence, "It", the same word. Unavoidable in any English sentence, and here it is the machine |
| bit | Kit's answer to "Does it hurt?" ("Bit.") in the passage, and the ordinary word; one substitution from "Kit" |
| kid | The ordinary word for a child, one substitution from "Kit"; allowed in the client's mouth or Tess's thoughts, never as a name for Kit |

## Rules of the conceit

**The world (given, and not stated in the story).** It is 2040. Frontier progress stopped in September 2026. Every person has, always on, one model of that quality, theirs alone, never much better than 2026's and never superhuman; nobody has two or none, and a share cannot be pooled, sold or accumulated. The model is fluent, competent and fallible; it is not a character and has no will. The story never names the year, the stop, or the arrangement; it shows them: everyone's is the same, the client's bud at Heptonstall plans a felling in the voice that Tess's grades her father in, and a bud in any ear but its owner's is silent. Three things in the story only this world can do, and the draft leans on them rather than on the silent-bud lock, which a 2025 phone also has: there is no second opinion anywhere, because every bud in the valley is the one voice at the one ceiling, so the only judgment Tess knows that is not that voice is her father's, made by eleven years of not using it; the bud is there before every decision, so that at four on Thursday the plan arrives before she has decided anything, and going back up is a departure chosen rather than a thing left undone; and at five the bud grades her father through the wall as he speaks, sentence by sentence, in her ear. The bowl is strange only because every other house is not: in 2040 it made that house the one place a child could not check a word said to it, which in 2025 was every house. The manuscript shows this by the client's house (a bud in every ear, a trampoline for the children) and by Kit at Calder High like everyone, and says none of it.

**The bud.** The model is worn as an earpiece, "the bud", kept in the ear; the outline's choice, kept. A bud answers only its owner: Tess cannot ask Rob's or Kit's, nobody can ask hers, and in another person's ear a bud says nothing at all, which is how, on Friday, she tells Kit's from her own, since nothing else does; that silence is a lock, and it does nothing else in the story. What Kit asked his on Thursday can never be known ("Stuff."). Worn, it hears what its owner hears and may speak unasked to say what it has noticed or to offer a next step ("He's found it. You could still go in."); it never argues for itself, never apologises, never asks to be worn, never uses her name, and never says it wants or feels. It answers a whisper at a whisper. Its register is the passage's: complete, mild sentences; "generally considered good practice"; "it may also be worth"; "comparable to typical advice"; a choice of three where a person would give one. It is fallible the way a thoughtful generalist is fallible, never the way a fool is: it cannot weigh a silence, it cannot see wire inside a trunk, and it cannot hold its reasonable father still at the lights from one asking to the next. It is competent enough to name what Tess will not: on Monday, asked about the curve, it says it may also be worth noting that the earliest entries follow a change in the household, and she tells it to go on. No bud in this story is a recording, a witness or a lifelog; nothing is ever played back. The audit runs on what Tess has told hers, and on nothing else.

**The bowl.** Inside the house every bud goes in the bowl by the door; outside it (school, the care home, the client's garden) buds are worn as everyone's are. That is the rule's whole reach: nothing has been said to a child in that house, in eleven years, with a bud in the room, until Thursday. It began the week their mother went to Manchester, in April 2029; Tess remembers its first night as her father's hand at her ear, at the door, taking hers out, and nothing said. The manuscript gives the bowl's age ("eleven years"; "since she was eight") and the first night's image without the mother; her one clause comes at six on Thursday, with the trains, and a reader who wants to can count from Kit's age back to the bowl. Rob has never explained the bowl and never named the thing in it. He refers to any bud by its owner ("yours", "his", "Kit's", "your sister's", "Give it here") and to nothing about it otherwise. His own has not left the bowl in eleven years; he goes to work without it and has a phone for calls; his keys go in on top of the buds every evening. A bud in the bowl is on and hears the hall (the door, the keys, the bottom of the stairs) and nothing that is said upstairs. Nobody in the story uses a noun for the model but "bud"; people say "mine", "yours", "it".

**The audit.** Tess's word, from the home's inspections. Since August, on every night she is home, she has taken hers up after Rob goes to bed and put it back before his alarm at five; the rim of her left ear is red in the mornings, Rob has seen it and said nothing, and he has not come to her door since. Forty-one entries, her list, made from her own telling over six weeks; eleven runs, the tenth on Sunday night and the eleventh on Monday; each run grades each entry against what a reasonable father would have done. The runs agree wherever the entry is plain and disagree only where it is not. All eleven score the party worse than the bud: a reasonable father turns the van round at the sick. All eleven draw the same curve, and when she asks, the bud says the shape is built into any scoring of a learner, that it cannot separate learning from luck or from the second child being easier, and that the earliest entries follow a change in the household; she passes over the last, and the omission is hers. All eleven score the drive from Halifax at fifteen "comparable to typical advice", and the manuscript never says what that grade misses. Only the lights move: run four graded him well there, run nine badly, run ten kinder than nine, run eleven kinder still, and the bud offers three other reasons for the moor each time; the eleven fathers are the readings the lights permit, not noise in the instrument, and no sentence in the manuscript invites a reader to average them. No run narrates an alternative life or a different father as a scene: each is a grading of the same events, and she envies none of them. There is no twelfth run, and the story never calls that a price. Nothing from the audit, not a grade, an entry or the curve, is ever spoken to Rob or Kit, and Rob never asks what it found. Three entries are named in the story and no others: nine, the party (plainly wrong, and its cost still in her); thirteen, the moor and the lights (undecidable; the answer the story refuses); fifteen, the silent drive from Halifax (graded middling; the writer knows the grade is wrong, and the manuscript does not say so). The three do not sum, and nobody in the story adds them.

**Rob's judgment.** Real, and shown once in the present (the beech), once plainly wrong in the past (the party), once undecidable (the lights). The party has a scene's weight in beat 2: a birthday party in Mytholmroyd, Tess nine, that had started before they got there; she asked to go home; she was sick in the van on the way; he wiped her face with the rag from under the seat, said "You'll be fine once you're in", and walked her in with his hand on her back, into a room already going. The error is placed after the sick, so that the bud's grade is credible: before the sick, the sentence is what typical advice says; after it, a reasonable father turns the van round, and Rob kept to his sentence past the evidence. It is a wrong man's error, not a cruel one, and the scene gives him nothing crueller than the sentence and the hand. From then on she has been early to everything, so as never to walk into a room already full: on the client's wall an hour before the van on Wednesday, at every shift by most of an hour, at the door on Saturday fifty-four minutes before the 9:14 for a walk of ten. The earliness is shown as dread, not reliability: she would rather sit on a wall or stand on a platform than wait in a house for a thing to start. Nobody in the story names this as the party's residue; the reader is shown the party once and the earliness three times. The beech: the client's bud says the big limb over the lawn in three; Tess on the ropes has its line before Rob says it, because she learned to see from him; Rob lays it whole between the greenhouse and the trampoline, and it goes there; then the chain sparks on fence wire the tree closed over decades ago, and nobody could have seen it; the client says his had not mentioned wire. The client's bud is Tess's own in another ear, and she knows it: it plans in the voice that grades her father at night, and it misses the line and the wire as hers would have. On Thursday he starts the sentence and stops it ("No. I don't. Why did you?"), which the bud grades well and which the story leaves as either the curve rising or a man failing to do for his son what he did for his daughter; then "Give it here", and Kit's bud goes back in the bowl by his hand. The lights have a second reading, shown once and never settled: the sentence as the wish of a man who put every bud in a bowl so that his children would have to come to him. It surfaces at six on Thursday, when Tess hears her own version of the sentence rise as her own wish, and nowhere else; nobody says it of Rob. On Friday he watches Tess try the two small buds in her ear, put hers back and take Kit's, and says "Right." and goes through: it is her he is letting, not the bowl he is giving up; he learns nothing at the bowl that he has not known since August, and his own bud is in the bowl on Saturday morning as it has been for eleven years. He never reads the list, never asks for it, and never says why he kept the bowl; the reason Tess gives him (so that he and not the bud would know his children) is hers, never confirmed.

**Tess's own practice.** She has followed every care plan to the letter for a year. Since the moor she has rung him once: at fifteen, from a flat in Halifax, one in the morning, and he came and said nothing the whole way; otherwise she has made herself someone nobody need fetch (she takes the bus to Halifax, and walks to the station on Saturday). Her care for Kit is of the same kind, done without asking him: on Monday at four, going down to put hers back, she takes his football boots off the step, knocks them clean and stands them under the hall table, as she has since she was fourteen; the manuscript shows this once, as an ordinary thing, and does not gloss it. Thursday at four is her first departure from a plan, and it is run on Kit: the bud gives the plan (wake him, return it, talk to him later) before she has decided anything, and the other (put it back herself); she does neither, and the story says once, in her own words, why she goes back up (to see what her father does) and that Kit is asleep, and stops there; it does not say "done on", "done to", or who it is done to. This is said once and not repeated, argued or named ("practised on", "raised or", "consent", "judgment", "vanity", "counsel" and "model" never appear). She goes back up with hers still in, so that at five two places in the bowl are empty under his keys, hers and Kit's: she lets them both be found, and Rob, with two doors to choose from, goes to Kit's; "He had not come to her door once since August" carries that, and the manuscript lets it. At five the bud grades Rob live and starts on her; she takes it out before it can finish. At six the sentence that rises in her, in her father's form, is exactly "You wanted him to have to come up", unasked; as it rises she hears whose wish is in it, hers, and the manuscript gives that once, in a clause; she does not say the sentence, then or ever. Rob's original, at the lights, is exactly "You wanted me to have to come and get you". At six she can also do the sum about the trains (see Kit) and does, inside the mother's one clause, and asks Kit nothing and tells nobody. Friday at the bowl is her second departure and is a decision made for Kit, in front of Rob: she puts one small bud in her left ear and it speaks (hers), puts it back, puts the other in and it is silent (Kit's), and carries Kit's up, leaving hers in the bowl: the rule kept for herself by her own hand, the first night since August, and broken for Kit in front of the man who kept it. The question rises in her there (whether it was true, at the lights) and she does not ask it, for the reason she gave Kit no sentence at six. Friday night, the night before Leeds, is the first since August without hers, and she wants it: not for a twelfth run, which she knows would give her a twelfth father, but for the small questions about tomorrow that everyone asks theirs, what to take, how early to be there, the kind she has not put to a person since thirteen. She does not go down, because going down would make Friday hers: the point of leaving hers in the bowl was that the night's breaking of the rule was for Kit and not for her, and fetching hers back would unmake it. The wanting and the not going are the price, in the manuscript as in the spine; "no run twelve" is a fact of the night and never its price, and the draft does not play it as the sacrifice. Through the wall she hears Kit whisper, in her whisper, and cannot hear the answers. On Saturday morning she takes hers from the bowl at twenty past eight and leaves with it in her ear: she goes as everyone goes, and the story does not say what that means.

**Kit.** Twelve. Wears his bud at school like everyone; Thursday was the first time he took it from the bowl at night, and he learned that from her (the wall; her mornings). He speaks in monosyllables to Rob ("Dunno", "Stuff", "Bit"). At six on Thursday he asks Tess about her, not about himself: "Did you see mine was gone?", and she says "Yes."; then he asks whether the Leeds train goes from Hebden or you change at Halifax, and knows before she does (it is direct); and he knows the ones the other way as well, to Manchester, where their mother has been since he was one. That is the mother's one clause in the manuscript, spent here, and the whole of Kit's trace: what he asked in the night is never given, and the reader gets only that he knows the times both ways. Tess can do the sum and does, in that clause; she does not ask him what for, does not tell Rob, and the manuscript does not return to it; nobody says Kit will go anywhere, and no scene follows from it. On Friday she gives him the bud with "Ask it. It'll give you three"; his fingers close on it; she does not stay to see it go in. That night he whispers to it, in her whisper, through the wall. He sleeps in it, left ear, and by Saturday the rim has begun to go red; at the door he tells Rob, from it, that the 9:14 is four minutes late. He never asks the story's question, and "Why did I do it?" is not in the manuscript.

**Words and numbers.** No year is written in the manuscript; ages carry the time. No clock time is in digits but the 9:14; "twenty past eight" is in words. No numbers on the first page; "forty-one" and "eleven" once each, on the second. The door's numbers, which a judge will count: she leaves at twenty past eight, fifty-four minutes before the 9:14, for a walk of ten; the narration may give "fifty-four" once, or give no number and let the reader count, and never says "fifty"; by the timetable she is forty-four minutes on the platform, by Kit's bud forty-eight, and neither figure need appear. The mother is never named ("their mother", "Mum") and appears in one clause, at six on Thursday, which places her in Manchester since Kit was one and gives no cause. The client at Heptonstall and the care home are unnamed. Deliberate changes from the outline: "counsel" is dropped from the story's vocabulary (nobody in a Hebden Bridge hallway says it); the party at nine is the one plain error, shown in Beat 2 at a scene's weight, its error placed after the sick and its cost still visible; the mother's leaving is fixed as the bowl's start, without a stated cause, and her clause is spent at six rather than at the bowl; Kit's question at six is about Tess, not about himself, and his trace is the trains both ways; the two small buds are alike and told apart only by which one speaks; "It was the silence" is not said.

## Physical details that must not drift

- The house: a stone terrace on the hillside above the station in Hebden Bridge, a ten-minute walk down. The front door opens onto the hall; the stairs come straight down to it. Three bedrooms up: Rob's at the back; Tess's, the middle front room; Kit's, the box room over the hall, hers until she was eleven. Tess's and Kit's share a wall, on Tess's left as she sits up in bed. The front step is where Kit leaves his football boots; under the hall table is where Tess stands them.
- The bowl: brown-glazed earthenware, once a fruit bowl, a chip on the rim; on the narrow hall table inside the front door. It holds every bud in the house, and Rob's keys on top of them from evening to five. Rob's bud is the old kind, larger, and has not left the bowl in eleven years; Tess's and Kit's are the small kind, pale, matte, the size of a fingernail, and alike: no scratch, no mark, nothing tells them apart but which one speaks in her ear. On Monday at four it holds Rob's and Kit's under the keys, and then Tess's too. On Thursday at five it holds Rob's alone under the keys. On Friday evening it holds three buds and the keys; on Friday night, Rob's and Tess's; from twenty past eight on Saturday, Rob's alone.
- The bud in the ear: Tess wears hers in the left ear; since August the rim of that ear is red and hot in the mornings. Kit wears his in the left too, from Friday night, and the rim has begun to go red by Saturday. A bud going into the bowl makes a click that carries up the stairs. Kit's bud in Tess's ear on Friday says nothing.
- Tess at five on Thursday: sitting up in bed, left palm flat on the wall; the hand goes from the wall to her left ear when Rob says "Your sister's goes red". She takes the bud out with the same hand; it goes on in her palm, very small.
- Kit's bed creaks when it takes his weight; the board by the bed is the one she knew by heel.
- Rob: forty-seven; alarm at five every working morning of her life; the van (white; the heater roars; a rag under the driver's seat); a head torch, round his neck at the lights in 2034; the saw, whose chain sparks on the wire; his keys. He says "Right." to begin things, and on Friday it is all he says.
- The party at nine: a birthday party in Mytholmroyd, already going when they get there; sick in the van on the way; the rag; the fixed line, said after the sick; his hand on her back through the door. No other detail is fixed; the writer may give the house a gate and a noise through the door, and gives Rob nothing that would make him cruel rather than wrong.
- The beech at Heptonstall: a big limb the client's bud would have taken over the lawn in three; Rob lays it whole between the greenhouse and the trampoline. Fence wire, decades old, deep in the trunk; the chain sparks and is ruined; "Grew round it." The client is a man with a bud in, relaying, who says afterwards that his had not mentioned wire; the garden has a low wall on the lane, where Tess has sat for an hour when the van comes; the day is dry, bright and still.
- The week's weather: dry throughout; Wednesday bright and still; Saturday grey with mist in the valley.
- The line: Hebden Bridge station is on the line that runs down the valley through Mytholmroyd and Halifax to Leeds, and the other way, up the valley through Todmorden, to Manchester. The 9:14 is direct to Leeds; you do not change at Halifax, and Kit says so before Tess can. "The other way" in the manuscript means toward Manchester, and the manuscript says so once, at six.
- Tess's bag on Saturday: one holdall. Her bud is in her ear when she leaves, out of the bowl at twenty past eight.
- The 9:14 from Hebden Bridge to Leeds: four minutes late, said by Kit from his bud; Rob does not check and would not.
