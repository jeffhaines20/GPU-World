An hour after her father went up, Tess went down for her bud.

She did not put the light on. The bowl stood on the narrow table inside the front door, where it had stood since she was small, brown-glazed, with a chip out of the rim, and his keys lay in it on top of the buds, where they went every evening when he came in. She lifted the keys by the ring and felt under them. Her father's bud was the old kind, bigger, and it had not been out of the bowl since the week the bowl began. The small ones were alike, pale and matte, the size of a fingernail, with no mark on either to tell it from the other; she took the nearer, because the near side was where she put hers. The keys went back on top. She went up on the edges of the stairs, where they kept quiet.

Her room was the middle room at the front. She sat up in bed with her back to the headboard and the wall on her left, put the bud in her left ear, and said, at a whisper, that she was ready.

"Whenever you like," the bud said, at a whisper, because she had.

She whispered because of the wall. On the far side of it Kit turned over and his bed creaked, and she waited with her hand cupped over her ear until the board beside it had settled; she knew that board. Then the house was still, and the hour was hers.

"Run eleven," she said.

The care home in Halifax, where she had done a year of nights, was audited twice a year: a woman came with a standard and a list and went down what had been done against what should have been. Audit was the home's word. It was the only one Tess had for what she had been doing since August, so it was the word.

The letter from Leeds had come in August. She had read it standing at the bowl, the letter in one hand and her bud in the other, and had known before she finished it that she was leaving in six weeks, and that before she went she wanted to know what the years in this house had cost her, in a figure she could read. That night she carried the bud up the stairs instead of leaving it. Every night since, when she was home, she had taken it up once he had gone up, and put it back before his alarm.

The list was in a notebook from work. Forty-one entries, in her own hand: every occasion she could remember on which her father had decided something about one of his children, most of them about her, each given to the bud in her own words. Each run graded each entry against what a reasonable father would have done. That was the bud's phrase, in its own voice, every time, and she had stopped hearing it as a phrase. Eleven years he had kept the bowl. She had been eight. She remembered the first night of it as his hand at her ear, at the door, taking hers out, and nothing said.

It gave her first what every run gave her, because she asked for it first. The party, which was the earliest entry, a birthday when she was nine: on that the runs did not move. A reasonable father turned the van round when the child was sick. Then the curve. It had no way to show her the curve, so it gave it to her in words, year by year: her father against the bud, worse than it early and better than it late, her years down in the bottom left and Kit's up in the top right. She had drawn it from its words inside the cover of the notebook on the first night, and ten nights since had given her no reason to redraw it.

"Why that shape," she whispered.

"Any scoring of a learner draws it," the bud said. "Learning can't be separated here from luck, or from a second child being easier than the first. It may also be worth noting that the earliest entries follow a change in the household."

"Go on," she said, and it went on, as she had told it to, to the next entry.

Then the one it would not hold still on. Thirteen. She had walked out of Calder High at lunch and gone up Midgley Moor, and stayed up it until it was dark. Her father had come up the moor towards her with the head torch lit, not calling. When he reached her, the first thing he did was take the bud out of her ear and zip it into his jacket, as he had taken it at the door when she was eight. What came after, it had only from her, six years late and in her words. They walked down to the road with nothing said. Then the van, with the heater roaring, and the lights in Mytholmroyd, on red, where he had turned in his seat and told her the one thing he had ever told her about what she was.

Run four had graded him well there. Run nine had graded him badly. Run ten had been kinder than nine, and tonight's was kinder than ten. Each night, when she pushed it, the bud offered three other reasons a girl of thirteen might go up a moor, different ones each time, mild and complete, each as likely as his. Tonight's were that she had wanted to be on her own; that there had been something at school; and that she had not thought about being found at all. Three from it, and one from him, and she could not tell which of the four was hers. She had told it everything she knew about that day. It had given her the same father at the party every night and a different man in the van at Mytholmroyd. It was gone three.

Since the moor she had made herself into someone nobody need fetch. She took the bus to Halifax, and the bus back at the end of a night with the valley going grey, and when the bus did not come she walked. She had phoned him once.

At four she took it out and went down. Under the keys the bowl held her father's, the big old one, and Kit's, which had been there since he came in from Calder High and would be there until he left for it. She put hers in beside Kit's, and it went in with a click. Kit's football boots were on the step outside, where he had kicked them off. She brought them in, knocked them together over the mat, stood them under the hall table, as she had done for years, and went up.

* * *

On Wednesday there was a beech to come down in a garden at the top of Heptonstall, and Tess was sitting on the garden wall an hour before the van. The morning was dry and bright and the lane was quiet. She had walked up from Hebden Bridge with her bud in, because outside the house you wore it, like everyone, and it had given her the lane, and then, since she knew the lane, had gone quiet. She had been early to everything since she was nine. She had been at every shift by most of an hour, in the staff room with the care plans, going down each one to the letter before the night had started. She would rather sit an hour on a wall than wait in a house for a thing to begin.

From the wall she had the garden: the beech, taller than the house, with its big limb out over the lawn; the greenhouse under the end of it; the trampoline with its net; a swing. The house had children in it. Through the open door she could hear them being got ready for school, a woman and a child, and the ordinary noise of a house that was late.

The party had been in Mytholmroyd, in a house with a gate. She had asked, in the van on the way, to go home, and he had kept driving, and then she had been sick, into the footwell, and he had pulled in and got the rag from under his seat and wiped her face with it, not roughly. "You'll be fine once you're in," he said. The party had started before they got there. She could hear it from the van, through the door of the house, a noise with no gaps in it, and she had gone in with his hand on her back, through the gate and through the door, into a room that was already going.

The client came out at half past eight with a mug and a bud in his ear and stood on his lawn and told her the plan for the limb. In three: the end first, then the middle, then the stub back to the trunk, and the lawn roped off below. He said it as it came, in complete mild sentences, from his ear to his mouth, and she knew the voice. It was hers, at night, grading her father. On her last shift she had got as far as "Can I ask you something" with the nurse in charge, meaning to ask about the moor, and the nurse's hand had gone to her own ear before Tess had finished the sentence, and Tess had said something about a care plan instead. There was no one in the valley to ask who would not ask theirs, and theirs was this one.

"You'll be Rob Haworth's lass," the client said, in his own voice, which was broader. "He'll see what's best." A boy of about Kit's age came out onto the step behind him with his in, eating toast, and stood there listening to something she could not hear.

The van came up the lane on the hour and her father got out and looked at the tree.

"Right," he said.

The man gave her father the three again. Her father looked at the limb while the man talked, and at the greenhouse under it, and at the trampoline, and at the grass between them, and Tess, on the ropes below, had the line of it before he said it: not in three; whole; and down into that gap. She had learned to see that from him, on Saturdays.

"Whole," her father said. "Between the glass and the trampoline."

The man touched his ear, and waited, and whatever it said to him he kept to himself. "Go on, then," he said, in the broader voice, and the boy looked at him.

Her father went up. The saw started above her. She kept the rope in both hands and her eyes up and did not look at the two of them on the path, listening to something she could not hear. When the limb came it came in one piece and swung on the line she had seen and lay down on the grass where he had said it would, with a stride to spare either side.

"That's landed where he said it would," her bud said in her ear, and on the step the boy said it too, aloud, to no one, the same words in the same order, half a breath behind. Her father, who had neither in his ear, started on the trunk.

The chain hit the wire a foot and a half down. It sparked, and screamed, and he had the saw off and out before anyone else had moved. The wire was deep in the trunk, old fence wire, grey, that the beech had closed over before any of them were born, and there was rot about it, and the chain was ruined.

"Grew round it," her father said.

The client had come across the lawn and stopped at the limb, as if that were the thing, and looked at the wire, and touched his ear.

"Mine never mentioned wire," the client said. On the step his boy's hand had gone to his own ear, and his mouth was moving, asking.

Her father did not answer that. He fetched the spare chain from the van.

They drove home down the hill with the ruined chain on the dash between them, and he did not talk, which was the van's usual. The winter she was fifteen she had phoned him at one in the morning from a flat in Halifax, the once since the moor, and he had come, and the silence had lasted the whole way home, from the door of the flat to the bowl, and up the stairs, and into the next day. Every run had graded that drive with the same phrase, comparable to typical advice, and she had never asked it what the advice would have been.

* * *

At four on Thursday she went down to put hers back, and the place in the bowl where Kit's had been was empty.

"His isn't here," she whispered, and before the whisper was finished the plan was in her ear. Wake him; take it from him; put it back; talk to him later in the day, when he was up. Or the other: take it from his ear while he slept, and put it back herself, and say nothing. "Either is within what's usually advised," the bud said.

She stood with the keys in her hand. She knew where Kit had learned to take his up. He had had six weeks of her through the wall, and six weeks of her at breakfast with one ear red. Under the keys the bowl now held their father's bud alone, the big old one, where it had lain since the bowl began, hearing the door and the keys and the foot of the stairs. She did neither of the things in her ear. She put the keys back on it and went up with hers still in, to see what her father would do when his alarm went and there were two places empty under his keys, hers and Kit's. Kit was asleep; she could hear that through the wall.

Her father's alarm went at five, as it had every working morning of her life, and she heard him on the stairs, then at the bowl, and then, which never happened, on the stairs again.

Kit's door. The bed took his weight. It had been her room until she was eleven; she knew which board his heel was on.

"Right," her father said.

A small sound from Kit, the sound of being woken.

"He's found it," the bud said in her ear. "You could still go in."

"Shh."

"I know why you did it," her father said.

Her palm was flat on the wall. Six years she had lived inside the rest of that sentence. The lights in Mytholmroyd, the heater roaring, the head torch still round his neck: You wanted me to have to come and get you.

"You did it because—"

She waited for the rest. It did not come.

"No," her father said. "I don't. Why did you?"

"Dunno."

"What did you ask it?"

"Stuff."

"Right."

Then, after long enough that she thought he had finished: "Does it hurt? In your ear."

"Bit."

"Your sister's goes red."

Her hand left the wall and found her own ear, the left, round the bud. The rim was hot under her fingers, and had been, she supposed, every morning since August.

"He's asking rather than telling," the bud said, "which is generally considered good practice, though it may also be worth—"

She took it out. It went on in her palm, very small, grading him.

"Give it here," her father said. She heard Kit give it, heard him go down, and, because the stairs came down to the door, heard the click of Kit's going into the bowl beside his own, and the door, and the van.

He had not come to her door. He had not come to her door once since August.

At six Kit was in her doorway, twelve and in his school shirt, one hand on the frame, and he did not come in.

"Did you see mine was gone?"

"Yes."

The sentence rose in her, in her father's shape. You wanted him to have to come up. It came whole, the way his had come at the lights, and it was ready for the doorway before she caught what was in it. The wish in it was hers. She was the one who had left Kit's place in the bowl empty for their father to find, and had sat with her palm on the wall to find out which door he chose. Whatever Kit had wanted at four, the sentence fitted her first; and so perhaps his had fitted him, at the lights, with the torch still round his neck.

It might have been true of Kit. She did not say it. Their father had found him at five, and he had come as far as her door and not in, and she let him wait there.

"Does the Leeds train go from Hebden," Kit said, "or do you change at Halifax?"

She had the times somewhere. "It's direct," he said, before she had found them. "Saturday it's the 9:14." He had the ones the other way along the line as well, up the valley, to Manchester, where their mother had been since he was one; he gave her the first of the morning and the last at night, ten to seven and twenty to eleven, as if she had asked. The bowl was as old as that. She did not ask him what the times the other way were for, and he went down to the bowl for his, where their father had put it, and to school.

* * *

Her father came in on Friday at the usual time, and his keys went into the bowl on top of three buds.

Tess was in the hall. She had been in the hall a while. She came past him to the table and lifted the keys off and took one of the small ones and put it in her left ear, in front of him.

"Your father's in," it said, because the last thing to reach it in the bowl was his keys coming down. So that one was hers. She put it back, and it clicked. She took the other and put it in, and in her ear it said nothing, and went on saying nothing. That was all that had ever told the two of them apart: hers would speak to her, and Kit's would speak to nobody but Kit. She closed her fingers round it.

Her own she left in the bowl under the keys, where it had not spent a night since August. She turned for the stairs with Kit's in her hand. Her father had watched all of it from the door with his jacket still on.

"Right," he said, and went through to the kitchen.

That was the whole of what was said at the bowl, then or after. The question was in her mouth: how he had known, at the lights, or whether he had. The man who could have answered it was through the kitchen door with his back to her, washing his hands at the sink. She had stood in a doorway the morning before with a sentence for Kit and had not given it. This was its pair, and she did not take it. She went up.

Kit was on his bed with his boots on the floor and his door open, and his left hand was at his ear, where there was no bud yet. She held it out on her palm.

"Ask it. It'll give you three."

His fingers closed round it. She did not stay to see it go in.

That night she sat up in bed with the wall on her left and her ear empty. Kit's door was shut; it was never shut. Through the wall came the creak of his bed, and the board, and then a sound she could not make words of, the pitch of a whisper or a boy turning over. She could have had it with her palm flat on the wall. She left her hands in her lap. Downstairs, under his keys, her father's bud and hers lay together in the bowl for the first night since the letter; his had been there for every key and every click since the bowl began, and in all that time there had been no ear for it to speak into. She wanted hers. For the things everyone asked theirs the night before: what to take, how early to be there, whether the holdall would go on the rack, the kind of question she had not put to a person since she was thirteen. A twelfth run would have given her a twelfth man in the van with the heater on, and she had eleven of those. The bowl was thirteen steps down, and the fourth stair from the bottom was the one that spoke. She did not go down. She had left hers in the bowl so that what she did there was done for Kit, and going down for it now would make it a thing she had done for herself.

* * *

Saturday was grey, with mist in the valley over Hebden Bridge station. At twenty past eight she lifted the keys and took hers from beside his, the old one, and put it in, and it answered, before she had said a word to it, that the holdall would go on the rack and the mist would be off the platform by ten. Fifty-four minutes before the 9:14, for a walk of ten. The mist would be down when she got there, and she would stand in it.

She stood at the door with the holdall. Kit's boots were on the step outside, where he had kicked them off, and she left them there. Kit was on the bottom stair with his in, the left ear. "Dad," he said, from it. "The 9:14's four minutes late." In her left ear, in the same words and the same voice, hers said it too, so that she heard it twice and their father once.

Her father did not check. He was looking at the rim of Kit's ear, which was going red.
