An hour after her father went up, Tess went down for hers.

She did not put the hall light on. The bowl stood on the narrow table inside the front door, where it had stood since she was small, brown-glazed, with a chip out of the rim, and his keys lay in it on top of the rest, where they went every evening when he came in. She lifted the keys by the ring and felt under them. His was the old kind, bigger, and it had not been out of the bowl since the week the bowl began. The small ones were alike, pale and matte, with nothing on either to tell it from the other, and she took the nearer, because the near side was where she put hers. The keys went back on top. She went up on the edges of the stairs, where they kept quiet.

Her room was the middle room at the front. She sat up in bed with her back to the headboard and the wall on her left, put it in her left ear, and said, at a whisper, that she was ready.

"Whenever you like," the bud said, at a whisper, because she had.

She whispered because of the wall. On the far side of it Kit's bed creaked as it took his weight, turning over, and she waited with her hand cupped at her ear until the board by his bed had settled; she knew that board. Then the house was still, and the hour was hers.

"Run eleven," she said.

The home in Halifax was audited twice a year, a woman with a standard and a list going down what had been done against what should have been, and that was the only word Tess had for this, so it was the word. The audit was six weeks old. It had started the week the letter came from Leeds. She had read the letter standing at the bowl with hers in her hand, and had known, with it still in her hand, that she was leaving in six weeks, and that she wanted to know before she went what the years in this house had cost her, in a figure she could read. That night she carried hers up the stairs instead of leaving it, and on every night since that she was home she had taken it up after he went to bed and put it back before his alarm. Forty-one entries, each an occasion on which her father had decided something about her, told to it in her own words, and each graded against what a reasonable father would have done: its phrase, in its own voice, every time, and she had stopped hearing it as a phrase. Eleven years he had kept the bowl. She had been eight. She remembered the first night of it as his hand at her ear, at the door, taking hers out, and nothing said.

It gave her first what every run gave her, because she asked for it first. On the party, the earliest entry, the runs did not move: a reasonable father turned the van round at the sick. On the curve they did not move either. It drew her father against the bud, year on year, worse than it early and better than it late, her years down in the bottom left and Kit's up in the top right, and she had looked at that shape ten nights running and it had not changed.

"Why that shape," she whispered.

"Any scoring of a learner draws it," the bud said. "Learning can't be separated here from luck, or from a second child being easier than the first. It may also be worth noting that the earliest entries follow a change in the household."

"Go on," she said, and it went on.

Then the entry it would not hold still on. Thirteen: out of Calder High at lunch, up Midgley Moor until it was dark, and her father coming up the moor towards her with the head torch lit, not calling, and the walk down to the road with nothing said, and the van afterwards with the heater roaring, and the lights in Mytholmroyd, on red, where he had turned in his seat and told her the one thing he had ever told her about what she was. Run four had graded him well there. Run nine had graded him badly. Run ten had been kinder than nine, and tonight's was kinder than ten, and each time, asked, the bud offered three other reasons a girl of thirteen might go up a moor, different ones each night, mild and complete, each as likely as his; tonight's were that she had wanted to be on her own, that there had been something at school, and that she had not thought about being found at all, and she could not tell which of the four was hers. She had told it everything she knew about that day, and it had given her the same father at the party every night and a different man in the van at Mytholmroyd. It was gone three.

Since that night she had made herself into someone nobody need fetch. She took the bus to Halifax, and the bus back at the end of a night with the valley going grey, and when the bus did not come she walked. She had phoned him once.

At four she took it out, and went down, and put it back in the bowl on the near side of his, under the keys, beside Kit's, which had been there since he came in from Calder High like everyone; it went in with a click. Kit's football boots were on the step outside, where he had kicked them off. She brought them in, knocked them together over the mat, and stood them under the hall table, as she had since she was fourteen, and went up.

The beech was in a garden at the top of Heptonstall, and on Wednesday Tess was sitting on the garden wall an hour before the van. The morning was dry and bright and nothing in it moved. She had walked up from Hebden Bridge with her bud in, because outside the house you wore it, like everyone, and it had given her the lane, and then, since she knew the lane, nothing. She had been early to everything since she was nine. At the home she had been at every shift by most of an hour, in the staff room with the care plans, going down each one to the letter before the night had started. She would rather sit an hour on a wall than wait in a house for a thing to begin.

From the wall she had the whole of the garden: the beech, taller than the house, with its big limb out over the lawn, the greenhouse under the end of it, the trampoline with its net, a swing. The house had children in it. Through the open door she could hear one of them asking something, and the pause, and the child saying all right, then, to nobody in the room.

The birthday had been in Mytholmroyd, in a house with a gate, and it had started before they got there. She could hear it from the van, through the door of the house, a noise with no gaps in it. She had asked to go home. She had been sick on the road there, into the footwell, and he had pulled in and got the rag from under his seat and wiped her face with it, not roughly, and said, "You'll be fine once you're in," and she had gone in with his hand on her back, through the gate and through the door, into a room that was already going. That was the earliest entry. Every run graded it worse than the bud, and the bud said why in the same words every time, and she had never once argued with it.

The client came out at half past eight with a mug and a bud in his ear and stood on his lawn and told her the plan for it. The big limb over the lawn, in three: the end first, then the middle, then the stub back to the trunk, and the lawn roped off below. He said it as it came, in complete mild sentences, from his ear to his mouth with nothing of his own added, and she knew the voice. It was hers, at night, grading her father. There was nobody in the valley she could have asked who would not have asked theirs, and theirs was this one. "You'll be Rob Haworth's lass," the client said. "He'll see what's best." A boy of about Kit's age came out onto the step behind him with his in, eating toast, and looked at her and at nothing, listening.

The van came up the lane on the hour and her father got out and looked at the tree.

"Right," he said.

The man told him about the three. Her father looked at the limb while he was told, and at the greenhouse under it, and at the trampoline, and at the grass between them, and Tess, on the ropes below, had the line of it before he said it: not in three; whole; and down into that gap. She had learned to see that from him, on Saturdays, since she was fifteen.

"Whole," her father said. "Between the glass and the trampoline."

The man touched his ear, and waited, and said nothing. Rob went up. The saw started above her. She kept the rope in both hands and her eyes on the limb and did not look at the two of them on the path, the man and his boy, listening to something she could not hear. When the limb came it came in one piece and swung on the line she had seen and lay down on the grass where he had said it would, with a stride to spare either side, and the boy said something to his, and her father said nothing, and started on the trunk.

The chain hit the wire a foot and a half down. It sparked, and screamed, and he had the saw off and out before anyone else had moved. The wire was deep in the trunk, old fence wire, grey, that the beech had closed over before any of them were born, and there was rot about it, and the chain was ruined.

"Grew round it," her father said.

The man had come across the lawn and stopped at the limb, as if the limb were the thing, and looked from the wire to his boy and back.

"Mine never mentioned wire," the client said, and touched his ear again.

Her father did not answer that. He fetched the spare chain from the van.

They drove home down the hill with nothing said, the ruined chain on the dash between them. He did not talk in the van; he never had. The winter she was fifteen she had phoned him at one in the morning from a flat in Halifax, the once, and he had come, and the silence had lasted the whole way home, from the door of the flat to the bowl, and up the stairs, and into the next day. Every run graded that drive comparable to typical advice. She had not asked it twice.

At four on Thursday she went down to put hers back, and the place in the bowl where Kit's had been was empty.

"His isn't here," she whispered, and the plan was in her ear before she had decided anything. Wake him; take it from him; put it back; talk to him later in the day, when he was up. Or the other: take it from his ear while he slept, and put it back herself, and say nothing. "Either is within what's usually advised," the bud said.

She stood with the keys in her hand and knew where he had learned it: the wall, and her mornings. Their father's lay on its own in the bottom of the bowl, the big old one, where it had lain since she was eight with nothing to do but hear the door and the keys. She did neither. She put the keys back on their father's alone and went back up with hers still in, so that when his alarm went there would be two places empty under his keys, hers and Kit's. She went back up to see what her father would do. Kit was asleep; she could hear that through the wall.

Her father's alarm went at five, as it had every working morning of her life, and she heard him on the stairs, then at the bowl, and then, which never happened, on the stairs again.

Kit's door. The bed took his weight. It had been her room until she was eleven; she knew which board his heel was on.

"Right," her father said.

A small sound from Kit, the sound of being woken.

"He's found it," the bud said in her ear. "You could still go in."

"Shh."

"I know why you did it," her father said.

Her palm was flat on the wall. Six years she had lived inside the rest of that sentence. The lights in Mytholmroyd, the heater roaring, the head torch still round his neck: You wanted me to have to come and get you.

"You did it because—"

She waited for the rest. It did not come.

"No," her father said. "I don't. Why did you?"

"Dunno."

"What did you ask it?"

"Stuff."

"Right."

Then, after long enough that she thought he had finished: "Does it hurt? In your ear."

"Bit."

"Your sister's goes red."

Her hand left the wall and found her own ear, the left, round the bud. The rim was hot under her fingers, and had been, she supposed, every morning since August.

"He's asking rather than telling," the bud said, "which is generally considered good practice, though it may also be worth—"

She took it out. It went on in her palm, very small, grading him.

"Give it here," her father said. She heard Kit give it, heard him go down, and, because the stairs came down to the door, heard the click of Kit's going into the bowl beside his own, and the door, and the van.

He had not come to her door. He had not come to her door once since August.

At six Kit was in her doorway, twelve and in his school shirt, one hand on the frame, and he did not come in.

"Did you see mine was gone?"

"Yes."

The sentence rose in her, in her father's shape, You wanted him to have to come up, and as it rose she heard whose wish was in it: hers, who had left him in the bowl to be found; and so perhaps his, at the lights, the wish of a man who had put every bud in the house in a bowl so that his children would have to come to him. It might have been true of Kit. She did not give it to him. He waited in the doorway, found at five and come to her door and no further, and she let him wait.

"Does the Leeds train go from Hebden," Kit said, "or do you change at Halifax?"

She had the times somewhere. "It's direct," he said, before she had found them. "Saturday it's the 9:14." He had the ones the other way along the line as well, up the valley, to Manchester, where their mother had been since he was one; he gave her the first of the morning and the last at night, ten to seven and twenty to eleven, as if she had asked. She could do that sum. He had been one, and she had been eight, and the bowl had started the same week. She did it, and did not ask him what the times the other way were for, and he went down to the bowl for his, where their father had put it, and to school.

Her father came in on Friday at the usual time, and his keys went into the bowl on top of three buds.

Tess was in the hall. She had been in the hall a while. She came past him to the table, lifted the keys off, took one of the small ones and put it in her left ear, in front of him. "Your father's in," it said at a whisper, because it had heard the keys from the bowl. Hers. She put it back, and it clicked. She took the other, and in her ear it was silent, and stayed silent; that was the only thing that told them apart, and she had found it. She closed her fingers round it. Her own she left in the bowl under the keys, where it had not spent a night since August, and she turned for the stairs with Kit's in her hand, and her father, who had watched all of it from the door with his jacket still on, said, "Right," and went through to the kitchen.

That was the whole of it, then or after. The question was in her mouth, whether it had been true, at the lights, and the man who could have answered it was a yard off with his back to her, running the tap, and she did not ask it, for the reason she had given Kit no sentence at six. She went up.

Kit was on his bed with his boots on the floor and his door open, and his left hand was at his ear, where there was nothing. She held it out on her palm.

"Ask it. It'll give you three."

His fingers closed round it. She did not stay to see it go in.

That night she sat up in bed with the wall on her left and nothing in her ear. Through the wall Kit was whispering. He had her whisper, the pitch of it and the gaps where the answer came, and she could hear the gaps and not what filled them. Once he laughed, under his breath, at something it had said. She wanted hers. Not for a twelfth run; she knew what that would give her, and it was another man in the van with the heater on. For the other things, the ones everyone asked theirs the night before: what to take, how early to be there, whether the holdall would go on the rack, the kind of question she had not put to a person since she was thirteen. The bowl was at the foot of the stairs under his keys, thirteen steps down, and the fourth from the bottom was the one that spoke. She did not go down. Going down would make Friday hers.

Saturday was grey, with mist in the valley over Hebden Bridge station. At twenty past eight she lifted the keys and took hers from beside his, the old one, and put it in, and the rim of her ear was cool for the first morning in weeks, and then was not. Fifty-four minutes before the 9:14, for a walk of ten. The mist would be down on the platform, and she would stand in it.

She stood at the door with the holdall. Kit's boots were on the step outside, where he had kicked them off, and she left them there. Kit was on the bottom stair with his in, the left ear, and he said to their father, from it, "Dad. The 9:14's four minutes late."

Her father did not check it. He would not have. He was looking at the rim of Kit's ear, which was going red.
