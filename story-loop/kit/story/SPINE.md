# The spine

One page. Written after the premise is chosen, read by the human at G2, and changed freely until then.

**Point of view and tense:** Third person, close on Tess, past tense. Tess, because the story's evidence is what one person hears through a wall and what a machine tells her in a whisper, and because she is the one who is wrong about where she stands; Rob and Kit stay on the far side of the wall, heard and not entered. Close third rather than first, because first person would hand over the verdict she reached, and close third can withhold what she withholds (the sentence she does not give Kit; the question she does not put to Rob; the sum she does at six and tells nobody) without lying. Past tense because the passage, whose register the story reaches for, is in it. The narration notices as a night worker notices (which board, which ear, the keys apart from the buds), never explains the world, and never says which of the eleven fathers was real.

**Who:** Tess Haworth, nineteen, of Hebden Bridge; a year of nights as a care assistant in Halifax, every care plan followed to the letter, and early to every shift by most of an hour; on Saturday she takes the 9:14 to Leeds to train as a nurse, where she will be taught when not to. Her father, Rob, a tree surgeon, has kept every bud in the house in a bowl by the door for eleven years, since the week their mother went to Manchester (Kit one, Tess eight; one clause in the story, spent at six; no cause). In a valley where every other child has had the one voice in its ear since before school, the bowl made that house the one place a child could not check a word said to it. Tess remembers its first night as a hand: his, at her ear, at the door. She wants one bill she can believe before she goes: what the eleven years cost her. What she is wrong about: that the one practised on stands outside the practice as its judge. Her judgment was made in the house she is auditing; the harm and the gift are one thing and cannot be totalled apart; and she is a first-year at the bottom of her own curve, whose first departure from a plan is run on her brother and whose second is a decision made for him.

**Obstacle:** The audit holds still where the entry is plain and nowhere else. All eleven runs score the party worse than the bud and draw the same curve; at the lights, the entry she came for, run four graded him well, run nine badly, run eleven kinder than ten, because the entry holds a man who saw her and a man pleasing himself, and eleven fathers are what the lights permit. Every second opinion is the same bud in another ear, and nobody in the valley is not that voice but the man being graded. The only thing no rerun can vary is a live case, and the only live case in the house is Kit; to use Kit as the case is to do to him the thing she is costing.

**The turn:** Friday evening, at the bowl, in front of her father, Tess finds Kit's bud by trying the small ones in her own ear, puts hers back, and carries his upstairs, and Rob says "Right." and lets her; after that nothing can be said to Kit in that house that Kit cannot check, by his father's leave, and "Give it here" cannot be said again.

**Ending image:** Saturday, twenty past eight, from the door, one bag in her hand and her own bud in her ear, out of the bowl for the last time: Kit, bud in, telling Rob the 9:14 is four minutes late; Rob, not checking, because he would not, looking at the rim of Kit's ear, which has begun to go red. What it costs: the bill she came for, let go at the bowl, where the one person who could say whether it was true at the lights stood in front of her and she did not ask; the question leaves the house with her, and so does what Kit knows the other way along the line. The night before Leeds spent without the thing she has used every night since August: she wanted it, not for a twelfth run but for the small questions about tomorrow everyone asks theirs, and did not go down, because going down would have made Friday hers; and the girl who was sick in the van on the way to a party that had started without her leaves fifty-four minutes before the 9:14 for a walk of ten, the way that party made her. Rob has lost being the only one who knows his boy, taken by his daughter with one word between them. And she has given Kit the three she wanted at thirteen and cannot now have, the sentence she lives inside having been given her at the lights; whether three from a bud or one from a father is the better thing to be given, she will not be there to see.

**The question:** Was Rob entitled to learn, on Tess, to be the man who knew his children, or was that vanity charged to a child who could not be asked? And by Thursday, was Tess entitled to let Kit be the case, and by Friday to decide for him? The answer the story refuses: any total. The party (plainly wrong, and still in her at the door), the silence (plainly right, and unscoreable) and the lights (undecidable) do not sum, and being right at the lights would not pay for the years at the bottom of the curve; so nobody in the story says whether the eleven years were a gift or a charge, or Friday a rescue or a spending. The form of the refusal is the lights: an audit that agrees with itself about the party and the curve gives the lights a different man every night, because the entry has two in it.

**Length target:** About 3,500 words; the contest's bounds are 1,000 and 5,000.

## Beats

1. Monday, three in the morning, her week off, a night worker's clock. An hour after Rob goes up, hers from the bowl, from under his keys, on the boards that do not speak; in her room, the bud in her left ear, she whispers because of the wall, and on the far side of it Kit's bed takes his weight. She asks it to go again. Run eleven, forty-one entries, and first what every run agrees on: the party, worse than the bud; the curve, worse than the bud early and better late, her years bottom left and Kit's top right. Asked, the bud says any scoring of a learner draws that shape, that it cannot tell learning from luck or an easier second child, and that it may also be worth noting that the earliest entries follow a change in the household; she tells it to go on, and the omission is hers. Then the entry the runs will not hold still on: thirteen, out of Calder High at lunch and up Midgley Moor till dark, the head torch, the heater roaring in the van, and at the lights in Mytholmroyd the one time he told her what she was; run four graded him well there, run nine badly, tonight's is kinder than last night's; the bud offers three other reasons, and she cannot tell whose is hers. Since that night she has made herself someone nobody need fetch, bar once. At four, going down to put hers back, past Kit's in the bowl, one ordinary thing she has done for him since she was fourteen: his football boots off the step, knocked clean, stood under the hall table. The click. (No numbers on the first page; "forty-one" and "eleven" once each, on the second.)

2. Wednesday, a beech in a garden in Heptonstall. Tess is on the client's wall the better part of an hour before the van, as she has been everywhere before everyone since she was nine, and the party has a scene's weight and no verdict: a birthday in Mytholmroyd that had started before they got there; she asked to go home; sick in the van on the way; the rag from under the seat, "You'll be fine once you're in", and his hand on her back through the door into a room already going. The earliest entry, scored worse than the bud by every run, whose reasonable father turns the van round at the sick. The client, bud in, relays its felling plan (the big limb over the lawn, in three) in the voice that grades her father at night; she knows it for hers in another ear, and knows what it will miss. On the ropes below, she has the limb's line before Rob says it, because she learned to see it from him and the thing in the client's ear did not. Rob lays it whole between the greenhouse and the trampoline, and it goes there. Then his chain sparks on fence wire the tree closed over decades ago. "Grew round it." The client says his had not mentioned wire; Rob is looking at the cut. The van home, nothing said; in memory the other drive, fifteen, one in the morning, fetched from Halifax without a word the whole way, which every run scores comparable to typical advice. The beat ends on the grade against the drive, and no verdict.

3. Thursday, four in the morning, going down to put hers back before his alarm: Kit's place in the bowl is empty, and she knows where he learned it. Her bud gives the plan before she has decided anything (wake him, return it, talk to him later) and the other (put it back herself). She does neither, and goes back up with hers still in, so that at five two places are empty under his keys, hers and Kit's. Once, in her own words, the story says why she goes back up: to see what her father does; and that Kit is asleep. Nothing more. Five: the passage, whole and as written, to "He had not come to her door once since August", which now carries that he had two doors to choose from and chose Kit's.

4. Thursday, six, Kit in her doorway. Not the story's question; his: "Did you see mine was gone?" "Yes." The sentence rises in her unasked, in her father's form ("You wanted him to have to come up"), and as it rises she hears whose wish is in it: hers, who left him to be found to see what their father would do; and so perhaps his, at the lights, the wish of a man who put every bud in a bowl so that his children would have to come to him. It might be true of Kit; she does not give it, and the story does not say what it was at the lights. What Kit asks instead is whether the Leeds train goes from Hebden or you change at Halifax, and he knows before she does; he knows the ones the other way as well, to Manchester, where their mother has been since he was one: the mother's one clause, spent here. Tess can do that sum, and does, in that clause, and does not ask him what for; the manuscript does not return to it.

5. Friday evening, Rob home; his keys go into the bowl on top of three buds, the old large one and two small ones alike. In front of him she puts one of the small ones in her left ear, and it speaks, so it is hers; she puts it back and takes the other, silent in her ear, Kit's, and leaves hers in the bowl: the rule kept for herself by her own hand, the first night since August, and broken for Kit in front of the man who kept it. Rob has watched all of it. "Right," he says, and goes through; that is the whole of what is said, then or after; he learns nothing he has not known since August, and his own bud does not move. The question is in her mouth (was it true, at the lights) and she does not ask it, for the reason she gave Kit nothing at six. Kit's room: "Ask it. It'll give you three." Kit's fingers close on it; she does not stay to see it go in. That night, through the wall, Kit whispers in the whisper he learned from her, and she cannot hear what it says back. She wants hers: not for a twelfth run, which would be a twelfth father, but for the small questions about tomorrow everyone asks theirs; and she does not go down, because going down would make Friday hers. The night before Leeds is spent without it. That is the price; "no run twelve" is not.

6. Saturday, twenty past eight, at the bowl for hers, and at the door with the bag: the last image, as written above, and nothing after it.

## What this story must not become

The nearest bad version is the one where the list gets read: Tess sets the audit before Rob, he defends himself or breaks, and they reconcile or part. Nothing from the audit is ever said to Rob. Its neighbours:

- The atrophy story, and its mirror, the noble refuser. The bud is not always right (the wire, the drive) and not a fool; Rob's judgment is real (the beech) and was once plainly wrong (the party). Nobody is dumb because of the machine or wise for refusing it.
- The faulty instrument. Where the entry is plain, all eleven runs agree; the eleven fathers are what the lights permit. An audit a reader could average is the wrong audit.
- The 2025 swap. A bud silent in the wrong ear is a lock, and a phone has one; it tells the buds apart and nothing more. What only 2040 does here is the client's bud being hers in another ear with nobody else to ask, the plan arriving at four before she has decided, and the bud grading her father through the wall as he speaks. A draft the swap survives has leaned on the lock.
- The other-lives story. No run is a life she could have had; each grades the same life, and she envies none.
- The kindness reading. Thursday's plain sentence guards Thursday; Friday is guarded by being done in front of him and by its price: the night before Leeds without the bud, wanting it, and the question only Rob could answer, let go. "No run twelve" is not the price and is never played as the sacrifice; and Friday is not the moment Rob learns anything, since he has known since August, and "Right." is a man letting his daughter do a thing, not a man seeing the light.
- The lecture. "Practised on", "raised or", "consent", "judgment", "vanity", "counsel" and "model" do not appear. The bud may say "good practice"; nobody says what the story is about. Nor is there a verdict on the drive: "It was the silence" is not said, nor anything like it; the grade is set against the drive and the reader does the rest.
- The bud as a character. It never wants, apologises or asks to be worn; it hedges, offers three, and grades. Kit's bud in her ear is silent, and nobody remarks on it.
- The stated metaphor. "Grew round it" is said once, by Rob, of the wire. Nobody says a child grows round a father. The curve is one image, once; the only percentage-shaped phrase is "comparable to typical advice".
- The mother story. One clause, at six, no scene, no cause. Kit's trains are a trace, never a plot: Tess does the sum once, tells nobody, and nobody says Kit will go anywhere.
- The house style of the five outlines: no cut from a hard line to an ambient sound; no "said it the way [a job] reads [a thing]"; no "did not remember" a small act of the hand; no "looked at the window, where"; no ending on a verdict withheld (a pen held over a line).
- Kit as a device. He is twelve, says "Stuff", "Bit" and "Dunno", asks about her and about trains, and never asks the story's question.
