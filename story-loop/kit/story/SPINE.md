# The spine

One page. Written after the premise is chosen, read by the human at G2, and changed freely until then.

**Point of view and tense:** Third person, close on Tess, past tense. Tess, because the story's evidence is what one person hears through a wall and what a machine tells her in a whisper, and because she is the one who is wrong about where she stands; Rob and Kit stay on the far side of the wall, heard and not entered. Close third rather than first, because first person would hand over the verdict she reached, and close third can withhold what she withholds (the sentence she does not give Kit; the question she does not put to Rob) without lying. Past tense because the passage, whose register the story reaches for, is in it. The narration notices as a night worker notices (which board, which ear, the keys apart from the buds), never explains the world, and never says which of the eleven fathers was real.

**Who:** Tess Haworth, nineteen, of Hebden Bridge; a year of nights as a care assistant in Halifax, every care plan followed to the letter, and early to every shift by most of an hour; on Saturday she takes the 9:14 to Leeds to train as a nurse, where she will be taught when not to. Her father, Rob, a tree surgeon, has kept every bud in the house in a bowl by the door for eleven years, since the week their mother went to Manchester (Kit one, Tess eight; one clause in the story, no cause). Tess remembers the bowl's first night as a hand: his, at her ear, at the door. She wants one bill she can believe before she goes: what the eleven years cost her. What she is wrong about: that the one practised on stands outside the practice as its judge. Her judgment was made in the house she is auditing; the harm and the gift are one thing and cannot be totalled apart; and she is a first-year at the bottom of her own curve, whose first departure from a plan is run on her brother and whose second is a decision made for him.

**Obstacle:** The audit will not hold still: eleven runs, eleven reasonable fathers, and the gap between runs wider than any entry. Every second opinion is the same bud in another ear. The only thing no rerun can vary is a live case, and the only live case in the house is Kit; to use Kit as the case is to do to him the thing she is costing.

**The turn:** Friday evening, at the bowl, in front of her father, Tess finds Kit's bud by trying the small ones in her own ear, puts hers back, and carries his upstairs, and Rob says "Right." and lets her; after that Tess cannot again be the only one in that house who asks, and Rob cannot again be a man who did not know.

**Ending image:** Saturday, twenty past eight, from the door, one bag in her hand and her own bud in her ear, out of the bowl for the last time: Kit, bud in, telling Rob the 9:14 is four minutes late; Rob, not checking, because he would not, looking at the rim of Kit's ear, which has begun to go red. What it costs: the bill she came for, since the last night she could have run it went to Kit; she leaves the way the party made her, fifty minutes early for a walk of ten, and the reader has seen why; Rob has lost being the only one who knows his boy, taken by his daughter with one word between them; Kit has the three and will never be told what he was, the one thing she has wanted since thirteen; and the question she let go at the bowl leaves the house with her.

**The question:** Was Rob entitled to learn, on Tess, to be the man who knew his children, or was that vanity charged to a child who could not be asked? And by Thursday, was Tess entitled to let Kit be the case, and by Friday to decide for him? The answer the story refuses: any total. The party (plainly wrong, and still in her at the door), the silence (plainly right, and unscoreable) and the lights (undecidable) do not sum, and being right at the lights would not pay for the years at the bottom of the curve; so nobody in the story says whether the eleven years were a gift or a charge, or Friday a rescue or a spending. The audit's disagreement with itself is the form of the refusal.

**Length target:** About 3,500 words; the contest's bounds are 1,000 and 5,000.

## Beats

1. Monday, three in the morning, her week off, a night worker's clock. An hour after Rob goes up, hers from the bowl, on the boards that do not speak; in her room, the bud in her left ear, she whispers because of the wall, and on the far side of it Kit's bed takes his weight. She asks it to go again. What it says of her father is what a reasonable man would say, kinder than last night's; the distance between the two nights is wider than any entry. Then the entry itself: thirteen, out of Calder High at lunch and up Midgley Moor till dark, the head torch, the heater roaring in the van, and at the lights in Mytholmroyd the one time he told her what she was. The bud offers three other reasons. She cannot tell whose is hers. Since that night she has made herself someone nobody need fetch. Last, the one thing all eleven runs agree on: the curve, worse than the bud early and better late, her years bottom left and Kit's top right; asked, the bud says any scoring of a learner draws that shape, and it cannot tell learning from luck or an easier second child. (No numbers on the first page; "forty-one" and "eleven" once each, on the second.)

2. Wednesday, a beech in a garden in Heptonstall. Tess is on the client's wall before the van arrives, as she has been everywhere before everyone since she was nine: the party that had started without her, "You'll be fine once you're in", sick in the van on the way; the earliest entry, and every run scores it worse than the bud would have done. The client, bud in, relays its felling plan (the big limb over the lawn, in three). On the ropes below, Tess has the limb's line before Rob says it, because she learned to see it from him and the thing in the client's ear did not. Rob lays it whole between the greenhouse and the trampoline, and it goes there. Then his chain sparks on fence wire the tree closed over decades ago. "Grew round it." The client says his had not mentioned wire; Rob is looking at the cut. The van home, nothing said; in memory the other drive, fifteen, one in the morning, fetched from Halifax without a word the whole way, which run eleven scored comparable to typical advice. It was the silence.

3. Thursday, four in the morning, going down to put hers back before his alarm: Kit's place in the bowl is empty, and she knows where he learned it. Her bud gives the plan (wake him, return it, talk to him later) and the other (put it back herself). She does neither. Once, in her own words, the story says why she goes back up: to see what her father does; and that Kit is asleep. Nothing more. Five: the passage, whole and as written, to "He had not come to her door once since August."

4. Thursday, six, Kit in her doorway. Not the story's question; his: "Did you see mine was gone?" "Yes." The sentence rises in her unasked, in her father's form ("You wanted him to have to come up"), and might be true, and she does not give it. What Kit asks instead is whether the Leeds train goes from Hebden or you change at Halifax, and he knows before she does. (Kit's own thing, never given: what he asked in the night was the trains to Manchester. The reader gets only that he knows the times.)

5. Friday evening, Rob home; his keys go into the bowl on top of three buds, the old large one and two small ones alike. In front of him she takes one of the small ones and puts it in her left ear, and it speaks, so it is hers; she puts it back and takes the other, which is silent in her ear, Kit's, and leaves hers in the bowl. Rob has watched all of it. "Right," he says, and goes through; that is the whole of what is said, then or after. The question is in her mouth (was it true, at the lights) and she does not ask it, for the reason she gave Kit nothing at six. Kit's room: "Ask it. It'll give you three." Kit's fingers close on it; she does not stay to see it go in. That night, the first since August, hers is in the bowl and there is no run twelve; through the wall Kit whispers, in the whisper he learned from her, and she cannot hear what it says back.

6. Saturday, twenty past eight, at the bowl for hers, and at the door with the bag: the last image, as written above, and nothing after it.

## What this story must not become

The nearest bad version is the one where the list gets read: Tess sets the audit before Rob, he defends himself or breaks, and they reconcile or part. Nothing from the audit is ever said to Rob. Its neighbours:

- The atrophy story, and its mirror, the noble refuser. The bud is not always right (the wire, the silence) and not a fool; Rob's judgment is real (the beech) and was once plainly wrong (the party). Nobody is dumb because of the machine or wise for refusing it.
- The other-lives story. No run is a life she could have had; each grades the same life, and she envies none.
- The kindness reading. Thursday's plain sentence guards Thursday; Friday is guarded by its price (hers back in the bowl, the last night given away) and by being done in front of him. Without either, Friday is a sister being nice.
- The lecture. "Practised on", "raised or", "consent", "judgment", "vanity", "counsel" and "model" do not appear. The bud may say "good practice"; nobody says what the story is about.
- The bud as a character. It never wants, apologises or asks to be worn; it hedges, offers three, and grades. Kit's bud in her ear is silent, and nobody remarks on it.
- The stated metaphor. "Grew round it" is said once, by Rob, of the wire. Nobody says a child grows round a father. The curve is one image, once; the only percentage-shaped phrase is "comparable to typical advice".
- The mother story. One clause, no scene, no cause. Kit's trains are a trace, never a plot.
- The house style of the five outlines: no cut from a hard line to an ambient sound; no "said it the way [a job] reads [a thing]"; no "did not remember" a small act of the hand; no "looked at the window, where"; no ending on a verdict withheld (a pen held over a line).
- Kit as a device. He is twelve, says "Stuff", "Bit" and "Dunno", asks about her and about trains, and never asks the story's question.
