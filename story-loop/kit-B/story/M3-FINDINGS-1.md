### From REV-09 (6 findings carried)

REV-09 F1: MINOR — Noor's "I can't tell you" (line 58) is the one line where a person does the story's work instead of her own.
   - The narration has given her words for what she heard: "the heart came back a fraction late ... as if there had been a gap before it the width of a breath held" (line 18), and "like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called" (line 132). She never uses them with anyone.
   - To Marga and to her counsel, the silence can be defended: she wants an unprimed second listener, and she knows the counsel will translate "late" into "No deceleration".
   - To Sanne it cannot. Sanne is at eight centimetres, about to be driven over a five-kilometre bridge against her own counsel's advice. Any midwife would tell her she does not like how the heart is coming back, which is true and which no counsel can refute.
   - The line carries weight. It is what makes Sanne's consent rest on "Marga's hands in the bag", and what lets the review say the record holds only "the student's wanting it". A reader who does not believe the line will feel the consent, and the later grievance, become shaky.
   - The draft half-covers it. Sanne's counsel has just said "I recommend staying" (line 54), and Noor may be refusing to give it anything to answer. But nothing shows her deciding that, so the line reads as what the record needs rather than what she would say.
   - Two ways out: make the silence visibly her choice against the counsels at every collar, or let her say the true thing and let the review turn it into wanting anyway. The second would sharpen the idea, not lose it.

REV-09 F2: MINOR — Noor's decision (lines 40–44) is briefed rather than weighed, and the briefing is the wrong way round: it explains what the story explains again later, and holds back the one fact the decision turns on.
   - "She thought of the misses, and of her score on them, which was everyone's. The simulator had nothing in it to overrule" (line 42) cannot be understood until Friday, four scenes later: "births a counsel had heard nothing wrong in and something was ... she had sat those in 2038 and scored at chance, like everyone" (line 96).
   - So on a first reading "Let me" is simple conviction. Only on a second reading is it what it really is: a bet against her own ear, which has been measured and found to be at chance.
   - Meanwhile the paragraph spends its weight on things said again elsewhere. "The simulator had nothing in it to overrule" here repeats "it could not write a heart it could not itself hear" at line 96. The registry here repeats the review's "The record holds what was said, done and wanted" at line 86.
   - It also holds the one sentence where the narration, not a character, passes judgement on the system: "It measured agreement and nothing else". Its second half, "six in nine years were too few to measure anything else by", is the fair version and makes the point on its own.
   - The thoughts that belong to Noor at 02:13 are the stranger who will sign her off in January and the March cord "twice round the neck and tight". They would carry the paragraph if the misses were plain in it.

REV-09 F3: MINOR — The dike is set up and never used.
   - Sanne "was a dike inspector for the water board and had wanted this at home" (line 14), and the house is "under the dike" (lines 6, 114).
   - She is the one person in the story whose work is finding a fault before it shows. The story's question is whether to act on a small sign nobody can prove, against a computed risk, and that is the question her trade and her province live by.
   - It bears on nothing: not her "All right", not the complaint. In the last scene it survives only as a faint echo in "Where in it", for a reader who makes the link alone.
   - Her final act is believable without it, since "a training decision" is reason enough. But this is the readiest way to make her listening come from her own life rather than from the story's need to hand the question on, and to set the question inside something the reader already knows.

REV-09 F4: NIT — The knot settles nothing only for a reader who knows that a loose true knot can pull tight as the baby descends. That knowledge is what makes "True knot. Loose. Look" (line 76) the exact image of the story's question: a danger that was real and had not yet happened. A reader who does not know it takes the review's "a loose true knot is not evidence that transfer was needed" (line 84) as the last word, and Noor's photograph reads as her clutching at straws. For that reader the question tips a little toward "nothing there". A few words from the obstetrician would restore the balance without deciding it.

REV-09 F5: NIT — Sanne sends the complaint and calls for Noor on the same morning (lines 108, 118). That order is the one the ending needs, because it guarantees the listening can change nothing on the record. The dialogue lets the reader supply a reason ("It was very fair", then "It says I was probably a training decision", then "What did you hear?"). But Joost's "She's had it since Wednesday. She hasn't played it" tells us she waited a week, not what changed on the eighth day. Most readers will not stop here; a careful one will see the timing as the story's rather than hers.

REV-09 F6: NIT — "had last overruled his counsel in 2029, and said so at birthdays, as a good thing, which it was" (line 92) has two readings. Either the 2029 overruling is the good thing, or the years since without one are. This is the one sentence where the narration gives its own verdict on overruling. Either reading serves the story, but the reader should be able to tell which is meant.


### From REV-10 (10 findings carried)

REV-10 F1: MINOR — The load-bearing sentence is the story's thesis put in the counsel's mouth. The review says: "The record holds what was said, done and wanted. It does not hold what was heard, and I cannot report a hearing that left no trace in it."

Everywhere else the counsel speaks in a flat first person: "I find no indication for transfer", "I recommend", "This is consistent with a training decision". The first half of this sentence, with its list of three and its turn against it, is the story's own voice. It hands the reader the frame of the last scene one section before Sanne asks her question.

The substance has to stay: it is the only thing on the page that tells Sanne there is something to ask Noor for. Cut the first half and keep the second: "What the record supports is the student's wanting it and the supervisor's letting it stand. I cannot report a hearing that left no trace in the record." The reader can assemble "said, done and wanted" from three departures already timed to the second. Change the wording, not the reason.

REV-10 F2: MINOR — At the decision point, the reason behind "Let me" comes behind a block of biography, in shorthand the reader cannot yet decode. The explanation arrives three sections later.

Between "I didn't hear it" and "Let me", the landing gives:
- Marga's CV in one sentence: "sixty-five, trained from 1998 on women who had no counsel, this practice hers since 2002, retiring on the thirty-first of January with Noor her last";
- then two sentences the reader can only half read: "She thought of the misses, and of her score on them, which was everyone's. The simulator had nothing in it to overrule."

Friday defines the misses, in a paragraph that explains two tests and reads as the story answering an objection. The March cord just before "Let me" keeps the balance, so the reader does not misread it. But the reason is told twice: once where it is needed and cannot be read, and once where it can be read and is no longer needed.

Two ways out:
- Give the landing the definition in a clause, and cut Friday to the foam pad and "it could not write a heart it could not itself hear".
- Or keep Friday, and cut the landing to the sentence the reader can read.

Either way, move the CV off the landing. The opening's chair, where Marga has sat since ten, can hold most of it.

REV-10 F3: MINOR — The last paragraph is built of refrains:
- "and rose when it rose", the second time in three paragraphs;
- "the bed on its blocks", the third time in the story;
- "at the height Noor would have worked at", the second time in the scene;
- "where the heart came back", to close.

The next-to-last sentence, "Sanne lay on the bed on its blocks, at the height Noor would have worked at, listening.", is there for the reversal: the payer at working height, doing the listening. That is worth landing. But the phrase was already given twenty lines earlier, with Noor's idle hands, so the second time reads as the story pointing at its title. Use the phrase once, at the end, and let the earlier line carry the stitches and the hands alone. "Her other hand stayed on her daughter's back" keeps the hand in view without the second "rose when it rose".

REV-10 F4: MINOR — The clock is told three times, and the review's sentence about Marga is quoted twice within a page and a half:
- The landing: "whoever came after, who had never seen her at a bed and would sign her off or not from the record".
- The board: "Twelve weeks. Marga had ten."
- The complaint: the review's sentence again, "word for word", then "Marga's successor started in January and had never seen Noor at a bed; the sign-off was a stranger's now".

The board's two short sentences are the best telling, because they leave the arithmetic to the reader; the complaint paragraph then does it for them. Keep "word for word", which shows Sanne sent her counsel's draft unedited, but don't quote the sentence a second time, and cut "the sign-off was a stranger's now".

The dates point the same way. By the story's own arithmetic, the complaint arrives on the eighth day, a Tuesday, and twelve weeks on is 12 February. So the board's Thursday is eleven weeks before 31 January, and the complaint's Tuesday is ten. "Twelve weeks. Marga had ten." is wrong where it stands and right in the complaint paragraph. Leave the board with its rule, and move the line.

REV-10 F5: MINOR — The same two-sentence close ends two consecutive sections: "Nobody said anything about the knot. Marga said nothing at all." and then "Nobody said what was in it. Marga said nothing about the night." Add "Neither of them said anything else" and "had said perhaps a dozen words", and the narration keeps pointing at Marga's silence until the silence becomes the narration's comment rather than her act. The second pair is also odd straight after the narration has said what was in the box. Keep the Goes pair, where the silence follows her one look at her figure, and end the box scene on "Noor carried it to the car."

REV-10 F6: NIT — The remainder of REV-07 F2. The review reads Noor's seven departures against harm, one by one, and prices the section in fifths. A counsel that thorough would say how departures like this one go across the country, even if the answer is "too few to tell". It says nothing, and the registry's "nothing else" answers only for the registry. One clause in the review keeps the balance.

REV-10 F7: NIT — "The review had given it a paragraph." The quoted review gives the knot one sentence, the last of its first paragraph, so one of the story's best lines is contradicted by the quotation three pages earlier. Give the knot's sentence its own paragraph in the review, and the line's irony becomes exact.

REV-10 F8: NIT — "Noor counted on Sanne's face" is heard first as the idiom, "relied on", until "toward the place" corrects it. It is the third double sense in three versions of the last sentence, and the only one that carries the story's meaning: she is relying on the payer's face to hear for her. If it is chosen, keep it. If not, "Noor counted, on Sanne's face, toward the place" removes it.

REV-10 F9: NIT — "with the drip in her arm, and the contractions slowed and then stopped". A stalled labour arriving at Goes gets oxytocin, and contractions stopping on oxytocin will stop a midwife reader. If the drip is fluids, nothing needs to change. If it is oxytocin, the usual course is that the contractions come back on the drip and she stays at eight centimetres.

REV-10 F10: NIT — "The house was under the dike on the dike side of Zierikzee" reads as a stammer in the story's second sentence. One "dike" is enough.
