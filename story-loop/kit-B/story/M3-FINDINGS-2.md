### From REV-12 (5 findings carried)

REV-12 F1: MINOR — The explaining goes to the familiar part of the idea, and the newest part is only summarized. That no test can find an ear is said three times:
- on the landing: "played them to every midwife in the country, to find the ears. It had found none ... The simulator, which was the counsel's, had nothing in it to overrule";
- in the Friday simulator: "The simulator could not write a heart the counsel could not hear. It knew no other. The misses were the other test, and she had sat those";
- by Sanne's counsel: "better than chance, midwives included".

The third is needed, because Sanne hears it before she listens. The second only repeats. Meanwhile the story's newest thought is that the perception is not refuted but worn away by a fair voice's patience. It is shown once, on the first night ("I find no gap." / "She played it again."). After that it is compressed into "By Friday she heard it one time in five. Since Sunday she had not heard it at all ... and the patience was worse." Friday is the middle of that wearing-away, yet the Friday scene spends its words explaining the simulator again. A reader will notice the repetition. What they never see is the repetition that does the damage, the same patient answer given again, and that is the thing they would have carried away.

REV-12 F2: MINOR — The one place a person does what the plot needs is Marga's "Your call." Everything the text has shown points the other way:
- Of her six departures from her counsel, the two the text shows both start with her own ear and her one word, "Goes".
- She has just said "I didn't hear it".
- Both counsels say stay.
- She is the one the review says "could have overruled the transfer and did not."

The text gives her a look "for the length of a contraction coming on and going off", her arms folded, and Noor's memory of March. Later a reader can read backwards: she packs the bag and "did not look up", checks her figure after the knot, hands Noor a box instead of taking the phone. But at the moment itself the look holds nothing the reader can see, and afterwards Marga never says a word about the night. Readers will accept the decision because the plot turns on it. To believe it, they need one thing in that minute, seen or done, that shows what she is weighing.

REV-12 F3: MINOR — The trap the night rests on reads as arranged, not found. This is about earning the idea, not disagreeing with it.

"There was nowhere left to learn whether an ear was real except a woman at eight centimetres" follows from a single test, and that test used only recordings: the misses were "mixed ... blind" and "played ... to every midwife in the country". Yet this is a world where every collar logs every word ("the record scored it as agreement"; "Both were logged at fourteen minutes past two"). Nine years of births already hold every worry a midwife spoke aloud, every "Marga. The next one. Listen.", each beside how that birth ended. The academy gathered exactly the births in which "a counsel had heard nothing wrong and something had been wrong". Whether a midwife spoke up in those births before the harm is one search away. A reader who knows how such questions get studied will ask why nobody looked, and the text does not say.

For Noor's own ear the trap would still hold, because one midwife never sees enough misses ("six departures in nine years were too few to make anything else of"). But the text claims it for ears in general ("to find the ears. It had found none"). One clause on why the live record cannot answer either would make the trap the world's.

REV-12 F4: NIT — The word choice leans where the facts don't. "Fair" comes four times ("fairly", "It was fair", "very fair", "fairly"), and "patient", "patiently" and "patience" three times, all of them about the counsel or its work. Noor's "It was fair" and Sanne's "It was very fair" are concessions and carry the motif honestly. By the last one, "Sanne's counsel told her, fairly, low, pitched for her", the word lands as an accusation. The question needs the counsel to be the honest party, and the refrain makes it slightly less so.

REV-12 F5: NIT — On the bridge the counsel goes quiet at the one point where speaking would have foretold the cost: "About the five minutes it said nothing. Nothing had yet happened that it could speak to." This is a voice that speaks unasked on the landing ("spoke before she asked it anything") and on the bridge ("about one in thirty"), and that measures intervals to the second ("I have measured the interval from the end of each contraction"). Contractions spacing out to three, four and five minutes is something that has happened, and it is what ends in the section at Goes. The silence keeps the machine from being seen to predict the stall, which slightly shelters Noor's side of the question.


### From REV-13 (7 findings carried)

REV-13 F1: MINOR — The thesis that REV-10 F1 cut from the review now stands in the narration at the decision point, where it explains the story's plainest line. This is what sits between "Why?" and "I can't tell you":

"Noor had the words for it. The heart was coming back late; she had heard it twice. The words were true, and she could say them. The clip at Sanne's collar ... would answer them before she had finished, in its three sentences, fairly, and Sanne would have her own counsel on one side and a student on the other. What was not said, nothing at any collar could answer."

Steelman: this is the world's sharpest consequence, and it belongs in the story. Where every reason is answered at every collar, judgment can only be exercised unsaid. The paragraph also makes the last scene's "What did you hear?" the refused "Why?" asked again.

It has three costs.
- **The aphorism states what two scenes show.** "What was not said, nothing at any collar could answer." is the claim REV-10 F1 took out of the counsel's mouth ("It does not hold what was heard"). It now comes in the story's own voice, a section earlier. The replay has already shown what saying it buys: "I find no gap." The last scene then shows the claim and corrects it: Sanne's collar answers the unsaid from the record ("Four minutes and fifty-one seconds in ... I find nothing there"), and she listens anyway.
- **"I can't tell you" is told as a tactic, and as an untruth.** The reader could take the line as inability, as refusal, or as an ear's knowledge that will not go into words. Now it is told as a tactic, and "she could say them" makes it untrue. That also re-reads Noor's "No" on the landing a minute earlier. A reader who knows consent will notice a student withholding her reason from a woman in labour so that the woman's counsel cannot answer it. The story may want that cost on the scale, but the reader can find it without the narration's help.
- **"The words were true" grades Noor's side.** It is the one place the narration grades a claim on her side, while its grading of the record has thinned to "It was fair." Read as the narration's word, it settles the fact the replay keeps open.

Fix: keep "Noor had the words for it. The heart was coming back late; she had heard it twice." and the clip sentence, which is Noor reasoning in concrete terms. Cut "The words were true, and she could say them." and the aphorism. The reader will hear "I can't tell you" both ways, and the last scene does the rest.

REV-13 F2: MINOR — The last paragraph now explains its own staging and its own verb.
- **The clip sentence.** "The heart went into Sanne's ear and not into the room." is followed by "Noor heard nothing of it; the clip at Sanne's collar did." Its first half repeats what the sentence before has just shown. Its second half is the scene's sharpest new fact, the counsel inside the last listening, delivered as a pointed antithesis.
- **The count sentence.** "Four hundred plays had put the count in her, and she did not need the number." tells the reader how to read "counted" before they reach it. It repeats "four hundred" from the hinge paragraph, which has already put the count in her.

The REV-10 F3 refrains are gone, and two sentences of commentary have taken their place. They are the only two sentences in the scene that tell the reader what to take from it. The wobble in "counted on" that REV-10 F8 named was the smaller flaw.

Fix: fold the clip into what is seen ("The heart went into Sanne's ear and into the clip at her collar, and not into the room."), and cut the count sentence. "Toward the place" already steers the idiom.

REV-13 F3: MINOR — The REV-10 F2 fix gave the misses a paragraph where I asked for a clause, and it changed what "Let me" follows. January, March, June and the registry come first, so about 260 words of thought now stand between "I didn't hear it" and "Let me".

The order has changed too:
- The March cord, which REV-10 credited with keeping the balance next to "Let me", is now three sentences and a paragraph away from it.
- The last sentence before "Let me" is the spine's Obstacle in narration: "There was nowhere left to learn whether an ear was real except a woman at eight centimetres, and the woman was behind the door."

That gives Noor's motive at the decision point as "to learn whether an ear was real". It is the "to find out" that route (a) of REV-07 F1 turned into "to become". This is MINOR, not a reopening of that MAJOR, because the review still reaches its verdict without the hearing. But the page now invites the reader to judge "Let me" as an experiment on a woman.

Friday also repeats the landing's last fact: "The misses were the other test, and she had sat those."

Fix, either way:
- keep the definition, "It had found none" and the two scores, then end on "The simulator, which was the counsel's, had nothing in it to overrule. The woman was behind the door.";
- or move the misses ahead of March, so the cord stands next to "Let me" again.

In both cases, cut Friday's last sentence.

REV-13 F4: NIT — "'Yes.' Sanne had the pitch of it exactly: the landing's, through a door pulled to."
- In draft-01 the landing's pitch belonged to Noor's "Yes". Here it is Sanne's, and "through a door pulled to" puts her ear at the door at 02:14.
- That is the best answer REV-07 F4 has had: Sanne, too, holds a hearing the record keeps only as words.
- No disposition row names the change.
- The paragraph sets Noor's "Yes." in front of a sentence about Sanne, so the speaker wobbles for a beat. "'Yes,' Noor said." settles it.

REV-13 F5: NIT — On the landing, "Whoever came after Marga would be a stranger who ... would sign her off, or not, from the record" is stated as certain. Only a complaint makes it true: the board later puts her sign-off on or before 12 December, while Marga is still there. Make it her fear ("might"). Then "Marga had ten" closes on a risk she saw coming, not on a fact she already had.

REV-13 F6: NIT — "It cut her eight minutes. In the record they were 02:06 to 02:13." By the clock that is seven minutes. Write "seven".

REV-13 F7: NIT — The first night is told twice.
- The replay scene shows it: four "late"s, and "the foot, the stair".
- The hinge paragraph then opens on it again, with a second simile: "The first night she had heard it after every contraction: ... like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called."

This is the shape of REV-05 F3 in one clause, at the place the story can least afford a pause. The simile is one of the story's best lines. Either start the hinge at "By Friday" and give the simile to the replay, or accept the clause as the simile's price.
