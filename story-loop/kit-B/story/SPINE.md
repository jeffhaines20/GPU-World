# The spine

One page. Written after the premise is chosen, read by the human at G2, and changed freely until then.

**Title:** Working Height

**Point of view and tense:** Third person limited to Noor, past tense, close enough to hear the doppler with her and no closer: it reports what she hears, sees and does, and quotes the record (the counsel's sentences, the review) as text, never as essay. Why: the story turns on a sound only she heard, and the question is whether it was there. First person makes that a question about her honesty; a wider third, or one line inside Sanne's head, settles it. Close third lets the reader hear it with her at ten past two and lose it with her by day eight, and keeps Sanne opaque, which is what makes "Again" a test and not a pardon. Past tense because the story is a record played back, and it is about what drains out of a replay.

**Who:** Noor Brouwer, 24, last month of her midwifery training in Zierikzee. Her concordance, 97.1, is the lowest in Zeeland; she checks it as often as a runner checks her pace. Her father pilots ships up the Westerschelde and has not overruled his counsel since 2029; he says so at birthdays, as a good thing, and it is. She wants to be a midwife who knows, not one who relays. What she is wrong about: she thinks the departure is a bet on her own hearing, staked with her own number, that an outcome would settle. Both halves are wrong. The stake was Sanne's, and the review prices it to the decimal; and nothing that could happen, a knot, a scar, a well child, could tell anyone, herself included, whether she heard anything. By day eight she has learned the first half; the second she cannot learn, because a week of replay has taught her only that whatever she heard was not in the sound. Sanne is wrong about one thing too: that the review told her what happened to her.

**Obstacle:** The counsel is right far more often than she is. The only place to learn whether she is ever right where it is not is a live birth: the simulator is the counsel's and plays only hearts the counsel can hear; Marga's sense will not go into words; a woman at eight centimetres, asked to be the test, would say stay. Afterwards the record reads wanting, never hearing, so the one act that might have been judgment looks, on paper, exactly like the thing it might also have been.

**The turn:** Sanne, who sent the complaint that morning and holds the price to the decimal, asks not whether Noor was right but what she heard, and after the recording says "Again", and after that the review's clean account cannot be the whole of her daughter's birth again: she has begun spending the one thing the night bought her, a verdict, on the one thing it cannot hold.

**Ending image:** Sanne on the bed still on its blocks, at a midwife's working height, her daughter asleep on her chest, Noor's phone at her ear. Her own counsel, asked, has given her the second to listen at and told her, fairly, that nothing is there. She listens anyway. Noor stands beside the bed and watches her listen; she cannot hear this play. What it costs: Sanne gives up the clean account. She was probably a training decision, three in five, and could have left it there; now she has a scar, a hospital for every future birth, and the same doubt Noor has, with no one to send it to. Noor gets no pardon: the complaint stands, her sign-off is deferred past Marga into a stranger's reading of her record, she cannot hear it any more, and the thing she spent a stranger's birth to find out is being listened for by the stranger, out of her hearing. The reader is refused the one thing they want, which is for someone to hear it. The story ends before the play does.

**The question:** May an apprentice spend a stranger's birth to find out whether her hearing is worth anything, when that is how every midwife who ever outheard the counsel was made, and when the stranger can audit what it cost her and why the apprentice wanted it, but never what she heard? The answer refused: whether Noor heard anything, and so whether the night was judgment or vanity. The knot is loose and settles nothing; the recording holds a heart; the counsel finds nothing each time; Marga did not hear it; Sanne does not say what she hears. Both sides stay strong: the counsel was right about all it could see and a woman paid; a hearing past 2026 is the only way past the ceiling, and the one who paid asks for it. The story answers Gawande by taking away his two supports in its first four beats (necessity, since a surgeon as good as 2026's is on every collar; the vague bill, since the review prices it) and testing in the last two what is left: not whether the lesson was needed, but whether the one who paid for it can take it up. It never argues this; the review does the taking away in its own fair voice, and Sanne does the testing with one word.

**Length target:** About 3,500 words; hard bounds 1,000 and 5,000. The passage (about 330 words) sits inside beat 6 and is the register; the night is written at the same temperature.

## Beats

1. **The bedroom under the dike, Zierikzee, night of 12 to 13 November 2040.** Sanne on the bed on its hired blocks, at working height; Joost at her head; Marga in the chair by the door; Noor with the doppler, her counsel at her collar agreeing with everything, her figure at the edge of her eye like a pace. First baby, eight centimetres, all normal. At 02:10 Noor hears the heart come back after a contraction a fraction late, like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called. The counsel, through the same doppler, hears nothing wrong. Marga listens through the next contraction and shakes her head.
2. **The landing, 02:14.** The counsel, low and fair, in four sentences: no indication; transfer risks a birth on the bridge; listen through three more; can she say what she heard? "No." Marga: "I didn't hear it." Noor thinks of January, and of the two of Marga's 0.4 she was at, a cord twice round the neck and nothing. "Let me." "Your call." All on the record; none of it names the want. She tells them. Their counsels say what hers says. "Why?" "I can't tell you." Sanne agrees because a professional is afraid.
3. **The Zeelandbrug, 02:40, and Goes.** Five kilometres of white lamps over the black Oosterschelde; Sanne on all fours on the stretcher; the counsel updating the odds of a birth on the bridge; Noor's figure, checked once, at 96.9 since 02:14. There is no birth. The contractions space out: the danger she chose passes and another begins. At Goes labour stops at eight centimetres; section at 07:40. The obstetrician lifts the cord: a true knot, loose. Noor photographs it. Apgar nine and ten. Marga: "Keep that."
4. **The practice, day two.** The review, written by Sanne's counsel to Sanne as "you" and sent to the practice as notice; Noor reads it in that form. Probably avoidable, three in five; every future birth in hospital; the knot "not evidence that transfer was needed"; "Let me" and "Your call" quoted with their times; ten past two reanalysed, no late deceleration; her seven departures this year, all small; "consistent with a training decision". A complaint drafted, not sent. The reader rereads the night. Day three, the board through her own counsel: no live births until it is settled; the simulator for her hours; sign-off deferred. Marga retires in January; a review can take eight weeks. Her father sends a heart and Goes's section statistics, which are good.
5. **The practice's back room, Friday, and the nights.** Her counsel plays her a birth: its doppler, its contractions, its woman. She listens for anything to overrule; the counsel has never made a heart it could not hear. She scores a hundred. Marga, packing the practice into boxes, will not listen to ten past two and says why in five words or fewer. Every night Noor plays it: the first night after every contraction, by Friday one time in five, from Sunday not at all. She stops asking the counsel, because its answer is the same each time, patiently, and the patience is worse.
6. **The house under the dike, day eight.** Sanne sent the complaint that morning and asked the practice for Noor the same morning; Marga said she need not go. The bed still on its blocks, for Joost's reason. The passage as written, to "Again." Then Sanne asks her own counsel for the second; it gives it and says, fairly, that nothing is there. She takes the phone and puts it at her ear. Noor watches her listen. End there.

## What this story must not become

- The obvious version: the midwife overrules the AI and is proved right or wrong. The knot stays loose; no one, Noor included, says it justified the transfer; she does not offer the photograph. The replay goes the way of *Blow-Up*: certainty drains, nothing is found, no second sound, no number, no twist in the file.
- Bohjalian: a trial. The complaint is administrative and exact; no lawyers, no hearing, no verdict scene, no registration at stake. The harm is a scar priced at three in five and a hospital for the next birth.
- The pardon. "Again" is not forgiveness. Sanne does not withdraw the complaint, does not say Noor was right, does not say what she hears, does not touch her; nothing in beat 6 is warmer than the passage. Her listening could still end with the complaint standing and nothing in the file.
- The sibling confrontation: Sanne never asks whether Noor knows or was right. Her questions are "What did you hear?" and "Again", and the second is not a question.
- AI makes us dumb. The counsel is not the antagonist and has no will: fair, patient, right about all it can see, blind only to what did not happen; never more than four sentences at a time, never a lie, never a hedge to seem wise, nothing a good 2026 model would not say. Nobody is stupider for it, and the father's kind of good is real.
- A lecture. No one says Gawande's argument or "practising on people"; Noor does not think it as an essay; the review is quoted, not explained; Marga explains nothing, and the story does not translate her.
- The father as foil (no call, no birthday scene); sentiment about the baby (she sleeps, unnamed, and no one understands anything by looking at her).
- The batch's reflexes, which a critic will search for by name: the cut from a hard line to an ambient sound (furnace, radiator, fridge, rain ticking); "said it the way [a job reads things]" (Sanne reads no one like a gauge); "did not remember" a small act of the hand; "looked at the window, where". None of these; the passage's "as it had been said" is used once and not generalised.
- A verdict withheld as a pose. The ending is an act, a woman listening at her counsel's second against her counsel's finding, not a pen held over a line. The last sentence belongs to Noor watching, not to what Sanne hears.
