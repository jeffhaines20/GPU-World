# The spine

One page. Written after the premise is chosen, read by the human at G2, and changed freely until then.

**Title:** Working Height

**Point of view and tense:** Third person limited to Noor, past tense, close enough to hear the doppler with her and no closer; the record quoted as text, never as essay. The story turns on a sound only she heard: first person makes that a question about her honesty; a wider third, or one line inside Sanne's head, settles it. Close third lets the reader hear it with her at ten past two and lose it by day eight, and keeps Sanne opaque, which makes "Again" a test and not a pardon. Past tense because the story is a record played back.

**Who:** Noor Brouwer, 24, last month of her midwifery training in Zierikzee; concordance 97.1, the lowest in Zeeland. She wants to be a midwife who knows, not one who relays. What she is wrong about: that the departure is a bet on her own hearing, staked with her own number, that an outcome would settle. The stake was Sanne's, and Marga's, and the review prices it; and nothing that could happen could tell anyone whether she heard anything. The misses told her that in 2038 and she did not believe it of her own ear. Marga Dekker, 65, trained from 1998 on women who had no counsel: six departures in nine years, one a cord twice round the neck, tight, which the counsel had not heard, five women moved for nothing; at chance on the misses like everyone; retires in January. Sanne, first baby, said "All right" at 02:16 against her own counsel, has had the recording since Wednesday and has not played it, and sent the complaint on day eight. She is wrong about one thing: that the review told her what happened to her.

**Obstacle:** The counsel is right far more often than she is. The simulator is the counsel's and plays only hearts the counsel can hear. The misses are the other test, and everyone has failed it: the registry's recordings of births a counsel heard nothing wrong in, played blind to every midwife since 2033, and everyone scores at chance, Marga included; so whatever a midwife's ear is, no recording has yet held it. Marga's sense will not go into words. A woman at eight centimetres, asked to be the test, would say stay. So the only place left is a live birth, and afterwards the record reads wanting, never hearing, so the one act that might have been judgment looks, on paper, exactly like the thing it might also have been.

**The turn:** Sanne, who sent the complaint that morning and has its price in the review's own words, asks not whether Noor was right but what she heard, and after the recording says "Again", and after that the review's fair account cannot be the whole of her daughter's birth again: she has begun spending the one thing the night bought her, a verdict, on the one thing it cannot hold.

**Ending image:** Sanne on the bed still on its blocks, her daughter asleep on her chest, Noor's phone at her ear. Her own counsel, asked, has given her the second to listen at and told her, fairly, that nothing is there. She listens anyway. Noor stands beside the bed and counts to the second on Sanne's face. What it costs Sanne: the clean account, had since Wednesday with the recording unplayed beside it, sent this morning. Listening at her counsel's second against its finding is her second departure from it, made knowing what the first cost; if anything is at the second, or if she wants there to be, the account she sent is not the whole of her daughter's birth. Noor gets no pardon: the complaint stands, her sign-off is deferred past Marga into a stranger's reading of her record, she cannot hear it any more, and what she wants, watching, is what the review read off the record: to hear it, through Sanne now, which is spending her again. The last sentence has an object, the second, and no verb of hearing.

**The question:** May an apprentice spend a stranger's birth to find out whether her hearing is worth anything, when that is how every midwife who ever outheard the counsel was made, and when the stranger can audit what it cost her and why the apprentice wanted it, but never what she heard? The answer refused: whether Noor heard anything, and so whether the night was judgment or vanity; the misses are either why the recording is useless or why the ear is a story. Against: the counsel was right about all it could hear; a woman paid, and so did Marga's number; everyone scores at chance and Noor knew it at 02:14; the want is unreformed in the last line. For: Marga's cord was real; nothing recorded can test an ear, so a live birth is the only way past the ceiling; the one who paid asks for it and listens against her own counsel's finding. Gawande's two supports, necessity and the vague bill, go in the first four beats; the last two test whether the one who paid for the lesson can take it up, and nobody argues it.

**Length target:** About 3,500 words; hard bounds 1,000 and 5,000. The passage (about 330 words) sits inside beat 6 and is the register.

## Beats

1. **The bedroom under the dike, Zierikzee, night of 12 to 13 November 2040.** Sanne on the bed on its hired blocks, at working height; Joost at her head; Marga in the chair by the door; Noor with the doppler, her counsel at her collar agreeing with everything. First baby, eight centimetres, all normal. Once, Marga says "Left side" a breath before the counsel does: her ear at work, scored as agreement. At 02:10 Noor hears the heart come back after a contraction a fraction late, like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called. The counsel, through the same doppler, hears nothing wrong. Marga listens through the next contraction and shakes her head.
2. **The landing, 02:14.** The counsel, in four sentences: no indication; transfer risks a birth on the bridge; listen through three more; can she say what she heard? "No." Marga: "I didn't hear it." Noor thinks of January, and of the two of Marga's six she was at: 2039, one word from Marga, and at Goes the cord twice round and tight, unheard by the counsel; June, the same word, and nothing. "Let me." "Your call." Both are logged at 02:14, Noor's eighth this year and Marga's seventh in nine; both figures move. January is the reason each has and neither says: after it, no one who has seen Noor at a bed will be there to let her or stop her. None of it names the want. She tells them. Their counsels say what hers says. "Why?" "I can't tell you." "All right." Sanne agrees because a professional is afraid.
3. **The Zeelandbrug, 02:40, and Goes.** Five kilometres of white lamps over the black Oosterschelde; Sanne on all fours on the stretcher; the counsel updating the odds of a birth on the bridge; Noor's figure, checked once, at 96.9. There is no birth. The contractions space out: the danger she chose passes and another begins. At Goes labour stops at eight centimetres; section at 07:40. The obstetrician lifts the cord and says what it is: a true knot, loose. Noor photographs it. Apgar nine and ten. Marga, who never looks at her figure, takes out her phone at 07:50 and looks, and Noor sees her do it. Nobody says anything about the knot.
4. **The practice, day two.** The review, written by Sanne's counsel to Sanne as "you": probably avoidable, about three in five; the knot "not evidence that transfer was needed"; "Let me" and "Your call" quoted with 02:14, "All right" with 02:16; the recording reanalysed, four contractions, no late deceleration in any; Noor Brouwer's seven departures this year; Marga Dekker's seventh in nine, with the fair sentence that she could have overruled and did not, and that a complaint naming her cannot conclude before 31 January; "consistent with a training decision". A complaint drafted, not sent. Day three, the board through her own counsel: no live births until the complaint is settled; the simulator for her hours; sign-off deferred; a complaint can take eight weeks, and Marga retires in ten. Her father sends a heart and Goes's section statistics, which are good.
5. **The practice's back room, Friday.** Her counsel plays her a birth. She listens for anything to overrule and scores a hundred. The other test she sat in 2038: the misses, blind, and she scored at chance, like everyone, like Marga. Marga is packing the practice into boxes. Noor holds out the phone with ten past two on it. Marga does not take it; she puts a box in Noor's hands instead: the practice's birth books, handwritten, one a year from 2002 to 2030. Nobody says what is in the box, and Marga says nothing about the night. Every night Noor plays ten past two; the passage tells what happens to it, once, and this beat does not.
6. **The house under the dike, day eight.** Sanne sent the complaint that morning and asked the practice for Noor the same morning; Marga said she need not go; Noor goes because it is the room where she heard it. Joost at the door, one line: Sanne has had it since Wednesday and has not played it. The bed still on its blocks. The passage as written, to "Again." The second play. Then Sanne asks her own counsel for the second; it gives it and says, fairly, that nothing is there. She takes Noor's phone and puts it at her ear. Noor knows the second and counts to it, watching Sanne's face. End there, before the play does.

## What this story must not become

- The obvious version: the midwife overrules the AI and is proved right or wrong. The knot stays loose; no one says it justified the transfer; the photograph is not offered or praised. The replay finds nothing: no second sound, no twist in the file. The misses are a fact, not a scene: no one retakes them, no one finds an ear on them, no one explains why everyone scores at chance.
- Bohjalian: a trial. The complaint is administrative and exact; no lawyers, no hearing, no verdict scene, no registration at stake. The board's process is "the complaint", never "the review"; "three in five" prices the section, never the motive, and only Sanne merges them, never the narration.
- The pardon, or growth. "Again" is not forgiveness. Sanne does not withdraw the complaint, say Noor was right, say what she hears, touch her, or ask whether Noor knows; her questions are "What did you hear?" and "Again", and the second is not a question. Nothing in beat 6 is warmer than the passage. Noor's want at the end is the want the review read; she does not learn to want Sanne not to hear it, no gesture says she has, and the story does not punish the want either.
- AI makes us dumb. The counsel is not the antagonist and has no will: fair, patient, right about all it could hear, blind only to what left no trace, nothing a good 2026 model would not say. Nobody is stupider for it.
- A lecture. No one says Gawande's argument or "practising on people"; Noor does not think it as an essay; the review is quoted, not explained; Marga explains nothing and says nothing about the night after "Your call"; the box is not read, by her or the narration. The father is not a foil (one message, no scene); the baby is not sentiment (she sleeps, unnamed).
- The batch's reflexes, which a critic will search for by name: the cut from a hard line to an ambient sound (furnace, radiator, fridge, rain ticking); "said it the way [a job reads things]"; "did not remember" a small act of the hand; "looked at the window, where". None; "as it had been said" is used once. "Gallop" is the passage's word and appears nowhere else.
- A verdict withheld as a pose. The ending is an act, a woman listening at her counsel's second against its finding. The last sentence belongs to Noor, has an object, the second, and no verb of hearing.
