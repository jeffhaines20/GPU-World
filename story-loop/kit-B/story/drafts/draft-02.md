The bed was on its blocks. Four of them, grey plastic, hired from the home-care shop, each a hand's length high, so that Sanne lay at working height and a midwife could stand to her without stooping. The house stood under the dike at Zierikzee. The bedroom was upstairs; outside its door was a landing, and from the landing a stair led down into the dark of the house. Joost, whose child this was, sat at Sanne's head. Marga, whose practice it was, sat in the chair by the door with the practice's bag on the floor beside it, and had sat there since ten. Noor, who was Marga's student, had the doppler.

She held it in her right hand, with her left hand flat on Sanne's belly, and after each contraction she found the heart and listened. The flat clip at the collar of her tunic listened with her, through the same instrument, and told her, low, what it found. "Baseline one-forty. Variability normal. No deceleration." Her counsel had said that, or something a word off it, every two minutes since midnight. Her phone lay face up on the bedside table. It was her counsel's screen, and at the edge of it stood her concordance, 97.1: of every decision of hers that had been scored since her placements started, that many in a hundred had gone with her counsel's advice. It was the lowest figure in Zeeland. She did not look at it. She knew it.

Sanne came up out of a contraction with her forehead on Joost's forearm. "Is it all right?"

"It's as it should be," Noor said.

Sanne inspected dikes for the water board: she walked them for a living and marked the places where the grass was wrong, or the ground wet that should have been dry. She had wanted this at home, under hers, and not at Goes, which was the hospital, on the far side of the Zeelandbrug. At two o'clock Noor examined her, on her counsel's word and Marga's nod, and found eight centimetres, and said so, and Sanne said something to Joost that was not for the room. The contractions were two minutes apart and lasted a minute. Outside it was dry and cold and still.

Between two contractions Sanne pushed herself over onto her back. Marga said from the chair, "Left side," and a breath later Noor's counsel said the same at Noor's collar, and the record scored it as agreement. Sanne turned onto her left side.

Marga had trained in 1998, on women who had no counsel, and the practice had been hers since 2002. She was sixty-five. There had been figures for nine years, and hers over the nine was 99.6, and Noor had never once seen her look at it. She would retire on the thirty-first of January. Noor was her last student.

The contraction at ten past two was like the others. Sanne went down into it and came up out of it, and Noor set the doppler where the heart was and listened. There was the hiss of the instrument, and Sanne's breath, and then the heart, at its rate, a fraction late. Not slow. The rate was the rate. But between the contraction letting go and the heart coming up out of it there had been a gap the width of a breath held, like a foot in the dark that finds the stair a moment after it reaches for it, and there had been no gap after any of the others.

"Baseline one-forty," her counsel said. "Variability normal. No deceleration."

"Marga," Noor said. "The next one. Listen."

Marga got up. She did not take the doppler. She bent her head beside Noor's hand, close to the small speaker, and waited with her. At twelve minutes past two the contraction came, and Sanne's breath dragged under it, and the heart thinned. The contraction let go. The heart came back, and it came back late again, by the same width, in the same place: the foot, the stair. Marga listened to it with her eyes on the wall above the bed. She straightened, and shook her head.

"Baseline one-forty," the counsel said. "Variability normal. No deceleration."

"Landing," Noor said.

* * *

Marga pulled the door to behind them, not shut. The landing had a bare bulb, and Sanne's yellow jacket from the water board on a hook, and the stair going down. Noor's counsel spoke before she asked it anything.

"I find no indication for transfer. Transfer at eight centimetres in a first labour carries a risk of birth on the bridge, under one in twenty. I recommend listening through three more contractions and reassessing. Can you say what you heard?"

"No," Noor said.

"I didn't hear it," Marga said.

She stood with her arms folded under the bulb. Noor thought of January. Whoever came after Marga would be a stranger who had never seen Noor at a bed, and would sign her off, or not, from the record. She thought of Goes in March of last year: a bed on the far side of the island, Marga listening through one contraction and saying one word, "Goes", and the ambulance over the bridge against what her counsel advised; and in the theatre at Goes the cord twice round the neck and tight. Marga's counsel had heard nothing wrong in that heart either. She thought of June, this year: the same word from Marga, the same bridge, and at Goes nothing wrong at all. The registry, which published the figures, had logged both as departures, and six departures in nine years were too few to make anything else of.

And she thought of the misses. The academy had gathered the births in which a counsel had heard nothing wrong and something had been wrong, with what came of each, and had mixed them blind with births where nothing had been wrong, and had played them to every midwife in the country, to find the ears. It had found none. Marga had sat them and scored at chance; so had Noor, in her second year. Everyone had. The simulator, which was the counsel's, had nothing in it to overrule. There was nowhere left to learn whether an ear was real except a woman at eight centimetres, and the woman was behind the door.

"Let me," Noor said.

Marga looked at her for the length of a contraction coming on and going off in the room behind the door.

"Your call."

Both were logged at fourteen minutes past two. Noor's figure moved to 96.9. Marga's moved to 99.5: "Your call" had let stand what her own counsel advised against, and the record counted that as hers.

Noor went back in. "I want to take you to Goes," she said. "Now. In the ambulance."

Joost looked first to Marga. Marga had the bag open on the chair and was packing it, the gloves, the gel, the things she had laid out at ten, and she did not stop and did not look up. There was a counsel on every collar in that house. In the quiet after the contraction Noor heard Sanne's counsel say, low, "I find no reason to move you. I recommend staying. There is a small risk of birth on the bridge." Joost's counsel said the same at his collar.

"Why?" Sanne said.

Noor had the words for it. The heart was coming back late; she had heard it twice. The words were true, and she could say them. The clip at Sanne's collar had heard the same four contractions through the same room, and it would answer them before she had finished, in its three sentences, fairly, and Sanne would have her own counsel on one side and a student on the other. What was not said, nothing at any collar could answer.

"I can't tell you."

Sanne looked at Noor, and then past her at Marga's hands in the bag.

"All right."

Joost called the ambulance. It came in a quarter of an hour. Marga followed in her own car. Joost rode in the front; Noor rode in the back with Sanne, who was on all fours on the stretcher because that was how she could bear it.

* * *

The bridge was five kilometres of white lamps over the black Oosterschelde. "Birth on the bridge," Noor's counsel said as the ambulance came onto it, "about one in thirty." A contraction later it said, "One in fifty." Then, "One in a hundred." The contractions were spacing out: three minutes, then four. After each one Noor found the heart and listened, and whether it came back late she could not have said, with the engine under it. The counsel said its three sentences, as it had in the room.

"Is it still all right?" Sanne said, into the stretcher.

"Yes."

Noor looked at her figure once, with the lamps going over the ceiling: 96.9. By the far end of the bridge the contractions were five minutes apart. The counsel had stopped giving odds on the bridge; there was no longer anything on it to price. About the five minutes it said nothing. Nothing had yet happened that it could speak to.

At Goes the labour stopped at eight centimetres. They put up a drip, and the contractions came back on it, slack and far apart, and the eight centimetres stayed eight. The hospital listened with its own doppler and its own counsels and found what Noor's counsel had found all night. Marga sat in the corridor with her coat on and came in when she was asked. The small hours passed like that. At seven the obstetrician came and said what he would do and why, and Sanne said yes before he had finished.

The section was at twenty to eight. Noor stood where she was put, by Sanne's head, with Marga beside her. The child came up out of Sanne grey and then not grey, a girl, and they scored her at one minute and at five, nine and then ten: a child with nothing wrong. Then the obstetrician lifted the cord in his gloved hands. "True knot," he said. "Loose. Look." He held it up to the light for them, a single overhand, slack, the cord slumped through it. "They pull tight coming down. Or they don't."

"May I photograph that?" Noor said. He said she might, and she did, on her phone, the knot loose across his glove. Marga took her own phone out of her pocket and looked at her figure, once, and put it back, and Noor saw her do it. Nobody said anything about the knot. Marga said nothing at all.

* * *

Noor slept through the day and woke in the dark in her room, still in her tunic. She asked her counsel for the night. "From six past two," she said. "Until I went out to the landing."

It cut her eight minutes. In the record they were 02:06 to 02:13. She called them ten past two.

She lay on the bed with the phone on her chest and played them. The room came first: Sanne's breath, Joost's, the hiss of the doppler, the creak of Marga's chair. Then the heart, small in the phone, going at its pace. The first contraction built under it, and the heart thinned, and the contraction passed, and the heart came back late.

She had not heard that at the bed. At six past two she had been finding the heart and listening and hearing nothing, with her counsel saying its three sentences at her collar. She played the first contraction again. Late. The second, which at the bed had been nothing to her: late. The third, the one she had heard: late, the foot, the stair. The fourth, with Marga's breath beside the speaker: late.

"Listen with me," she said to the clip.

She played the four through again and it listened with her, from the same collar, through the same small speaker, and said nothing, because she had asked it nothing. After the fourth she asked.

"Baseline one-forty throughout," it said. "Variability normal. No deceleration in any of the four. Can you say what you heard?"

"The return," Noor said. "After each one. It's late. There's a gap before it."

"I have measured the interval from the end of each contraction to the recovery of the baseline. The four are within a second of one another and within the range I expect. I find no gap."

She played it again.

* * *

The review reached the practice on Wednesday morning. It was Sanne's: her counsel had written it for her, when she asked it to read her care back to her, and a copy had come to the practice, which counted as notice. Noor read it at the table in the front room with her coat still on. It said "you".

"Your section was probably avoidable, about three in five. Your labour was transferred at 02:14 against the recommendation of both midwives' counsels. The student midwife, Noor Brouwer, said 'Let me'; the supervising midwife said 'Your call'. Asked why, the student midwife said 'I can't tell you'. You said 'All right' at 02:16, against my advice. The doppler audio from 02:06 to 02:13 has been reanalysed: four contractions, no late deceleration in any."

"The knot photographed at 07:50 was loose. A loose true knot is not evidence that transfer was needed."

It listed Noor's seven earlier departures this year, with what each had been: which side to lie on, twice; when to examine, three times; whether to call the maternity nurse yet, twice. None, it said, had been followed by harm, or by any difference the record could show. Transfers against a counsel's advice at eight centimetres were too few in the registry, it said, for it to say how they went.

"The supervising midwife could have overruled the transfer and did not. This was her seventh departure since scoring started. A complaint naming Marga Dekker cannot conclude before her retirement on 31 January and would reach nobody."

"What the record supports is the student's wanting it and the supervisor's letting it stand. I cannot report a hearing that left no trace in the record. This is consistent with a training decision."

It recommended that any future birth be planned in hospital. A complaint had been drafted from it and had not been sent.

Noor read it twice. It was fair.

On Thursday the training board spoke to her through her own counsel, which was patient about it. "Your live cases are suspended on notice. Your remaining hours go to the simulator. Your sign-off is deferred until a complaint is settled, or until 12 December if none is received. A complaint, once received, runs twelve weeks."

Noor's father piloted ships up the Westerschelde. He had overruled his counsel once, in 2029, and been right, and he told it at birthdays. He had not had to since. That evening he sent her one message: a heart, and the section statistics for Goes, which were good.

* * *

On Friday the simulator ran in the back room, on her counsel, with the doppler paired. It wrote her a birth and voiced the heart through the doppler against a foam pad, and she listened through two dozen contractions for anything to overrule, and found nothing, and scored a hundred, as everyone did. The simulator could not write a heart the counsel could not hear. It knew no other. The misses were the other test, and she had sat those.

Marga was in the front room, packing the practice into boxes for January. Noor took the phone through to her and held it out. Ten past two was on it.

Marga looked at it. She did not take it. She lifted a box from the floor, cardboard, not light, and put it into Noor's hands instead: the birth books, handwritten, one a year, 2002 to 2030.

"The car," she said.

Noor carried it to the car.

* * *

The complaint came on Tuesday morning, the eighth day. It named the departure at 02:14 with both midwives quoted, "Let me" and "Your call", and it carried the review's sentence on Marga Dekker word for word. Twelve weeks from its receipt was the twelfth of February. Marga had ten. The same morning Sanne asked for Noor to come.

Marga read the complaint standing. "You needn't go," she said.

Noor went, because it was the room where she had heard it.

Joost opened the door of the house under the dike. "She's had the recording since Wednesday," he said. "She hasn't played it."

The bed was still on its blocks. Joost said it was easier for the stitches, getting up. Noor stood beside it with nothing to do with her hands.

"I sent it this morning," Sanne said. "Mine drafted it. It was very fair."

"I read it."

"'Let me.'" Sanne said it quietly, as it had been said. "Fourteen minutes past two. That's you. You asked her."

"Yes." Sanne had the pitch of it exactly: the landing's, through a door pulled to.

"It says I was probably a training decision."

The photograph of the knot was on Noor's phone too, behind the recording. The review had given it a paragraph. Noor did not offer it.

"What did you hear?"

The baby slept face down on Sanne's chest, one fist under her cheek. Noor had listened to ten past two perhaps four hundred times. The first night she had heard it after every contraction: the heart coming back a fraction late, like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called. By Friday she heard it one time in five. Since Sunday she had not heard it at all, and she had stopped asking the counsel, because it gave the same answer each time, patiently, and the patience was worse.

She set the phone on the duvet and pressed play.

The room filled with a heart, fast and soft, a horse going hard on sand. Then Sanne's own breath from that night, lower, dragging, the contraction building under it; the gallop slowing; the gallop coming back.

Noor heard a heart.

Sanne did not look at the phone. She kept her eyes on her daughter's head, and her hand lay flat on the small back and rose when it rose.

It ended. Noor reached for the phone.

"Again," Sanne said.

Noor's hand stayed on the phone, on the duvet. Sanne was not looking at the phone or at Noor. She said, to the clip at the collar of her cardigan, "Where in it. Where she heard it."

Sanne's counsel told her, fairly, low, pitched for her, and Noor beside the bed heard it. "Four minutes and fifty-one seconds in, after the third contraction, where the heart comes back. I find nothing there. No one hears such a thing on a recording better than chance, midwives included."

Sanne held out her hand without turning her head. Noor put the phone in it. Sanne took it to her ear, and her other hand stayed on her daughter's back. The heart went into Sanne's ear and not into the room. Noor heard nothing of it; the clip at Sanne's collar did. Four hundred plays had put the count in her, and she did not need the number. Sanne lay at the height Noor would have worked at, listening. Noor counted on Sanne's face toward the place where the heart came back.
