The bed was on its blocks. Four of them, grey plastic, hired from the home-care shop, each a hand's length, so that Sanne lay at working height and Noor could stand to her without stooping. The house was under the dike on the dike side of Zierikzee, the bedroom upstairs, a landing outside its door. Joost sat at Sanne's head. Marga sat in the chair by the door with the practice's bag beside it, and had sat there since ten, and had said perhaps a dozen words.

Noor had the doppler in her right hand and her left hand flat on Sanne's belly, and after each contraction she found the heart and listened, and her counsel listened with her through the same instrument and told her, low, at the collar of her tunic, what it found. "Baseline one-forty. Variability normal. No deceleration." It had said that, or something a word off it, every two minutes since midnight. Her phone lay face up on the bedside table, and at the edge of the counsel's display was her concordance, 97.1, the lowest in Zeeland. She did not look at it. She knew it.

Sanne came up out of a contraction with her forehead against Joost's forearm. "Is it all right?"

"It's as it should be," Noor said.

Sanne was a dike inspector for the water board and had wanted this at home. At two o'clock Noor examined her, on the counsel's word and Marga's nod, and found eight centimetres, and said so, and Sanne said something to Joost that was not for the room. The contractions were two minutes apart and lasted a minute. Outside it was dry and cold and still.

Between two of them Sanne pushed herself over onto her back, and Marga said from the chair, "Left side," and just after her Noor's counsel said the same at Noor's collar, and the record scored it as agreement, which it was. Sanne turned onto her left side. Marga's concordance was 99.6 over nine years, and Noor had never once seen her look at it.

The contraction at ten past two was like the others. Sanne went down into it and came up out of it, and Noor set the probe where the heart was and listened. There was the hiss of the probe, and Sanne's breath, and then the heart; and the heart came back a fraction late. Not slow. The rate was the rate. Late, as if there had been a gap before it the width of a breath held, where none of the others had had one.

"Baseline one-forty," her counsel said. "Variability normal. No deceleration."

"Marga," Noor said. "The next one. Listen."

Marga got up. She did not take the doppler. She bent her head down beside Noor's hand, close to the small speaker, and waited with her, and at twelve minutes past two the contraction came, and Sanne's breath dragged under it, and the heart thinned, and the contraction let go, and the heart came back, and it came back late again. Noor heard it. The same gap, in the same place. Marga listened to it with her eyes on the wall above the bed, and straightened, and shook her head.

"Baseline one-forty," the counsel said. "Variability normal. No deceleration."

"Landing," Noor said.

* * *

Marga pulled the door to behind them, not shut. The landing had a bare bulb and a stair going down into the dark of the house. Noor's counsel spoke before she asked it anything.

"I find no indication for transfer. At eight centimetres in a first labour it carries a risk of birth on the bridge, under one in twenty. I recommend listening through three more contractions and reassessing. Can you say what you heard?"

"No," Noor said.

"I didn't hear it," Marga said.

She stood with her arms folded under the bulb, sixty-five, trained from 1998 on women who had no counsel, this practice hers since 2002, retiring on the thirty-first of January with Noor her last. Noor thought of January: of whoever came after, who had never seen her at a bed and would sign her off or not from the record. She thought of Goes, March of last year, this same woman at a bed on the far side of the island saying one word after one contraction, "Goes," and the road over the bridge against her counsel's advice, and at Goes the cord twice round the neck and tight, which the counsel had not heard. And of June, the same word, the same bridge, and nothing. The registry had logged both as departures. It measured agreement and nothing else, and six in nine years were too few to measure anything else by.

She thought of the misses, and of her score on them, which was everyone's. The simulator had nothing in it to overrule.

"Let me," Noor said.

Marga looked at her for the length of a contraction coming on and going off in the room behind the door.

"Your call."

Both were logged at fourteen minutes past two, to the second: Noor's eighth departure this year and Marga's seventh in nine. Noor's figure moved to 96.9. Marga's moved to 99.5, because "Your call" let stand what her own counsel had advised against. Neither of them said anything else.

Noor went back in. "I want to take you to Goes," she said. "Now. In the ambulance."

Joost looked first to Marga. Marga had the bag open on the chair and was packing it, the gloves, the gel, the things she had laid out at ten, and she did not stop and did not look up. There was a counsel on every collar in that house, and in the quiet after the contraction Noor heard Sanne's say, low, "I find no reason to move you. I recommend staying. There is a small risk of birth on the bridge." Joost's said the same at his.

"Why?" Sanne said.

"I can't tell you."

Sanne looked at Noor, and then past her at Marga's hands in the bag.

"All right."

The record took that too, at sixteen minutes past two. Joost called the ambulance at nineteen minutes past. It came in a quarter of an hour. Marga followed in her own car, and Joost rode in the front, and Noor rode in the back with Sanne, who was on all fours on the stretcher because that was how she could bear it.

* * *

The Zeelandbrug was five kilometres of white lamps over the black Oosterschelde, and they were on it at twenty to three. "Birth on the bridge," Noor's counsel said, "about one in thirty." A contraction later it said, "One in fifty." Then, "One in a hundred." The contractions were spacing out: three minutes, then four. After each one Noor found the heart and listened, and whether it came back late she could not have said, with the engine under it; the counsel said its three sentences, as it had in the room.

"Is it still all right?" Sanne said, into the stretcher.

"Yes."

Noor looked at her figure once, with the lamps going over: 96.9. By the far end of the bridge the contractions were five minutes apart and the counsel had stopped pricing the bridge, because there was nothing on it to price, and it had said nothing yet about what five minutes apart at eight centimetres in a first labour might mean, because nothing yet was the case.

At Goes labour stopped at eight centimetres. Sanne lay in the delivery room on her side with the drip in her arm, and the contractions slowed and then stopped, and the hospital listened with its own doppler and its own counsels and found what Noor's had found all night, and Marga sat in the corridor with her coat on and came in when she was asked. At seven the obstetrician came and said what he would do and why, and Sanne said yes to him before he had finished. The section was at 07:40. Noor stood where she was put, by Sanne's head, with Marga beside her, and the obstetrician lifted the cord in his gloved hands and said, "True knot. Loose. Look," and held it up to the light for them: a single overhand, slack, the cord slumped through it.

"May I photograph that?" Noor said. He said she might. At 07:50 she did, on her phone, the knot loose across his glove. Marga took her own phone out of her pocket and looked at her figure, once, and put it back, and Noor saw her do it. A girl, nine at one minute and ten at five. Nobody said anything about the knot. Marga said nothing at all.

* * *

The review reached the practice on Wednesday morning, as notice, and Noor read it at the table in the front room with her coat still on. Sanne's counsel had written it to Sanne. It said "you".

"Your section was probably avoidable, about three in five. Your labour was transferred at 02:14 against the recommendation of both midwives' counsels; the student midwife, Noor Brouwer, said 'Let me', and the supervising midwife said 'Your call'. You said 'All right' at 02:16, against my advice. The doppler audio from 02:06 to 02:13 has been reanalysed: four contractions, no late deceleration in any. The knot photographed at 07:50 was loose, and a loose true knot is not evidence that transfer was needed."

It listed Noor's seven earlier departures this year with what each was: which side to lie on, twice; when to examine, three times; whether to call the maternity nurse yet, twice. None, it said, was followed by harm, or by any difference the record could show. It said that the supervising midwife could have overruled the transfer and did not, and that this was her seventh departure since scoring began, and then: "A complaint naming Marga Dekker cannot conclude before her retirement on 31 January and would reach nobody." It said: "What the record supports is the student's wanting it and the supervisor's letting it stand. The record holds what was said, done and wanted. It does not hold what was heard, and I cannot report a hearing that left no trace in it. This is consistent with a training decision." It recommended that any future birth be planned in hospital. A complaint had been drafted from it and had not been sent.

Noor read it twice. It was fair.

On Thursday the board spoke to her through her own counsel, which was patient about it. "Your live cases are suspended on notice. Your remaining hours go to the simulator. Your sign-off is deferred until a complaint is settled, or until 12 December if none is received. A complaint, once received, runs twelve weeks." Twelve weeks. Marga had ten.

Her father, who piloted ships up the Westerschelde and had last overruled his counsel in 2029, and said so at birthdays, as a good thing, which it was, sent one message that evening: a heart, and the section statistics for Goes, which were good.

* * *

On Friday the simulator ran in the back room, on her counsel, with the doppler paired. It wrote her a birth and voiced the heart through the doppler against a foam pad, and she listened through two dozen contractions for anything to overrule and found nothing and scored a hundred, as everyone did; it could not write a heart it could not itself hear. The misses were the other test, births a counsel had heard nothing wrong in and something was, mixed blind with births where nothing was and played to see who could pick them, and she had sat those in 2038 and scored at chance, like everyone.

Marga was in the front room packing the practice into boxes for January. Noor went through to her with the phone and ten past two on it, the stretch her counsel had cut for her the first night, 02:06 to 02:13, four contractions, and held it out.

Marga looked at it. She did not take it. She lifted a box from the floor, cardboard, not light, and put it into Noor's hands instead: the birth books, handwritten, one a year, 2002 to 2030.

"The car," she said.

Noor carried it to the car. Nobody said what was in it. Marga said nothing about the night.

* * *

The complaint came on Tuesday morning, the eighth day. It named the departure at 02:14 with both midwives quoted, "Let me" and "Your call", and it carried the review's sentence word for word: a complaint naming Marga Dekker cannot conclude before her retirement on 31 January and would reach nobody. Twelve weeks from its receipt was the twelfth of February. Marga's successor started in January and had never seen Noor at a bed; the sign-off was a stranger's now. The same morning Sanne asked for Noor to come.

Marga read the complaint standing. "You needn't go," she said.

Noor went, because it was the room where she had heard it.

Joost opened the door of the house under the dike. "She's had it since Wednesday," he said. "She hasn't played it."

The bed was still on its blocks. Joost said it was easier for the stitches, getting up, so Sanne lay at the height Noor would have worked at, and Noor stood beside it with nothing to do with her hands.

"I sent it this morning," Sanne said. "Mine drafted it. It was very fair."

"I read it."

"'Let me.'" Sanne said it quietly, as it had been said. "Fourteen minutes past two. That's you. You asked her."

"Yes." It had the pitch of the landing.

"It says I was probably a training decision."

The photograph of the knot was on the same phone, behind the recording. The review had given it a paragraph. Noor did not offer it.

"What did you hear?"

The baby slept face down on Sanne's chest, one fist under her cheek. Noor had listened to ten past two perhaps four hundred times. The first night she had heard it after every contraction: the heart coming back a fraction late, like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called. By Friday she heard it one time in five. Since Sunday she had not heard it at all, and she had stopped asking the counsel, because it gave the same answer each time, patiently, and the patience was worse.

She set the phone on the duvet and pressed play.

The room filled with a heart, fast and soft, a horse going hard on sand. Then Sanne's own breath from that night, lower, dragging, the contraction building under it; the gallop slowing; the gallop coming back.

Noor heard a heart.

Sanne did not look at the phone. She kept her eyes on her daughter's head, and her hand lay flat on the small back and rose when it rose.

It ended. Noor reached for the phone.

"Again," Sanne said.

Noor's hand stayed on the phone, on the duvet. Sanne was not looking at either. She said, to the clip at the collar of her cardigan, "Where in it. Where she heard it."

Her counsel told her, fairly, at her collar, low, pitched for her, and Noor beside the bed heard it. "Four minutes and fifty-one seconds in, after the third contraction, where the heart comes back. I find nothing there. No one hears such a thing on a recording better than chance, midwives included."

Sanne held out her hand without turning her head. Noor put the phone in it. Sanne took it to her ear, and her other hand stayed flat on her daughter's back and rose when it rose, and Noor heard nothing of it: the heart went into Sanne's ear and not into the room. Sanne lay on the bed on its blocks, at the height Noor would have worked at, listening. Noor counted on Sanne's face toward the place where the heart came back.
