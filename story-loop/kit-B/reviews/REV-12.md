# REV-12 — depth-critic, M3 round 2, draft-02 of story B (2026-10-31)

## The six questions

1. **What is the story actually about?** It is about a perception that leaves no trace. In a world where every judgment is scored against a fair machine, such a perception can only be tested on someone else's body, the test returns nothing, and the perception is never refuted: it wears away under the machine's patience until all its owner can do is hand the listening to the woman who paid for it. That is not the plot. The plot is a transfer against advice, a section and a complaint; my sentence is about the ear. Its last part is what a plot summary would leave out: the hearing passes from Noor, who has lost it ("Noor heard a heart."), to Sanne, who paid for it ("Sanne lay at the height Noor would have worked at, listening.").

2. **Is the question real?** Yes. The question is whether anything was there, and whether Noor was right to act on it. A thoughtful person could hold either answer, because the text gives evidence on both sides.

   For the ear: March ("the cord twice round the neck and tight. Marga's counsel had heard nothing wrong in that heart either") and the knot ("True knot ... Loose ... They pull tight coming down. Or they don't.").

   Against it: June ("at Goes nothing wrong at all"); the national test ("Marga had sat them and scored at chance; so had Noor"); the counsel's measurement ("The four are within a second of one another and within the range I expect"); and the price ("Your section was probably avoidable, about three in five.").

   The finest piece of balance is the first replay. Noor hears the lateness after all four contractions, including two she had not heard at the bed ("She had not heard that at the bed"; "The second, which at the bed had been nothing to her: late"). The counsel finds the four alike. So what changed at ten past two was either the heart or her attention, and the text never says which. The case against Noor even includes her own motive: "There was nowhere left to learn whether an ear was real except a woman at eight centimetres, and the woman was behind the door." That makes the review's "consistent with a training decision" accurate rather than cruel, and the text lets Sanne say it to her face.

   The text does not decide. "Noor heard a heart." could be read as a verdict, but the paragraph before it gives another cause ("the patience was worse"). The facts lean slightly against the ear; the sympathy and the word choice lean toward Noor (see Findings). Neither settles it. This is a question, not a slogan.

   A second real question sits under the first: may a true reason be kept from the person who carries the risk, because saying it would lose the argument? ("The words were true, and she could say them." / "I can't tell you.") That is left open too. Sanne's "What did you hear?" brings it back a week later, and Noor answers with the recording, not with words.

3. **Does the story earn its idea, or announce it?** It earns it. In five places the strange world changes what someone does.
   - "I can't tell you." Any reason spoken in that room can be answered by three fair counsels ("Sanne would have her own counsel on one side and a student on the other"), so Noor keeps hers back.
   - "Sanne looked at Noor, and then past her at Marga's hands in the bag." The one signal in the room that nothing records is what Sanne says yes to. The review knows only words, so it records "the supervisor's letting it stand." The record misses the hands just as it missed the ear. The text never points this out, and it is the best use of the idea in the night.
   - "'Your call' had let stand what her own counsel advised against, and the record counted that as hers." A permission is scored as an act.
   - "She had stopped asking the counsel ... and the patience was worse", and then "Noor heard a heart." The world changes what she is able to hear.
   - The reader. Through the night and the first replay, the narration states the lateness as fact ("and the heart came back late"; "She had not heard that at the bed"). The last scene relabels it as what she "had heard", then reports "Noor heard a heart." The reader loses the gap along with her.

   The places where the idea is explained rather than used:
   - The landing's "nowhere left to learn ..." is Noor thinking it through, and it carries weight.
   - "What was not said, nothing at any collar could answer." is a maxim the next line already acts out.
   - The review's "I cannot report a hearing that left no trace in the record." states the idea in the counsel's voice. That is in character, but it is the one place a character says the idea outright.
   - The Friday simulator passage explains again what the landing already explained.

   Nearly all this explaining goes to the familiar part of the idea (see Findings). I am not disagreeing with the idea; it is earned.

4. **Do the people behave like people?** Yes, in specific and often unflattering ways, and nothing here rises to disbelief.
   - Sanne says yes to the senior midwife's hands, not the student's words. She has "the pitch of it exactly: the landing's, through a door pulled to", so she probably heard Marga's "I didn't hear it" as well.
   - She hardens the review's hedge into a verdict: "It says I was probably a training decision".
   - She sends the complaint and asks for Noor on the same morning.
   - Noor goes "because it was the room where she had heard it". That is self-interested, and true.
   - Her "I can't tell you" comes a few lines after "she could say them". It is a refusal dressed as an inability, the same shape as her "No" on the landing.
   - She does not offer the knot photograph to the woman who paid the cost.
   - When Noor holds out the phone, Marga answers with a box of birth books and "The car."
   - The father sends "a heart, and the section statistics for Goes".

   The moment where the plot is served instead of the person is Marga's "Your call" (see Findings). Everything the text tells us about her points toward staying, and the minute she spends looking at Noor shows the reader nothing.

5. **What does the ending cost?** Something real, for each of the three parties.
   - Noor has lost the hearing itself ("Noor heard a heart."). Her hand stays on the phone ("Noor's hand stayed on the phone, on the duvet") before she gives it up. She ends outside the listening with only a clock ("Four hundred plays had put the count in her"), while "the clip at Sanne's collar did" hear it. At the end the machine hears the heart and she does not.
   - Sanne gives up deferring. All night she said yes to whoever stood over her: Marga's hands, the obstetrician ("said yes before he had finished"), her counsel's draft ("Mine drafted it"). Now she listens for herself, against "I find nothing there." She also gives up not knowing: she has had the recording "since Wednesday" and has not played it.
   - The reader is refused the answer, and that is honest rather than evasive. The counsel has just said a recording cannot settle it ("better than chance, midwives included"), so Sanne's face could not settle it either.

   The bodily cost was paid earlier: the section, "the stitches", "any future birth be planned in hospital". The bed "still on its blocks" holds it, and that is enough.

6. **Would a reader be different afterwards?** Yes, in a small way.
   - A reader who assumed the outcome tells you whether a hunch was right now has a knot that pulls tight "Or they don't."
   - A reader who assumed a perception is either proved right or exposed has watched one simply fade.
   - And they keep the foot that "finds the stair a moment after it reaches for it."

   For a larger change, the story's newest thought needs the room its oldest one gets. The oldest is that no test can find an ear, and it is explained three times. The newest is that a fair voice wears away what it cannot refute, just by being patient, and it gets two sentences of summary. The reader feels the result and is only told the cause.

## Findings

F1: MINOR — The explaining goes to the familiar part of the idea, and the newest part is only summarized. That no test can find an ear is said three times:
- on the landing: "played them to every midwife in the country, to find the ears. It had found none ... The simulator, which was the counsel's, had nothing in it to overrule";
- in the Friday simulator: "The simulator could not write a heart the counsel could not hear. It knew no other. The misses were the other test, and she had sat those";
- by Sanne's counsel: "better than chance, midwives included".

The third is needed, because Sanne hears it before she listens. The second only repeats. Meanwhile the story's newest thought is that the perception is not refuted but worn away by a fair voice's patience. It is shown once, on the first night ("I find no gap." / "She played it again."). After that it is compressed into "By Friday she heard it one time in five. Since Sunday she had not heard it at all ... and the patience was worse." Friday is the middle of that wearing-away, yet the Friday scene spends its words explaining the simulator again. A reader will notice the repetition. What they never see is the repetition that does the damage, the same patient answer given again, and that is the thing they would have carried away.

F2: MINOR — The one place a person does what the plot needs is Marga's "Your call." Everything the text has shown points the other way:
- Of her six departures from her counsel, the two the text shows both start with her own ear and her one word, "Goes".
- She has just said "I didn't hear it".
- Both counsels say stay.
- She is the one the review says "could have overruled the transfer and did not."

The text gives her a look "for the length of a contraction coming on and going off", her arms folded, and Noor's memory of March. Later a reader can read backwards: she packs the bag and "did not look up", checks her figure after the knot, hands Noor a box instead of taking the phone. But at the moment itself the look holds nothing the reader can see, and afterwards Marga never says a word about the night. Readers will accept the decision because the plot turns on it. To believe it, they need one thing in that minute, seen or done, that shows what she is weighing.

F3: MINOR — The trap the night rests on reads as arranged, not found. This is about earning the idea, not disagreeing with it.

"There was nowhere left to learn whether an ear was real except a woman at eight centimetres" follows from a single test, and that test used only recordings: the misses were "mixed ... blind" and "played ... to every midwife in the country". Yet this is a world where every collar logs every word ("the record scored it as agreement"; "Both were logged at fourteen minutes past two"). Nine years of births already hold every worry a midwife spoke aloud, every "Marga. The next one. Listen.", each beside how that birth ended. The academy gathered exactly the births in which "a counsel had heard nothing wrong and something had been wrong". Whether a midwife spoke up in those births before the harm is one search away. A reader who knows how such questions get studied will ask why nobody looked, and the text does not say.

For Noor's own ear the trap would still hold, because one midwife never sees enough misses ("six departures in nine years were too few to make anything else of"). But the text claims it for ears in general ("to find the ears. It had found none"). One clause on why the live record cannot answer either would make the trap the world's.

F4: NIT — The word choice leans where the facts don't. "Fair" comes four times ("fairly", "It was fair", "very fair", "fairly"), and "patient", "patiently" and "patience" three times, all of them about the counsel or its work. Noor's "It was fair" and Sanne's "It was very fair" are concessions and carry the motif honestly. By the last one, "Sanne's counsel told her, fairly, low, pitched for her", the word lands as an accusation. The question needs the counsel to be the honest party, and the refrain makes it slightly less so.

F5: NIT — On the bridge the counsel goes quiet at the one point where speaking would have foretold the cost: "About the five minutes it said nothing. Nothing had yet happened that it could speak to." This is a voice that speaks unasked on the landing ("spoke before she asked it anything") and on the bridge ("about one in thirty"), and that measures intervals to the second ("I have measured the interval from the end of each contraction"). Contractions spacing out to three, four and five minutes is something that has happened, and it is what ends in the section at Goes. The silence keeps the machine from being seen to predict the stall, which slightly shelters Noor's side of the question.
