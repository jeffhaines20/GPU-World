# REV-06 — depth-critic, M2, the spine of story B, "Working Height", second round (2026-09-26)

## Reading

1. **What the story is about.** It is about a kind of knowing that can only be tried out on someone else's body, and that any record keeps only as wanting. The fair audit handed to the woman who paid can price the night and name its motive, but it cannot hold what the night was for, and at the end she is listening for that herself. That is not the plot. The plot is: a trainee overrules the counsel on a sound, the mother is moved and has a section, a review and a complaint follow, and the mother plays the recording. What the spine adds is the imbalance of the record ("afterwards the record reads wanting, never hearing", Obstacle), and then it turns that imbalance onto the payer, who has the verdict and wants the sound.

2. **Is the question real?** Yes. It is a real question, not a slogan, and it survives either answer to the factual question: even if Noor heard nothing, every apprentice ear has false alarms, and someone pays for them.
   - For: Marga's cord (Who, beat 2). The ceiling, shown twice: the simulator "plays only hearts the counsel can hear", and the misses score at chance, so a live birth is the only place an ear could ever get past what the counsel can hear. The true knot (beat 3), which a reader will hold on to because nobody offers it. Sanne listening (beat 6).
   - Against: "three in five" (beat 4). The spine's hardest line, "A woman at eight centimetres, asked to be the test, would say stay" (Obstacle), which makes "I can't tell you" a consent to fear, not to the test. Noor checking her figure on the bridge (beat 3). The want still unchanged in the last line.
   - The text does not decide whether she heard anything. It leans against her in three places: "through the same doppler" (beat 1); her eighth departure this year against Marga's seventh in nine (beat 2); and the passage's replay curve (F4). The knot and "Left side" hold the question open.
   - The one place the spine decides is its own wording of the question (F2).

3. **Earned or announced?** Mostly earned. The idea is used wherever the world changes what someone does, or what their act becomes.
   - Marga's "Left side", said a breath before the counsel and "scored as agreement" (beat 1), is the whole idea in one clause: agreement is where an ear goes invisible.
   - "Let me" is the most natural thing a trainee says to her supervisor. It is logged at 02:14, quoted by the payer's counsel, and turned by the payer into "That's you. You asked her" (passage). The audit reads a want Noor never named ("None of it names the want", beat 2).
   - "She listens for anything to overrule and scores a hundred" (beat 5) shows the ceiling as a perfect score.
   - Noor's 96.9 on the bridge and Marga's phone at 07:50 are cost in the world's own currency.
   - The ending is the idea as an act: her counsel gives Sanne the second, says nothing is there, and she listens.
   - Announced: "The other test she sat in 2038: the misses, blind, and she scored at chance" (beat 5). The fact the question stands on arrives as a memory, and beat 5 never says what the misses are (F6). The review's long list in beat 4 would be a briefing if the passage were not where the reader hears it quoted back.
   - Left unused: the review's "a complaint naming her cannot conclude before 31 January" is the one place the audit gives the payer a lever over the departer, and Sanne pulls it offstage (F5).

4. **Do the people behave like people?** Mostly, and in places very well: Marga, who never looks at her figure, looking at 07:50; Sanne running the price and the motive together in "It says I was probably a training decision"; Noor not offering the photograph; the father's heart and section statistics; Marga not taking the phone.
   - I believe "Your call". Generosity, self-protection and January give it three readings the record cannot tell apart, which is the point.
   - I believe Noor skipping the counsel's "listen through three more". Someone who wanted confirmation would listen; someone who wanted the act would not. The review reads it that way. That is character, not plot.
   - The moment the plot needs something these people would not do is beat 2, in the room. The exchange runs from "I can't tell you" to "All right" while both their counsels say stay, and Joost, at the head of the bed and not in labour, never turns to the 65-year-old midwife by the door. People do that first. The spine needs "All right" to rest on the trainee's fear alone, and it is the line the review later quotes back to Sanne (F1).

5. **What does the ending cost?**
   - Noor: her sign-off passes from Marga to a stranger (if the reader can follow the dates, F5); the sound itself, gone since Sunday; the complaint, which stands; and, in the last line, the want, watching Sanne's face and spending her again.
   - Sanne: the clean account. By listening at the second her counsel calls empty, she lets the verdict she paid for, and sent that morning, stop being the whole of her daughter's birth. That cost is inward, and staked rather than lost. But it is real, because the passage has shown what the account was worth to her ("It was very fair").
   - Marga: her figure, her last weeks, her name in a complaint.
   - Nothing is refunded and nobody is pardoned. Ending "before the play does" is not a verdict withheld as a pose. By the story's own premise, whatever Sanne hears would settle nothing, and the act itself, listening against her counsel's finding, is on the page.

6. **Would a reader be different afterwards?** Yes, in a small way, and of the kind the brief asks for: the assumption the story works on is that a fair record is a full one. A reader could leave seeing that an audit can price a decision and read its motive and still say nothing about what the decision was made on, and that agreeing with a better tool is where judgment drops out of the record. That depends on two things landing: "Left side" being seen, and the misses being understood as the reason the question is open rather than as a score from 2038. If they don't land, a reader leaves with only the plot: the trainee was wrong and the mother softened.

## Findings

F1: MINOR — Beat 2, the bedroom at 02:14–02:16: the exchange goes from "I can't tell you" to "All right" while both their counsels say stay, and Joost, at the head of the bed and not in labour, never turns to Marga, which is the first thing he would do. The consent the review later prices ("All right" at 02:16) needs Marga visibly in that room doing something the parents take as assent (standing, reaching for the bag), which the review can then call "could have overruled and did not".

F2: MINOR — The Question section asks whether she may spend a birth "to find out whether her hearing is worth anything". Its own Who has already answered that ("nothing that could happen could tell anyone whether she heard anything"). It also rests the For side on "that is how every midwife who ever outheard the counsel was made", which runs together Marga's training on women who had no counsel, when her ear was the best on offer, and Noor's training against a counsel that is right far more often. The For that holds is the ceiling the spine already has (if nobody departs, no ear ever gets past what the counsel can hear), which is the world's "to become"; the question should be written to that, or the story answers it. This is the argument as stated not being earned, not a disagreement with the idea.

F3: MINOR — Noor's seven earlier departures this year (the review, beat 4; "Noor's eighth this year", beat 2) are the audit's best evidence of want and the reader's only evidence of what her ear has done at a bed, and the spine says neither what they were nor how they came out. Set beside Marga's seven in nine years, they make her a habitual departer and tilt the question, unless the story knows what they were and the review says so.

F4: MINOR — In the passage, Noor hears the late return "after every contraction" on the first night's replay, but beat 1 gives her one late return live, and Marga hears nothing on the next. Read together, the rise and fading look like a projection going out, and the text quietly settles the factual question. Beat 1 should say whether she heard it again live, so the curve can be read either way.

F5: MINOR — Noor's heaviest cost at the end, her sign-off passing from Marga to a stranger, rests on two date lines in beat 4 that point opposite ways: "can take eight weeks, and Marga retires in ten" reads as time enough, while "a complaint naming her cannot conclude before 31 January" says there is not. It also rests on an act the spine keeps offstage: Sanne sending a complaint that names Marga after her own counsel has told her what that will do. That act is the world's question in one move, the payer turning the audit on the departer, and the reader should see it rather than have to work it out.

F6: MINOR — The misses are the fact that keeps the question open, and beat 5 names them ("the misses, blind, and she scored at chance") without saying what they are. "A fact, not a scene" still allows, and needs, one plain clause: recordings of births the counsel heard nothing wrong in, played blind to see whether anyone can pick the ones that went wrong. Without it, the reader loses the reason no recording can settle anything.

F7: NIT — "Noor Brouwer's seven departures this year" (beat 4) against "Noor's eighth this year" (beat 2) will read as a slip unless the review plainly means the seven before this one.

F8: NIT — In the last beat "second" carries three senses (the second play, Sanne's second departure, the second on the recording), and "counts to the second" is also the idiom for "counts exactly". The last sentence's object is what the ending turns on, and it risks reading as an adverb of precision.
