# REV-09 — depth-critic, M3, draft-01 of story B (2026-10-29)

## The six questions

1. **What is the story actually about?** In one sentence: it is about what happens to a perception that cannot be put into words, in a world that scores every judgement against a fair machine. The record turns the hearing into a wanting, patient correction takes it away from the one who heard it, and the only person left who can still give it a hearing is the woman who may have paid for it. That is not the plot. The plot is a transfer, a section, a complaint and a replay. My sentence is what the plot is for, and the text says so in three places:
   - the review: "What the record supports is the student's wanting it and the supervisor's letting it stand. The record holds what was said, done and wanted. It does not hold what was heard" (line 86);
   - the replays: "By Friday she heard it one time in five. Since Sunday she had not heard it at all ... it gave the same answer each time, patiently, and the patience was worse" (line 132);
   - the last paragraph, where the woman who sent the complaint puts the phone to her ear.

   It is not a story about the machine being wrong. The draft takes care that the counsels are fair, and says so three times: "It was fair" (line 88), "It was very fair" (line 118), "Her counsel told her, fairly" (line 148). There is something under it.

2. **Is the question real?** Yes. There are two questions, and the text decides neither.

   The first question is whether Noor heard anything, and whether she was right to act on it. There is evidence on both sides.
   - For: Marga's March transfer, "the cord twice round the neck and tight, which the counsel had not heard" (line 40), and a true knot that is really there.
   - Against: Marga "shook her head" and says "I didn't hear it"; the reanalysis found "no late deceleration in any"; on the misses Noor "scored at chance, like everyone" (line 96); her seven earlier departures made no "difference the record could show"; and on replay, "Noor heard a heart" (line 138).

   The sharpest thing the draft does to keep this honest is to let acting on the hearing cause the harm it cannot prove it prevented. On the bridge "The contractions were spacing out: three minutes, then four"; at Goes "labour stopped at eight centimetres"; the review's verdict is "probably avoidable, about three in five".

   The second question is whether Marga's "Your call" was trust in an ear she did not share, or what the review calls "a training decision". Marga never answers it. The reader has her March and her June ("the same word, the same bridge, and nothing") and can argue it either way.

   The draft also refuses the nostalgic answer. The woman "trained from 1998 on women who had no counsel" heard nothing; the student who sat the misses in 2038 heard it.

   The text comes closest to deciding in two places where the narration comments in its own voice. "It measured agreement and nothing else" (line 40) leans toward Noor. The father's "as a good thing, which it was" (line 92), on its likelier reading, leans toward the counsel. Neither settles anything, and the ending stops before 4:51. This is a question, not a slogan. The knot settles nothing for a reader who knows what a loose true knot can do as the baby descends; for a reader who does not, it leans toward the review (see Findings).

3. **Does the story earn its idea, or announce it?** Mostly it earns it, though it explains it more than it needs to.

   Where the idea is explained:
   - in narration at the decision: "It measured agreement and nothing else, and six in nine years were too few to measure anything else by" and "The simulator had nothing in it to overrule" (lines 40–42);
   - again on Friday: "it could not write a heart it could not itself hear" (line 96);
   - twice inside the world of the story: the review (line 86), and the counsel's "No one hears such a thing on a recording better than chance, midwives included" (line 148).

   The two in-world statements come in the right voice: a counsel stating, fairly, the limit of its own record ("I cannot report a hearing that left no trace in it"). Both are then put to use, so neither is the lecture in dialogue the brief rules out.

   Where the idea changes what someone does:
   - Speech. Every human decision is spoken without a reason: "Let me", "Your call", "I want to take you to Goes" (literally a wanting), "All right". The only paragraphs of reasoning belong to the counsels. The reader feels, before the review says it, that the people have no sentences the record could hold.
   - Consent. "There was a counsel on every collar in that house", and Sanne's and Joost's both say stay (line 54). So the one dissenting voice in the room cannot give a reason, and Sanne consents to the only signal left: "Marga's hands in the bag" (line 60).
   - The record's language gets into a person. "It says I was probably a training decision" (line 126) puts the counsel's "probably" in Sanne's mouth.
   - Handed a recording, Marga answers with handwriting: "the birth books, handwritten, one a year, 2002 to 2030" (line 100).
   - The counsel's patience wears Noor's hearing away (line 132), and that pays off in "Noor heard a heart."
   - The best use is the last scene. Sanne's own counsel tells her that no one hears such a thing better than chance. She asks "Where in it. Where she heard it." (line 146) and listens anyway, with her other hand on the living child.

   The announcing is concentrated in the narration at lines 40–42 and 96 (see Findings). Nothing here or below is a disagreement with the idea: the idea is earned, and the findings are about how it is carried.

4. **Do the people behave like people?** At the hinges, yes, and that is where it matters most. Each of the three decisions that move the story has a reason that belongs to the person who makes it.
   - Marga's "Your call" comes after she "looked at her for the length of a contraction coming on and going off" (line 46), with her own March and June behind it.
   - Sanne's "All right" is given to Marga's hands, which is how people consent in extremity.
   - Noor's "Let me" goes against an interest she has just named: "whoever came after ... would sign her off or not from the record" (line 40).

   The small cracks are human too. Noor "had never once seen" Marga look at her figure, and Marga looks at it once, after the loose knot (line 78). Noor photographs the knot and later "did not offer it" (line 128).

   The moment where a character does what the plot needs is Noor's "I can't tell you" to Sanne (line 58). The narration has given Noor words for what she heard: "the heart came back a fraction late" (line 18), "like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called" (line 132). She never says them to anyone.
   - With Marga ("The next one. Listen.") and with her counsel ("No"), the silence has reasons a reader can supply: she does not want to prime a second listener, and she knows "late" will come back as "No deceleration".
   - With Sanne it has no such reason. Sanne is at eight centimetres and is being asked to cross a five-kilometre bridge. A midwife would say she does not like how the heart is coming back. That is true, it can be said, and a counsel cannot refute it.

   The story needs the record to hold nothing but "Let me", "Your call" and "All right", and needs Sanne's consent to rest on Marga's hands. This line is where that need shows through Noor.

   A smaller instance: the complaint is sent and Noor is called to the house on the same morning (lines 108, 118). That is the order the ending needs, because it means the listening can change nothing on the record. The dialogue gives just enough for the reader to supply Sanne's reason.

5. **What does the ending cost?** Something real, and nothing is taken back.

   Before the last scene, Noor has lost her live cases and the sign-off from the one person who had seen her at a bed ("the sign-off was a stranger's now", line 108). Her deepest loss is the hearing itself: "Since Sunday she had not heard it at all". Marga's last student goes to a stranger.

   In the last scene Noor gives up two more things, by choice:
   - her one piece of evidence: "The review had given it a paragraph. Noor did not offer it.";
   - the recording. After a hesitation ("Noor's hand stayed on the phone, on the duvet"), she puts it in the hand of the woman who complained, so that "the heart went into Sanne's ear and not into the room" (line 150). She ends outside her own hearing, reading it off another person's face.

   Sanne's "Again" is a risk of her own. She has sent a complaint she cannot take back, and she may now hear that her section was for something, or confirm that it was for nothing.

   The draft's best image of cost is the bed. It was raised on its blocks for a home birth that did not happen, and it stays raised because it is "easier for the stitches", so that "Sanne lay at the height Noor would have worked at" (line 116). The baby is unharmed ("nine at one minute and ten at five"). That is right: the cost falls on the three women, and on nothing the record can see.

6. **Would a reader be different afterwards, in any small way?** Yes, in a small way. A reader is likely to come away with three things:
   - "Let me" as the shape a hearing takes once it is written down: a wish, not a perception;
   - the passage where the hearing fades ("By Friday she heard it one time in five. Since Sunday she had not heard it at all"), which pays off in "Noor heard a heart.";
   - the last line, which puts the reader where Noor is: counting toward a place in a recording that a fair voice has just said holds nothing.

   The second is the one most likely to make a reader think differently about something they had assumed: that a perception, once you have had it, stays yours. It is also the draft's closest link to *The Yellow Wallpaper*, from the brief: a kind, reasonable voice patiently talking a woman out of what she perceived. It works in three sentences and should survive revision.

   A hurried reader may take away only the familiar version of the question, gut against model. The draft has the less familiar one too. Two things would make the change larger:
   - the reader should know, at "Let me", what Noor is betting against: her own ear, measured and found to be at chance;
   - the draft could use something it sets up and leaves alone: a dike inspector, in a house under the dike, in a province that lives by acting on small computed risks before a failure shows.

   Both are below. The draft is about 2,650 words against the brief's "about 3,500", so space is not the constraint.

## Findings

F1: MINOR — Noor's "I can't tell you" (line 58) is the one line where a person does the story's work instead of her own.
   - The narration has given her words for what she heard: "the heart came back a fraction late ... as if there had been a gap before it the width of a breath held" (line 18), and "like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called" (line 132). She never uses them with anyone.
   - To Marga and to her counsel, the silence can be defended: she wants an unprimed second listener, and she knows the counsel will translate "late" into "No deceleration".
   - To Sanne it cannot. Sanne is at eight centimetres, about to be driven over a five-kilometre bridge against her own counsel's advice. Any midwife would tell her she does not like how the heart is coming back, which is true and which no counsel can refute.
   - The line carries weight. It is what makes Sanne's consent rest on "Marga's hands in the bag", and what lets the review say the record holds only "the student's wanting it". A reader who does not believe the line will feel the consent, and the later grievance, become shaky.
   - The draft half-covers it. Sanne's counsel has just said "I recommend staying" (line 54), and Noor may be refusing to give it anything to answer. But nothing shows her deciding that, so the line reads as what the record needs rather than what she would say.
   - Two ways out: make the silence visibly her choice against the counsels at every collar, or let her say the true thing and let the review turn it into wanting anyway. The second would sharpen the idea, not lose it.

F2: MINOR — Noor's decision (lines 40–44) is briefed rather than weighed, and the briefing is the wrong way round: it explains what the story explains again later, and holds back the one fact the decision turns on.
   - "She thought of the misses, and of her score on them, which was everyone's. The simulator had nothing in it to overrule" (line 42) cannot be understood until Friday, four scenes later: "births a counsel had heard nothing wrong in and something was ... she had sat those in 2038 and scored at chance, like everyone" (line 96).
   - So on a first reading "Let me" is simple conviction. Only on a second reading is it what it really is: a bet against her own ear, which has been measured and found to be at chance.
   - Meanwhile the paragraph spends its weight on things said again elsewhere. "The simulator had nothing in it to overrule" here repeats "it could not write a heart it could not itself hear" at line 96. The registry here repeats the review's "The record holds what was said, done and wanted" at line 86.
   - It also holds the one sentence where the narration, not a character, passes judgement on the system: "It measured agreement and nothing else". Its second half, "six in nine years were too few to measure anything else by", is the fair version and makes the point on its own.
   - The thoughts that belong to Noor at 02:13 are the stranger who will sign her off in January and the March cord "twice round the neck and tight". They would carry the paragraph if the misses were plain in it.

F3: MINOR — The dike is set up and never used.
   - Sanne "was a dike inspector for the water board and had wanted this at home" (line 14), and the house is "under the dike" (lines 6, 114).
   - She is the one person in the story whose work is finding a fault before it shows. The story's question is whether to act on a small sign nobody can prove, against a computed risk, and that is the question her trade and her province live by.
   - It bears on nothing: not her "All right", not the complaint. In the last scene it survives only as a faint echo in "Where in it", for a reader who makes the link alone.
   - Her final act is believable without it, since "a training decision" is reason enough. But this is the readiest way to make her listening come from her own life rather than from the story's need to hand the question on, and to set the question inside something the reader already knows.

F4: NIT — The knot settles nothing only for a reader who knows that a loose true knot can pull tight as the baby descends. That knowledge is what makes "True knot. Loose. Look" (line 76) the exact image of the story's question: a danger that was real and had not yet happened. A reader who does not know it takes the review's "a loose true knot is not evidence that transfer was needed" (line 84) as the last word, and Noor's photograph reads as her clutching at straws. For that reader the question tips a little toward "nothing there". A few words from the obstetrician would restore the balance without deciding it.

F5: NIT — Sanne sends the complaint and calls for Noor on the same morning (lines 108, 118). That order is the one the ending needs, because it guarantees the listening can change nothing on the record. The dialogue lets the reader supply a reason ("It was very fair", then "It says I was probably a training decision", then "What did you hear?"). But Joost's "She's had it since Wednesday. She hasn't played it" tells us she waited a week, not what changed on the eighth day. Most readers will not stop here; a careful one will see the timing as the story's rather than hers.

F6: NIT — "had last overruled his counsel in 2029, and said so at birthdays, as a good thing, which it was" (line 92) has two readings. Either the 2029 overruling is the good thing, or the years since without one are. This is the one sentence where the narration gives its own verdict on overruling. Either reading serves the story, but the reader should be able to tell which is meant.
