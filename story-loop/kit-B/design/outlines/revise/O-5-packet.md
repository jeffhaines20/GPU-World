# Revision packet for O-5

## The outline as it stands

### O-5 — The Herring's Backbone
Conceit: On an island whose judges still swear to judge "as indifferently as the herring's backbone doth lie in the midst of the fish," a model keeps that oath perfectly and a statute makes keeping it compulsory. The only way to become a judge who sees past the middle is to be partial, on a live case, with someone else's child.
Who: Aalin Corlett, 44, a Deemster of the Manx High Court in Douglas, raised above a kipper yard in Peel. In seven years she has given 2,914 judgments, and every one agreed with Counsel. Before the First Deemster climbs Tynwald Hill for the last time on Thursday, she wants to give one judgment of her own: to learn whether anything is kept in her breast, where Deemsters were once said to keep the law.
Obstacle: First Deemster Juan Quayle's Concordance Act (2031): in a Deemster's first ten years a departure from Counsel is void unless he certifies it, and on Thursday he reads from the hill the amendment ending departure for every Deemster, for good. The oath forbids "affinity," and her sight may be only Peel. The price falls on Jen Hurst, a Liverpool-born nurse taking her son Fynlo, ten, home to a dying mother and a job, and on the boy. It cannot be bought off: Aalin can stake her office, but that is not the currency Jen pays in, and Jen has the same Counsel.
Turn: On Tynwald morning, in the chapel vestry at St John's, Juan holds out his hand for one of two sheets: her certificate or her withdrawal. He will not look at the boy, because he would see his own granddaughter. She hands him the certificate. He signs it on his knee, walks out along the rushes, and reads from the hill the Act that makes it the last. Fynlo stays, his mother crosses alone, and Aalin has departed once and never may again.
Last image: Saturday, an hour after the Liverpool boat left with Jen aboard: at the heritage bench in Quirk's yard, Fynlo runs the knife down a herring's back and opens it flat with the spine down the middle. He hangs it on the tenterhooks among rows of opened fish, every backbone in the midst, and does not look up.
Question: May Aalin keep a boy on the island against his mother, on a sight she cannot put into words, so that one judge here will have seen past the middle? Or is that a Peel woman's affinity and her wish to be someone, paid for by a come-over on the Liverpool boat?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That impartiality is what makes a judge worth having; once a machine keeps the oath of indifference perfectly, only partial sight can exceed it, and that can be learned only on someone's actual child, whose mother can read the bill.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: The rule-maker neither enforces his rule nor rescues her from it: disqualified by his grief, he makes her choose in front of him, signs, and shuts the door behind her, so her one departure is the island's last and no later case will teach her whether it was right. It is earned because his grievance was never a departure that went wrong (the Deemster who learned fathers on his son was right), and because Jen's audit has already made Aalin say "No" aloud.
Not the obvious version: A judge trusts her gut over the algorithm and is punished and vindicated, or humbled when the machine proves right. Here nothing arrives to vindicate anyone, and Counsel is right by every measure anyone can check. The question is not whether she sees truly but whether she may find out on a come-over's son, and the price leaves on the morning boat, in plain view.
Closest published: "Harrison Bergeron," Kurt Vonnegut: a law handicaps everyone down to equality and the exceptional one breaks it. Vonnegut's cap is satirical tyranny and his rebel admirable. Here the cap was made by a man who paid, for reasons the story honors; the exception may be only affinity; and one named mother bears the cost.
Beats:
1. Friday, Courtroom 2, Deemsters Walk: Counsel on every screen says grant leave to remove. Aalin, who met Fynlo in chambers, adjourns to Monday: the first act in seven years no model advised.
2. Juan's half-boxed chambers: the certificate, Thursday's amendment, and his bill. In 2028 a Deemster eleven months on the bench departed from Counsel on his son and sent the grandchildren to Bolton. He was right, and later thanked Juan for his son. The clock is set; the door has an owner.
3. Saturday, Quirk's yard, Peel: in the smoke Aalin watches Fynlo split herring beside his grandmother, knowing it improper. He holds the knife as her father did, and she cannot tell whether she is seeing him or herself at ten. She stays anyway.
4. Monday: she refuses leave. In the reason field she types continuity of care and attachment to home, and every screen in the room answers that it is insufficient. Jen stands with her phone lit. It is on the record.
5. Tuesday, 7 a.m., the car park: Jen, off a night shift, runs the judgment and each of Aalin's reasons through her own model and asks whether she knows. Aalin says no; Jen has it recorded.
6. Wednesday: Juan has heard the recording. He will not question her, and will sign whichever sheet she hands him. The debt is hers alone.
7. Thursday, Tynwald Day: vestry, two sheets, his pen skipping on his knee; the procession on the rushes. Juan reads the amendment in English, the Reader reads it in Manx, and the crowd checks the translation on their phones. The door shuts behind her.
8. Saturday: Aalin does not go to the pier. Jen crosses alone, "until September." At the yard, Fynlo is at the bench.
The passage:
Jen was waiting by the car in her scrubs from the night shift at Noble's, the rain darkening the shoulders. She held her phone out flat, like a plate.

"I've read it to mine," she said. "I want you to hear what it says."

The voice was pleasant and unhurried, near enough the voice that had read Aalin two thousand nine hundred and fourteen judgments before she signed them. *The judgment identifies no evidence that relocation would harm the child. It gives considerable weight to his connection to Peel, which the welfare report describes as ordinary for a boy of his age. A reviewing court would probably find the decision outside the range of reasonable outcomes. It may be worth asking whether familiarity with the community played a part.*

"Familiarity," Jen said. "Give me another reason. A real one. I'll put it in."

Aalin said that he was patient with his hands.

Jen typed with her thumb. *Patience is a strength, and it would go with him to Liverpool. It is not in itself a reason to refuse relocation.*

Aalin said he had gone quiet when she asked about Liverpool, and talked for ten minutes about the boat.

*Children often find familiar subjects easier to talk about. His stated wish was to live with his mother.*

She said, and heard how it would sound before it was out, that he held a knife the way her father had.

Jen did not type that one. She looked at Aalin for a while, the look she must give a monitor at four in the morning, counting.

"Fourteen years I've lived here," she said. "I have never once been from here. Do you know you're right?"

Rain ticked on the roofs of the cars. Somewhere down the hill a bin lorry was reversing.

"No," Aalin said.

Jen nodded, as if she had been given a figure she could work with, and put the phone back in the breast pocket of her scrubs, where it went on glowing through the cloth.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this outline

### reviewer-a — question 4, surprise 4, insight 5, grip 4, person 5, ending 4, prose 5 (total 31 of 35)

question: Every part of the question is staged (reasons spoken aloud and rebutted by the mother's model, "Do you know you're right?" "No," a lawmaker whose son was practiced on by a judge who turned out right, the island's last departure), but keeping a ten-year-old from his mother against his stated wish because of how he holds a knife tilts the scale so far toward vanity that the other side lives mostly in the abstract.
surprise: The rule-maker neither enforces his rule nor rescues her; he signs on his knee and reads out the Act that makes hers the last departure, which no reader would foresee, though once she refuses leave in beat 4 the rest (audit, certification, the boy stays) is visible.
insight: That once a machine keeps the oath of indifference perfectly, impartiality stops being what makes a human judge worth having, and only the partiality the oath forbids can exceed it, learnable only on someone's actual child, truly overturns an assumption, and the Act shows a society capping its practical wisdom at the frontier for good.
grip: Two clocks (Tynwald Thursday, the Saturday boat) and the car-park scene are strong, but the Concordance Act, its certification rule, the amendment, the 2028 backstory and the Manx ceremony are a heavy load of institution for 5,000 words, and a reader could stall in it before the car park.
person: Aalin, raised above a kipper yard in Peel and seeing her father's knife grip in a stranger's boy, and Jen in rain-dark scrubs off a night at Noble's, "never once been from here," are particular people in a place drawn down to the rushes on the hill.
ending: Fynlo opening a herring with the spine down the middle and hanging it on the tenterhooks without looking up is earned and keeps his state unknown, but the title emblem returning in the child's hands, "every backbone in the midst," is a touch designed.
prose: The phone held "out flat, like a plate," the model's plausibly hedged italics answering reasons given in reported speech, "the look she must give a monitor at four in the morning, counting," and the phone "glowing through the cloth" are at the level of the best published stories, and only the rain-ticked, bin-lorry pause is stock.
largest gap: Aalin's case for keeping the boy is so thin (a knife grip, a silence about Liverpool) against a mother, a dying grandmother and the boy's stated wish, and the Manx party opposing the move never gets a face or a claim, so readers will convict her in the car park and the question will close before Tynwald.

## The originality critic on this outline

**O-5 The Herring's Backbone.** (1) Ian McEwan, *The Children Act* (2014). Same: a woman judge in a family case meets the boy in person before ruling, in an unusual hospital visit, and overrides both his parents and his own stated wish; the cost lands on the boy afterwards. Different: there is no machine, and Fiona rules squarely within the law. Aalin departs from a counsel every litigant can run, under a statute that ends departures, and the cost leaves on the Liverpool boat. (2) Hall Caine, *The Deemster* (1887), the first Manx novel. Same: the Deemster as a figure, the herring's backbone as the image of fair justice (ch. 33, fetched: "as fair as the backbone lies down the middle of a herring", said of rough justice), and justice tangled with the Deemster's own family. Different: it is a Victorian melodrama of murder and outlawry, and nothing in O-5's plot comes from it. (3) Philip K. Dick, "The Minority Report" (1956). Same: a single dissent against a system whose predictions bind. Different: Dick's is a thriller of self-preservation; here the dissent is certified and becomes the last. (4) Kurt Vonnegut, "Harrison Bergeron" (1961), the outline's own choice, is weak; it shares only a law that caps the exceptional. Asimov's "The Evitable Conflict" (1950), with its always-right Machines, is about as loose. The Concordance Act's real-world model is the US Federal Sentencing Guidelines, binding until *United States v. Booker* (2005); "departure", the word the whole loop uses, is the Guidelines' own term. Verdict: familiar furniture (judge against algorithm), new house. The Manx oath, the Act read from Tynwald Hill, and a rule-maker who signs rather than enforces make this the least familiar arrangement of the five. MINOR at most, for the unnamed neighbours.

**Between the outlines.** O-1 and O-3 are one story twice. In both, a father keeps a paper record of his judgments about his child, one book a year, written at night. The grown child (19 in O-1, 23 in O-3) spends the last night before leaving by boat for a working life (deckhand; junior doctor) scoring the books at the kitchen table, as "Right"/"Wrong" or as ticks and crosses. The deciding entry is a teenage episode where the father guessed what was happening and stayed away. The child's answer is that the father's coming was the thing dreaded ("Because you'd have come"; "if Dad comes up those stairs into the gallery I will die"). Both close on the kitchen table after the child has gone, with a line left blank and a pen held over it (O-1) or lying across it (O-3). The only real difference is the father's sign: Wade always departed from the model, Magnus never did. That mirror is interesting, but it does not make two stories. O-4 tells the same premise a third time. Its plot departs furthest from O-1, but it keeps O-1's main elements: the father who never asked the counsel, the 19-year-old leaving home, the learning curve with the child's early years at the bottom, a theft as a key entry, and the question "raised, or practised on?" word for word. O-2 and O-5 are one skeleton twice. In both, a practitioner sits at one end of a concordance scale (Noor's 97.1 is the province's lowest; Aalin's 2,914 judgments all agreed with Counsel). A retiring elder who knows from the inside what a departure costs lets one happen (Marga's five women moved for nothing; Juan's son and grandchildren, spent on another Deemster's education). The one departure falls on a stranger and rests on a perception that cannot be put into words. Then a working woman who reads instruments for a living ("the way she would read a gauge"; "the look she must give a monitor at four in the morning") runs her own model's fair review and asks whether the practitioner knows ("Do you know now?"; "Do you know you're right?"). The two passages are that same scene. All five end on a verdict withheld: a pen held over the line; "Again"; a tick crossed through and a last line left blank; "could not tell"; "No". The passages also share one writer's reflexes. Four cut from the hard line to an ambient sound ("The furnace came on"; "The Rayburn ticked"; "The radiator ticked. Downstairs the fridge came on."; "Rain ticked on the roofs of the cars"). Three have a hard line said flatly "the way" a job reads things (a gauge, a potassium, the wind getting up). Two have someone who "did not remember" a small act of the hand (O-1, O-4). Two have someone look "at the window, where" (O-1, O-2). Three of the five name the same Chiang story as their closest neighbour. That choice is wrong for O-2, and O-3 and O-5 miss nearer neighbours (F5–F7).
