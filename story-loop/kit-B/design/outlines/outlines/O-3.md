### O-3 — Undispensed
Conceit: A pharmacist has not once gone against the counsel since the year it stopped improving. Every night he has kept a paper register of what he would have done instead. A judgment never acted on produces no outcome, so the only person who can grade it is the daughter it would have been practised on.
Who: Magnus Flett, 50, pharmacist on the main street of Stromness, Orkney, 2040. He is "clean" by the town's word and by his daughter's audit when she was nineteen. Nell, 23, is home for two nights before she starts as a junior doctor in Aberdeen. He wants her to mark his fourteen green notebooks: to learn whether, all along, someone was there.
Obstacle: No model can score a road not taken. It knows only what it was told, and only Nell knows what was true of Nell. Asking her is itself a live case: the counsel answers well (not this week; ask who the books are for). Nothing buys the key, because the key is her, and turning it costs her.
Turn: He gives her the books against the counsel. It is his first departure, and he runs it on her the week she starts on the wards. She marks them in his green dispensing pen. For fourteen years the life he didn't impose could have been the wiser one. By morning it has a score, in ink, and the score splits: he saw her truly, and what he would have done about it would mostly have hurt her.
Last image: The kitchen table after the boat has gone. The fourteenth book lies open at its last page, her green ticks and crosses running down the margin and stopping one line short. There is nothing beside his Friday line, and her capped pen lies across it.
Question: Was fourteen years of fidelity the humility that spared his daughter an apprentice's mistakes, or clean hands kept at her expense? And is making her grade him now the first judgment he has made, or a vanity she pays for?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That the man who always takes good counsel has no judgment and has cost no one; in fact he has the half of judgment that sees, which keeps in a drawer, and lacks the half that acts, which can only be learned on someone, so sparing her was also a thing done to her.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: The reader braces for a hollow man exposed or a follower who breaks free; instead the departure comes early, the counsel itself suspects it of vanity, and the suspense is a grade that splits judgment down its middle. It is earned because every mark answers an entry the reader has already read in his hand, and the one that matters is planted in the first scene, where the 2031 book falls open by itself.
Not the obvious version: The man who obeyed the machine is audited by his child and found empty. At the end he makes one choice of his own (climbs to the gallery; shines a torch into the garden), and it reads as liberation or as Ivan Ilyich's verdict. Here he was never empty, his departure is small and suspect, and the audit returns a grade that clears neither the fidelity nor the man.
Closest published: "The Beast in the Jungle," Henry James: a man waits all his life for his fate and learns at a grave that it was to be the man to whom nothing happened. James delivers the verdict. Here the unlived life was written down nightly, and the person it would have happened to grades it and leaves the last line blank.
Beats:
1. Friday night above the shop: he balances the controlled-drugs register, then writes the day in the fourteenth green book. On the shelf, the 2031 book still falls open at one page. He asks the counsel about showing Nell, and it is sensible: she starts Wednesday; a few entries, later; ask who they're for. He writes "I'd give them to her" and shelves them. The want is on paper, against counsel.
2. Saturday, the counter: Ina Harcus asks what he'd do about her husband's tablets. He gives her the counsel in his own voice. Hers said the same; she wanted a person to say it. The town trusts him, which is to say the model with his face.
3. Saturday, the Scrabster boat: Nell comes off quicker and thinner. At supper she tells him about her first departure on a ward. She pushed for a scan on a boy and it showed nothing, which cost him a dose of radiation and his mother a night of fear. She doesn't know if she was right. At twenty-three she owes; at fifty he never has.
4. Sunday on Brinkie's Brae: she recalls her audit (an empty page; "You're clean, Dad," said as of a tidy room) and asks whether he ever once disagreed with it. He doesn't answer on the hill.
5. Sunday night: he puts fourteen books on the kitchen table and turns his phone face down. She opens 2027. The departure is made, on her, and cannot be unread.
6. The reading: she laughs (Amy's mother invited for coffee), then stops laughing (the bath door; the car keys and the Pier Head). With his green pen she marks a tick where he saw what it couldn't and a cross where what he'd have done would have hurt her. The town's pages she cannot mark. The seeing gets ticks and the doing gets crosses, and 2039's "I'd" is no better than 2028's. The unlived life acquires a score.
7. One a.m., the swimming page (the passage). He learns he was right, that being right would have been a harm, and that she wanted it anyway.
8. Four a.m.: she reaches Friday's line, reads it twice, looks at him, caps the pen and goes up to sleep. His one departure sits ungraded; only she can grade it, and not yet.
9. Monday: the pier, ordinary sentences, the boat out past the Holms; home to the table as she left it.
The passage:
She had the green pen from the dispensary, the one that doesn't skip, and by one o'clock the margins of 2029 and 2030 were full of her. A tick where I had seen it. A cross where I would have done something about it. She never explained the system. She didn't need to. I have read margins all my life.

Then she turned a page and her hand stopped.

*4 Feb 31. N. (14) has stopped swimming. It says: her body, her decision; don't push; keep the door open. It isn't the swimming. Something at the pool. I'd go up on Tuesday and sit in the gallery.*

"You wrote this on a Tuesday," she said.

"I write them at night."

"It was a girl on the relay. She said one thing in the showers and everybody laughed." She said it the way she'd read me a potassium. "I sat in the changing room every Tuesday for a month. In my costume. Not going through."

I waited. I have become very good at waiting.

"Every Tuesday I thought, if Dad comes up those stairs into the gallery I will die. I'll get out and walk home wet and never speak to him again." She put the cap on the pen. "And every Tuesday I looked up at the gallery."

"It said—"

"I know what it said. I asked mine when I was nineteen. It said you'd done right." She turned the book round so the entry faced me, as if I might not have read it. "You did do right."

The Rayburn ticked. Under the house the tide was coming in over the slip, the way it does, without asking anyone.

"Which is it," I said. "Tick or cross."

She looked at the page a long time. Then she took the cap off the pen and made a tick, and through the tick, slowly, a cross, and turned the book back to herself, and went on reading.
