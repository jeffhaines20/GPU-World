### O-1 — Right, Late
Conceit: A father who never asked the model about his son kept instead, from the year the boy turned two, a notebook a year of predictions: what he expected, how sure he was, what happened. When the son brings his audit at nineteen, it meets this second ledger, which is both harsher than the machine's and kinder. The entry that decides it can be scored only by the son, and he cannot score it either.
Who: Wade Hautala, 48, night dispatcher at the county 911 center in Duluth, Minnesota, March 2040. All shift he reads his model's protocols to strangers; it has never heard his son's name. His son Owen, 19, ships out at dawn as a deckhand on the season's first laker. Wade wants him to leave knowing he was known and not relayed to. He pays with the notebooks, which prove he was also practicing.
Obstacle: The only instrument that can score him is his son. The model knows what a reasonable father would have done, and the notebooks know what Wade expected. Neither knows what happened inside Owen. No apology buys a verdict the son does not have, and the ship leaves at six.
Turn: On the night Owen, seventeen, stayed out, Wade wrote "Skyline lookout. Home by two if nobody comes after him. He has it with him. Conf 80," and scored it "Right, late." Owen's list says: north pier, nearly three hours, the model asking him six times to call his father. For seventeen years Wade has written the next thing down before it happened. Now he asks instead, and he cannot score the answer. At dawn that year's notebook leaves in Owen's sea bag, and the father no longer holds the record of his own best call.
Last image: The kitchen table after the door shuts. The call-log books stand in a row, spines taped OWEN 2023 to OWEN 2040, with a gap where 2038 stood. In the gap, folded in thirds and standing on edge, is Owen's handwritten list, the model's sentences and his son's in the same ink.
Question: Wade put a number on every risk he ran on his son before he could know it, and the numbers got better. The practice also charged a boy who wasn't his, and no one can add up its largest bet. Was he entitled to learn his son this way, or was his education paid from someone else's account?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That whether a parent knew their child is a fact about the parent. Here it is a verdict only the child can give, and the child, made by what he is judging, cannot quite give it.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: A reader of the card expects the son's list to be the bill; instead the father answers with a ledger that convicts him where the list only faulted him (a stranger's boy paid for the theft), vindicates him where the list only conceded, and turns the audit on a night neither record got right. It is earned because the logging habit is planted at the console and the hand-copied list in scene two, and "He has it with him" shows Wade's refusal leaning on the model in his son's pocket.
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché: the machine lists a father's failures, he defends himself or breaks, and they reconcile at the dock or don't. Here the father is not a romantic trusting his gut but an empiricist who priced every bet. The machine's audit is the gentler document. The ending changes not a heart but who holds the evidence.
Closest published: "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling," Ted Chiang. A father checks his memory of a quarrel with his daughter against a lifelog and finds the record against him. There the record is neutral and the subject is memory. Here both records take sides, the question is entitlement, and the deciding fact lies inside the son, where no record reaches.

The passage:
Owen turned the page and stopped, and Wade knew which page it was by how long he stayed there.

"Read yours," Owen said.

Wade did not need the book. "'Eleven March, twenty-three-oh-five. Not home. Script: call him, police at one. Not calling. He's at the Skyline lookout. Home by two if nobody comes after him. He has it with him. Conf eighty.'" He had written it at this table, with the porch light on. "'Outcome, oh-two-forty. Right, late.'"

Owen smoothed the list and found the line with his thumb. He read it in the voice he had used all night for everything on that paper, his and its, so that Wade still could not have said where his son stopped and the other began.

"'North pier, twenty-three-ten to oh-one-fifty. It asked me to call you six times.'"

The furnace came on. Below the hill a truck geared down toward the harbor, and Wade saw the north pier the way he had seen it on the canal cameras a hundred Marches: the ice glazed over the concrete, the light at the end, the black water lifting against the wall.

"Why didn't you call?"

It was, he would think later, the first time in seventeen years that he had asked before he wrote.

"Because you'd have come."

Wade waited for the rest. That was all of it.

"And if I had?"

Owen looked at the window, where there was nothing to see but the kitchen and the two of them in it.

"I don't know."

The pen was uncapped in his hand; he did not remember uncapping it. After Right, late there was room on the line for a word. He held the pen over it the way he held a finger over the transfer key when a caller went quiet, and the clock on the stove went past four, and he did not write anything at all.
