### O-2 — Working Height
Conceit: Every midwife's decisions are scored for concordance with her counsel, the model everyone carries, and the counsel writes the simulator. So the only place to learn when to overrule it is a real woman, who can then audit the lesson with the same model: its cost to the decimal, the student's wanting, and nothing of what she heard.
Who: Noor Brouwer, 24, in her last month of midwifery training in Zierikzee, Zeeland. Her concordance, 97.1, is the province's lowest, and she checks it as often as a runner checks her pace. Her father pilots ships up the Westerschelde and has not overruled his counsel since 2029; he says so at birthdays, as a good thing, and it is. She wants to be a midwife who knows, not one who relays. Her supervisor, Marga Dekker, 65, trained in 1998, retires in January at a concordance of 99.6; Noor was at two of the 0.4: a cord twice round the neck, and nothing.
Obstacle: The counsel is right far more often than she is. The only place to learn whether she is ever right where it is not is a live birth: the simulator is the counsel's, Marga's sense will not go into words, and a woman at eight centimetres, asked to be the test, would say stay.
Turn: On day two Sanne's counsel's review reads Noor's want off her record before she has admitted it, and finds no trace of what she heard. On day eight Sanne, who sent the complaint that morning, asks not whether Noor was right but what she heard. Noor can no longer hear it; she plays ten past two, and Sanne says "Again." The review's clean verdict was all the price bought her; asking, she has begun to spend it.
Last image: Sanne lies at a midwife's working height on the bed still on its blocks, her daughter asleep on her chest, Noor's phone at her ear. Her counsel, asked, has given her the second to listen at and told her, fairly, that nothing is there. Noor watches her listen.
Question: May an apprentice spend a stranger's birth to find out whether her hearing is worth anything, when that is how every midwife who ever outheard the counsel was made, and when the stranger can audit what it cost her and why the apprentice wanted it, but never what she heard?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That the stranger a practitioner learns on is repaid by the practitioner she becomes. In 2040 one nearly as good is on every collar, so a departure buys only someone better than 2026, which no one can price; and an audit reads a departure's wanting off the record, never its hearing, so where the outcome is ambiguous, judgment looks on paper like vanity paid for by a stranger.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: The departure is toward caution, against a counsel right about all it can see; the only evidence, a loose true knot, settles nothing; and what condemns Noor is not her hunch but "Let me" and seven small departures, from which a fair model infers what she has not said. The review is what a 2026 model would write: fair, hedged, blind only to what did not happen.
Not the obvious version: In the obvious story a young midwife overrules the AI and is proved right or wrong. Here nothing is proved, the departure is cautious, and the AI is the auditor, not the antagonist: what it finds is a motive, not an error.
Closest published: Atul Gawande, "The Learning Curve" (2002): novices learn on patients who never agreed, tolerable because they become the surgeons later patients need and the bill stays vague. The story answers him: a good-enough surgeon is on every collar and the bill is exact, so necessity no longer justifies the lesson; what might is a hearing beyond 2026, which cannot be shown. Chris Bohjalian, Midwives (1997): an unverifiable home-birth call becomes a trial after the mother dies; here both live, the call was cautious, the harm a scar priced at three in five.
Beats:
1. Zierikzee, November 2040, a house under the dike: Sanne, a dike inspector, and Joost; first baby; the bed on hired blocks. Eight centimetres, all normal. At 02:10 Noor hears the heart come back after a contraction a fraction late; the counsel hears nothing wrong.
2. The landing, 02:14. The counsel, patient and fair: no indication; transfer risks a birth on the bridge; can she articulate a concern? "No." Marga listens through two contractions: "I didn't hear it." Noor thinks of January. "Let me." "Your call." All on the record; none of it names the want.
3. She tells them. Their counsels say what hers says. "Why?" "I can't tell you." Sanne agrees because a professional is afraid.
4. The Zeelandbrug at 02:40: five kilometres of lamps over black water, Sanne on all fours on the stretcher, the counsel updating the odds of a birth on the bridge. There is none. The contractions space out: the danger she chose passes, another begins.
5. Goes. Labour stops at eight centimetres; section at 07:40. The obstetrician lifts the cord: a true knot, loose. Noor photographs it. Apgar nine and ten. Marga looks at the photograph and says only, "Keep that."
6. Day two: Sanne's counsel's review. Probably avoidable, about three in five; every future birth in hospital; the knot "not evidence that transfer was needed"; "Let me" and "Your call" quoted; Noor's seven departures this year listed, all small; "consistent with a training decision." A complaint drafted. The reader rereads the night.
7. Every night Noor plays ten past two, and what she heard drains out of it. Her father sends a heart and Goes's section statistics, which are good.
8. Day eight: the passage, then the last image.
The passage:
The bed was still on its blocks. Joost said it was easier for the stitches, getting up, so Sanne lay at the height Noor would have worked at, and Noor stood beside it with nothing to do with her hands.

"I sent it this morning," Sanne said. "Mine drafted it. It was very fair."

"I read it."

"'Let me.'" Sanne said it quietly, as it had been said. "Fourteen minutes past two. That's you. You asked her."

"Yes."

"It says I was probably a training decision."

The photograph of the knot was on the same phone, behind the recording. The review had given it a paragraph. Noor did not offer it.

"What did you hear?"

The baby slept face down on Sanne's chest, one fist under her cheek. Noor had listened to ten past two perhaps four hundred times. The first night she had heard it after every contraction: the heart coming back a fraction late, like someone answering to her name a moment after it was called. By Friday she heard it one time in five. Since Sunday she had not heard it at all, and she had stopped asking the counsel, because it gave the same answer each time, patiently, and the patience was worse.

She set the phone on the duvet and pressed play.

The room filled with a heart, fast and soft, a horse going hard on sand. Then Sanne's own breath from that night, lower, dragging, the contraction building under it; the gallop slowing; the gallop coming back.

Noor heard a heart.

Sanne did not look at the phone. She kept her eyes on her daughter's head, and her hand lay flat on the small back and rose when it rose.

It ended. Noor reached for the phone.

"Again," Sanne said.

## What changed
Reviewer-a was right that Marga's kitchen confession is a stock reveal in an over-full plan, so it is cut. Her history is now background detail (a 99.6 concordance; Noor was at two of the 0.4) and two words at Goes, "Keep that." The story now opens on the hearing, not the want. Reviewer-b was right that the 02:14 line put the thesis in a frightened mouth. Noor now says only "No" and "Let me." The fair review infers her want from her record, which makes a sharper claim: an audit sees motive, never hearing. Her father, a pilot who stopped departing in 2029, gives the want a source and gives Noor a life outside work. Closest published now names Gawande and Bohjalian and answers Gawande. The flagged prose habits are gone, and Sanne now asks what Noor heard. I refused to resolve the ending, which would make it safer. "Again" stays, and Sanne's own counsel pointing her to the second is new. The outline survives.
