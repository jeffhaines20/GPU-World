### O-3 — Undispensed
Conceit: A pharmacist has not once gone against the counsel since it stopped improving; each night he writes down what he would have done instead. A judgment never acted on has no outcome, so only the daughter it would have been practised on can grade it. The week he asks her to, her prescription is in his drawer.
Who: Magnus Flett, 50, pharmacist on the main street of Stromness, Orkney, 2040; "clean" by the town's word and by his daughter's audit at nineteen. Thora, 23, is home for two nights before she starts as a junior doctor in Aberdeen. He wants her to mark his fourteen green notebooks: to learn whether, all along, someone was there.
Obstacle: No model can score a road not taken; only Thora knows what was true of Thora. And asking her is live: a script for her sertraline came to his shop on Friday, and he had not known. The counsel answers both well: she chose where to send it, not what to tell you; the books, not this week, and ask who they're for.
Turn: He gives her the books against the counsel: his first departure in fourteen years, made on a daughter whose sertraline is in his drawer, the week she starts on the wards. She opens the last book first, reads Friday's line, says "You could have asked," and grades the other thirteen anyway. The grade splits: he saw her truly, what he'd have done would mostly have hurt her, and at fourteen she wanted it done anyway.
Last image: Nine on Monday; the boat went at half six. In the F drawer, her white bag: twenty-eight sertraline with her name on them. His phone, face up for the first time since Sunday, tells him, correctly, that she can get an emergency supply in Aberdeen.
Question: Was fourteen years of fidelity the humility that spared her an apprentice's mistakes, or clean hands kept at her expense? Is the night he takes from her, her tablets in his drawer, his first judgment or a vanity she pays for?
What the reader assumed and no longer does: That practising on someone is settled by asking them. Only she can grade the life he didn't impose, and her grade splits because she was split: every Tuesday she dreaded him on the gallery stairs and looked for him. The one who paid can price the harm, not the entitlement.
Why it cannot be predicted, and why it is earned: The reader braces for a hollow man exposed or a follower who breaks free; instead the departure comes early, already suspected of vanity, she reads his last line first, and the price turns up not in her margins but in the collection drawer. Earned: the drawer, "later," the face-down phone and the half-six boat come first; the 2031 book falls open in scene one.
Not the obvious version: The man who obeyed the machine is audited by his child, found empty, and makes one choice of his own: liberation, or Ivan Ilyich's verdict. Here he was never empty, his departure is early and suspect, the grade clears neither fidelity nor man, and the end is a cost nobody chose alone. Not atrophy: the question is not whether his judgment grew but whether he was entitled to find out on her.
Closest published: Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, fidelity to an authority judged late as humility or abdication; but Stevens's master was wrong and his lost life a love, while Magnus's counsel was mostly right and his unlived life, written nightly, is graded in a weekend by the one it would have fallen on. Scott Alexander's "The Whispering Earring": is anyone left inside a man who always takes perfect counsel? Here the counsel is fallible, and the books say yes.
Beats:
1. Friday, closing: Thora's script comes from Aberdeen, sertraline 100 mg. He makes it up himself, puts it in the F drawer, and asks the counsel about it and about the books. He balances the controlled-drugs register and writes the day in the fourteenth green book, ending "I'd give her the books." On the shelf the 2031 book still falls open at one page.
2. Saturday: while he gives Ina Harcus the counsel in his own voice, Thora comes in off the Scrabster boat, quicker and thinner, and says, passing the counter, that she'll get her prescription later. Neither names it.
3. Sunday, Brinkie's Brae: her first departure, as a student: a scan on a boy that showed nothing, his dose, his mother's night. Her audit at nineteen ("You're clean, Dad," as of a tidy room). Did he ever once disagree with it? He doesn't answer on the hill.
4. Sunday night: fourteen books on the kitchen table; he turns his phone face down. She opens 2040 first and reads Friday. "You could have asked." "It said to wait for you." "It would." She opens 2027.
5. The reading, in his green dispensing pen: a tick where he saw what the counsel couldn't, a cross where what he'd have done would have hurt her. She laughs (Amy's mother invited for coffee), then stops (the bath door; the car keys and the Pier Head). Once she checks a page with her own counsel, which knows her better than he does. The town's pages she cannot mark.
6. One a.m., the swimming page (the passage): he was right, being right would have been a harm, and she wanted it anyway.
7. Four a.m.: she caps the pen on 2039, says "Half six," and goes up. He sits with the marked books and doesn't turn his phone over.
8. Monday: the pier at six, ordinary sentences, the boat out past the Holms. Neither mentions the drawer. At nine he unlocks the shop.
The passage:
She had the green pen from the dispensary, the one that doesn't skip, and by one o'clock the margins of 2029 and 2030 were full of her. A tick where I had seen it. A cross where I would have done something about it. She never explained the system. She didn't need to; I've spent thirty years checking other people's working. 2040 lay face down at her elbow, where she'd put it after Friday.

Then she turned a page and her hand stopped.

*4 Feb 31. T. (14) has stopped swimming. It says: her body, her decision; don't push; keep the door open. It isn't the swimming. Something at the pool. I'd go up on Tuesday and sit in the gallery.*

"You wrote this on a Tuesday," she said.

"I write them at night."

"It was a girl on the relay. She said one thing in the showers and everybody laughed." She said it to the page. "I sat in the changing room every Tuesday for a month. In my costume. Not going through."

I kept my hands on the table.

"Every Tuesday I thought, if Dad comes up those stairs into the gallery I will die. I'll get out and walk home wet and never speak to him again." She put the cap on the pen. "And every Tuesday I looked up at the gallery."

"It said—"

"I know what it said. I asked mine when I was nineteen. It said you'd done right." She turned the book round so the entry faced me, as if I might not have read it. "You did do right."

"Which is it," I said. "Tick or cross."

She looked at the page a long time. Then she took the cap off the pen and made a tick, and through the tick, slowly, a cross, and turned the book back to herself, and went on reading.

## What changed
Both reviewers were right that nothing was risked on the page. Keeping the frame, as routed, I added a live case: on Friday Thora's sertraline script reaches his shop, and "I'd give her the books" is his answer, so the books are now a departure on a daughter breakable this week. She reads the last book first, so the whole night is live, and the price lands where no one chose it: the half-six boat goes, the bag stays in the drawer. Reviewer-b was right about capacity drift; the 2039/2028 line is cut. Reviewer-a was right that the blank line restated beat 8; it is gone, and the ending no longer shares O-1's. I refused to end on the tick-through-cross: that keeps the price on paper. Named Ishiguro and Alexander; Nell is Thora; cut the Stevens line, the ambient-sound cut and the potassium simile. It survives.
