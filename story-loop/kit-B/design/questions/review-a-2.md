importance: The intimate register — apology, condolence, the thing you say once and live with — is where most lives turn, and the card correctly notes the equality makes the loss total rather than class-bound.
rigour: The signalling chain (free to fake, therefore uninformative) is clean economics, but step (5) smuggles in a large empirical claim — that a culture "read sincerity off prose" as its primary instrument — which is historically shaky for most people, most of whom exchanged very few composed letters.

Q-11 | importance=4 | rigour=3 | novelty=5 | arguability=4 | story=5
importance: It is the card that explains how inequality and inheritance re-enter a world engineered to abolish them, which is a societal-scale claim about property, estates and class, not a niche.
rigour: The card is admirably honest that "fittedness compounds" is its load-bearing assumption, but it rests on that assumption twice over — that fourteen years of context is worth more than a house, and that a context is portable enough to price — and the premise's "never much better" pushes against both.
novelty: The inalienable share casting an alienable shadow, and the heir paying her whole allotted mind to host a dead man, is a genuinely new turn on inheritance that I have not seen in the AI commentary.
arguability: A reader can defend both "loading your father is keeping him" and "it is self-eviction," though the card's own objection (contexts do not transfer; the market is thin) tilts toward deflation slightly harder than the card admits.
story: Ines, who has not opened her own engine in three months, and the carpenter whose clients have noticed which days are which, are both crises with a visible daily price.
weakest step: The leap from "a taught context is more useful" to "a context is the most valuable thing a person can sell or leave" — a scale claim with no argument, only the assertion that it compounds, in a world where the ceiling is fixed and every buyer already has fourteen years of their own.

Q-25 | importance=5 | rigour=4 | novelty=5 | arguability=4 | story=4
importance: Isolation as an instrument — of punishment, coercive control, discipline and religious formation — failing everywhere at once is as large as social implications get, and the loss of found solitude touches how selves are made.
rigour: Step (2) does real work and the card names it, but the rebuttal is better than the card claims: it argues the coercive uses fail because they work by monopolizing interpretation, which is a strong and independent line, though it overreaches in saying the man in the hole is thereby not broken.
novelty: Reclassifying solitude from condition to act, and noticing that the cell's silence now "holds something that would answer," is far outside the loneliness-companion commentary the contest list would produce.
arguability: The trade — the end of the hole against the end of accidental aloneness — is real, but the card slightly stacks it, since almost no reader will actually argue that solitary confinement was worth preserving; the live disagreement is over whether the loss of found solitude is real at all.
story: The Carthusian novice building a cell by hand is vivid and thematically exact, but she is closer to an emblem than a person with a want and a price; the nurse in the parked car is the more ordinary crisis and is given only a paragraph.
weakest step: The claim that the abusive husband's isolation "stops working" because an interpreter cannot be fired — the card's own objection (fear and money, not a lack of second opinions, is what keeps her) is not answered, only restated as being about interpretation.

Q-16 | importance=5 | rigour=3 | novelty=4 | arguability=5 | story=4
importance: Whether a polity that cannot change its advisor can change its mind is a first-order question about legitimacy and the possibility of political novelty, and it applies to every voter at once.
rigour: The severe problem is step (1)'s stipulation that dispositions are fixed and uniform: 2026 models are notoriously framing-sensitive and sycophantic, so the same engine gives the polarized public polarized answers, and the card waves this away by asserting "shared boundaries, not conclusions" without showing why boundaries survive what conclusions do not.
novelty: Jefferson's dead hand rather than Orwell's telescreen is a genuinely fresh framing, and the Planck's-principle move — consensus has always died with its holders, and this one does not — is the sharpest sentence in the batch.
arguability: The freest-consent-ever reading is seriously defensible against the co-author reading, and a thoughtful reader could finish the card undecided.
story: The organizer whose opponent is the default answer is a clear crisis with a want and an unreachable adversary, though "cannot debate it, unseat it, or wait for it to die" is a position more than a place, and the man voting no is a vignette rather than a person.
weakest step: The unargued jump from "most people take the default answer" to a single shared window of the reasonable, when a model that mirrors the asker's framing yields as many windows as there are askers — amplification, not a frozen center.

Q-09 | importance=4 | rigour=2 | novelty=4 | arguability=4 | story=4
importance: If the wage splits into a uniform rent and a small human premium, that is the whole structure of work and of what an income means, and it lands on every adult.
rigour: This is the weakest-founded card in the batch: it needs the rent to be large, which it assumes outright ("I assume the rent is large"), and it needs the premium to be small, which contradicts the premise's own logic that a fixed-ceiling 2026 model is worth little without direction, taste and verification — and it also needs the pooling prohibition to be permeable by contract, a step it grants itself in a sentence.
novelty: Paine's universal proprietorship delivered by accident and received as an insult is a strong, unfamiliar frame, and the two-line payslip is a good concrete image; the eight-hour-engine-day union demand is the best invented detail here.

story: Tomasz, kept on full salary with no duties because the firm was never paying for him, is a real humiliation with a price, though "cannot say what he does for a living" is a familiar redundancy beat.
weakest step: The uniform-rent conclusion depends on engine-hours being separable from the person who holds them, but the same card's premise says coordinated cognition needs direction and verification — so the firm is buying a directed engine, and the differential never leaves the hand.

Q-05 | importance=5 | rigour=4 | novelty=5 | arguability=5 | story=5
importance: Converting ignorance from a condition into an act rewrites the moral standing of every "I didn't know" in a marriage, a family, a diagnosis and a workplace, which is as close to a whole-life question as the batch gets.
rigour: It follows tightly from the granted premise and nothing stronger — no superhuman model is needed, only a competent one that draws inferences from what it is told — and the card correctly locates and names its own soft joint at (3)–(4).
novelty: "Did you know?" acquiring a third answer, "I didn't ask," and the inversion that the change is in the ownership of one's own opacity rather than the world's, is the freshest move in the batch and nowhere near the surveillance cliché.
arguability: Duty-to-ask versus not-asking-as-the-last-costly-act-of-trust is a genuine two-sided question, and the objection that trust was never ignorance is strong enough to flip a reader mid-card.
story: A woman whose eleven-year discipline of not-asking is exposed by a gentle detective's question, and who will never know which act she was performing, is the best-shaped crisis here — want, price, place and an ending she cannot resolve.
weakest step: Step (3)'s claim that the asking itself constitutes suspicion, which assumes the question stays legible as an accusation rather than normalizing into a lookup, exactly as the card concedes.
