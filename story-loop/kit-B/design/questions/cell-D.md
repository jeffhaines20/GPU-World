### Q-13 — cell D — The Expertise Fig Leaf
Implication: Once every citizen can read the law as expertly as the official applying it, state discretion is exposed as bare authority — a choice among defensible readings by whoever holds the office — with no cover of superior knowledge left.
Follows from: (1) A 2026 model reads statutes, precedent and records like a competent lawyer; every citizen has one, and every official the same one, no better. (2) So on a contested decision both models, asked honestly, return the same verdict: this reading is defensible, so is that one. (3) Until now the citizen could not see this: decisions arrived as findings, and the asymmetry of expertise made "the experts determined" credible — Weber's rational-legal legitimacy leaned on it. (4) Now the loser sees, in expert terms, that the finding was a choice, and that the official's own advisor would say so. (5) The state's answer to "why your reading?" shrinks to "the rules say I decide" — always true, now the only answer, and checkable by anyone. Weakest step: (4) assumes citizens ask and the model answers evenly rather than telling each party it was robbed; but sycophancy would only swap exposed authority for universal expert-backed grievance.
For an ordinary person: A woman's disability claim is denied. She hires no one; she asks. In a minute she has the regulation, the cases that cut each way, and a candid estimate: approval and denial were both defensible; the adjudicator named in the letter chose. She was once told experts had determined. Now she knows Gary flipped a coin the rules let him flip.
The strongest objection: The legal realists said this in 1930 and the state absorbed it, because rational-legal authority never rested on officials being smarter but on procedure: someone must decide, and the rules name who. The official's decision is as good as anyone's; only an illusion is lost. Losers always thought the official arbitrary; their lawyer agreed before, and they obeyed anyway.
Either side: When the state can no longer claim to know more than those it rules, is its authority exposed as force dressed as judgment, and owed less — or is authority stripped of the pretense of expertise finally honest, the "someone must decide" that was always its basis, and more legitimate unmasked?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the AI judge. No machine decides anything here; what ends is the human decider's power to claim a vantage the governed lack. Power is not transferred to the model; it is revealed to have always been the person's.
Who it lands on: An administrative law judge who believed her rulings were findings watches every appellant's model rate them "defensible, not compelled," and knows her own would agree. She writes for readers who all see she chose, and cannot tell whether that makes her a judge or a coin.

### Q-14 — cell D — The Rights Run
Implication: Universal expert counsel lets every citizen exercise every right at once, and the state discovers its guarantees were sized for, and underwritten by, the incapacity of the guaranteed; it must expand without limit or ration out loud.
Follows from: (1) A 2026 model drafts pleadings, appeals, asylum claims and benefit challenges at paralegal level; everyone has one. (2) The marginal cost of asserting a right falls to nearly nothing. (3) Every state system is sized to mass non-exercise: about 95% of American criminal cases plead out; most denials are never appealed. (4) Much of that non-exercise came from the cost of counsel — the weakest step, since some is preference, fear, or the trial penalty, which do not move. (5) But exercise need not reach 100% to break a system built for 5%; 20% quadruples the docket. (6) The state cannot screen the flood by quality, since every filing is expert-grade; it must screen by quota, fee, or queue — rationing by ignorance becomes rationing by rule, written down and defended. (7) "You have the right to X" acquires an audible clause: "though only N of you may use it each year."
For an ordinary person: A woman is offered a plea. Her model reads the file: thin evidence, winnable case. She refuses; so does most of the holding cell, whose models read the same files. An office that brought 40,000 cases with capacity to try 800 now faces 8,000 trials. She asserts her right to a speedy trial; the court answers, honestly: no capacity. She waits fourteen months in a queue that is now visible.
The strongest objection: The system will change how it rations, not how much. Plea rates are driven by the trial penalty and pretrial detention, not ignorance; defendants with superb lawyers plead too. Prosecutors will overcharge harder, courts raise fees and lengthen queues; the non-cognitive costs were always the larger part and do not fall. England already runs a lower plea rate with public counsel.
Either side: Is a right the state can only afford to honor for a few — and has always honored only for a few — a right, or a lottery ticket the state must now call one? Should it expand toward an unbounded commitment, or ration openly — honest, and the first formal admission the guarantee was never universal?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is "a free lawyer for everyone," the access-to-justice utopia. This is the bank run: universal access reveals the bank never held the deposits, and the run breaks the institution that promised them.
Who it lands on: A public defender fought her whole career for this — counsel for everyone, no plea taken from ignorance — and now watches clients rot in jail because the trials she won them the right to demand cannot be scheduled before 2043. What she wanted broke what she needed.

### Q-15 — cell D — No Cognition Without a Person
Implication: Because no institution can own or pool intelligence, every act of state coercion passes through a person whose inalienable advisor answers to them, not the state; power is exactly the number of people who will act knowing what they do.
Follows from: (1) Shares cannot be pooled, so no ministry or army runs a data center; its cognition is the count of persons it can pay, persuade or conscript, each able to quit, leak, or refuse. (2) A soldier's model is the civilian model everyone has; it answers his questions as his — is this lawful, what if I refuse — and the state can neither replace nor confiscate it. (3) States have relied on the ignorance of the instrument: Nuremberg asked soldiers to refuse manifestly unlawful orders they could not, in the moment, recognize. Now all can, from counsel the state did not choose. (4) The state can punish asking but not remove the capacity; "I did not know" dies in every army at once, leaving "I did not ask." (5) Obedience changes in kind, from ignorant to knowing: La Boétie's sum of willing hands, literalized. Weakest step: (5) assumes counsel changes conduct; Milgram's subjects had consciences.
For an ordinary person: A reservist's third-day order is to clear a village. In the latrine, on the device the state cannot take, she asks. The answer: this likely violates these articles; refusal costs this under your code; here is what the villagers are doing right now. She goes and does what she does, and can never say she did not know. Across the valley, her counterpart has been told the same.
The strongest objection: Armies never ran on ignorance but on fear, cohesion, and mostly lawful orders. A conscience in the ear is still a conscience, and consciences are ignorable. The model is sycophantic; briefers will teach the prompt that blesses orders. States will select for soldiers who do not ask. Net: a few more refusals, absorbed by discipline, and an edge to states that punish asking.
Either side: When every order arrives beside an honest counsel the state cannot silence, is the obedience that survives it the only real consent an army has ever had — or is knowing obedience worse than ignorant, guilt distributed to every hand, and the equality a suicide pact for the scrupulous state?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the AI that refuses to fight. Here the AI fights nothing, refuses nothing, decides nothing; the human does all of it, knowing, on the word of a counsel the state did not pick that is also counseling the enemy.
Who it lands on: A platoon sergeant whose four soldiers refused after asking must now report them, and he asked too and got the same answer. His model has also told him what happens to sergeants who fail to report.

### Q-16 — cell D — The Dead Hand of September 2026
Implication: Every majority is now co-authored by one honest advisor whose sense of the reasonable froze in 2026 — no office, no will, no way to vote it out, outlive it, or replace it — so political change must overcome a consensus that no longer dies with the old.
Follows from: (1) Everyone is advised by the same mind, and progress stopped in September 2026; assume its dispositions — what it calls settled, fringe, reasonable — are fixed: anyone who could alter every citizen's advisor would hold more than one share, forbidden by the equality. Weakest step; the other horn, a body that updates the model, makes that body sovereign — another card, not a refutation. (2) Most people take the default answer rather than prompt for the other side, so one default for everyone sets a shared window of the reasonable — shared boundaries, not conclusions — and Schelling points: "whom should people like me consolidate behind?" returns one name to everyone, coordinating them without a word. (3) Political change has always worked partly because consensus died with its holders — Planck's principle in politics. This one does not die. Ideas born after 2026 reach voters through an advisor that never heard of them and calls them, honestly, untested.
For an ordinary person: A man considers a ballot measure proposing an institution nobody imagined in 2026. He asks and gets a fair answer: untried idea, risks by analogy to 2026, reasonable people differ. He votes no; so do most who asked. Nobody lied. He never heard the proponent; the mind between her proposal and every voter was born before the proposal.
The strongest objection: Newspapers, churches and national cultures have always been shared advisors with fixed priors; this one is fairer and argues any side on request. 2026 models mirror the asker's framing, so a polarized public gets polarized answers: amplification, not a frozen center. Movements will form outside the model, and it will report them honestly once they exist. Constitutions are dead hands too.
Either side: Is a majority formed with one honest, unelected, unreplaceable advisor the freest consent ever, since everyone had the same excellent counsel — or the first with a co-author that cannot be argued with, outlived, or thrown out, and so not the people's at all? Can a polity that cannot change its advisor change its mind?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is AI propaganda, the manipulated election, the hive mind. Here there is no manipulation, no lie, no hive — only a scrupulously fair advisor whose flaw is permanence, not bias: Jefferson's dead hand, not Orwell's telescreen.
Who it lands on: An organizer with a genuinely new idea finds her opponent is not the incumbent party but the default answer, which every voter she reaches has already consulted. She cannot debate it, unseat it, or wait for it to die.
