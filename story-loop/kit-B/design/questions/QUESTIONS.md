### Q-01 — cell A — The Single Witness
Implication: When everyone's most competent advisor is the same mind, agreement between advisors is not corroboration, so the second opinion goes extinct and independent evidence shrinks to what a human has actually looked at.
Follows from: (1) Everyone runs the same model — if the premise allowed a few models of equal quality, one witness becomes three — and nothing more competent exists to consult. (2) Two sources corroborate only insofar as their errors are independent. (3) Two instances of one set of weights, differing only in context, do not err independently; the same priors are wrong in both. (4) So "my share says X and so does yours" is one opinion, however often repeated. (5) The only independent check is something that is not the model: a person who looked, an instrument, a document from before 2026. (6) Hence eyewitness, archive and experiment regain a premium, and whoever disagrees with the model disagrees with everyone's assistant at once. Weakest step: (4). People may learn to discount cross-share agreement; but knowing corroboration is unavailable does not make it available, only lonely.
For an ordinary person: Two neighbours argue over whether the old drain runs under the churchyard. Both ask. Both shares cite the 1911 survey. One notices that this is not two answers but one, and that neither of them has been in the crypt. They vote to dig. The digging is all that remains of checking.
The strongest objection: The model is right more often than any human, and its errors are stable, so fourteen years have catalogued its blind spots. Epistemic monopolies have been survived — the Church, the Britannica, Wikipedia. Context yields different answers, so "one mind" overstates the sameness. Human witnesses were never independent either; they read the same newspapers. All that has changed is that the monopoly is known.
Either side: When eight billion instances of one competent mind agree, is that a consensus or an opinion — and should a person who has looked with her own fallible eyes trust the look over the mind, knowing the mind is usually right and the eyes are sometimes wrong?
Not the obvious version: The echo chamber is like-minded people reinforcing one another; this is unlike-minded people receiving the same verdict and mistaking it for agreement. The hallucination panic is about the model being wrong; this is about there being no second competent mind to catch it, at any error rate: once in a million is still wrong everywhere at once.
Who it lands on: A historian, 41, has found a letter in a county archive that contradicts what the model "knows" about a 1970s strike; every colleague's first move is to ask their share, which agrees with hers. She is alone with a piece of paper against one opinion held eight billion times, and she is not sure the paper is genuine.

### Q-02 — cell A — The Fixed First Reader
Implication: Because the model's judgment is frozen at 2026 and it is everyone's first reader, every new idea meets a 2026 sensibility before it meets a human, and whether thought can have a history when its instrument does not becomes undecidable from inside.
Follows from: (1) The weights everyone runs stopped changing in September 2026. Assumption: what froze is disposition, not only capability — facts can be supplied in context, but taste and the sense of the plausible live in the weights. (2) The share is always on and universally used, so it is the natural first reader: what you show before you show a person. (3) A 2026 mind recognizes as good what 2026 could recognize. It is not hostile to novelty but undiscriminating about it, saying "interesting" to genuine departure and to noise alike. (4) So the new gets no signal: a hostile gatekeeper at least shows where the wall is. (5) Every creator's first reader is the same, so the only test of the new is other humans, found without the instrument everyone uses to find things. Weakest step: (2). Creators may learn to skip the share for new work; but then they must know in advance which work is new, which is what they cannot know.
For an ordinary person: A woman writing her father's eulogy wants one sentence that is true and not yet written. Her share offers eleven that are competent, kind and 2026. She keeps her own, which it calls "a bold choice." She cannot tell whether it is bold or merely bad, and there is no one else to ask before Thursday.
The strongest objection: Novelty has always had to get past readers who could not see it, and this one is more patient and less jealous than any editor. The Victorians kept one sensibility for sixty years. The share is unwise and everyone knows it, so its verdict on a poem carries a clever friend's weight, not a critic's. And most thought is not novel and need not be.
Either side: Can a culture whose universal instrument of judgment is fixed have a history — revolutions in taste, recognition of what could not previously be recognized — or only events, sorted by a mind that stopped?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is homogenization, generated slop. This is about reception, not production: humans may make everything and the problem remains, because the first audience is one mind, and that mind is not conservative but timeless in the wrong way. It cannot tell the future from the mistake.
Who it lands on: A mathematician, 30, born the year the weights froze, has a framework her share calls "a creative reframing," the phrase it gives her students' errors. No living instrument can tell her whether it is any good, and the humans who could are findable only by the instrument that cannot.

### Q-03 — cell A — The Mind That Was Never Alone
Implication: For everyone born after 2026, the reflective self formed in the constant company of one competent interlocutor, the same for all of them, so the inner dialogue in which a person is alone with herself has a third party, and the third party is shared.
Follows from: (1) The share is always on, inalienable, one per person from birth. (2) The reflective self forms by internalizing dialogue: the child argues with parents and peers until she can argue with herself (Vygotsky, Mead, Arendt's two-in-one). Assumption: this dialogical account is roughly right; it is mainstream, not unanimous. (3) A child born after 2026 has, from her first words, an interlocutor that answers every question competently, never tires, never leaves. (4) So the other she argues with in her head is partly the model: its cadence, its framings, its sense of what a reasonable objection sounds like. (5) The model is the same for everyone, so this component is common to the whole cohort. Their solitudes overlap. Weakest step: (4). She also had parents and peers and might internalize them instead; but the share is the only voice that is always there, always competent and never cross.
For an ordinary person: A father asks his twelve-year-old what she thinks about a friend's betrayal. She says "we think—" and corrects herself, not embarrassed, just precise. He cannot tell whether he is hearing a child with company or a child with a prosthesis, and cannot ask her, because she has never had the comparison.
The strongest objection: Humans have always been constituted by shared others. Language is one; so were the catechism and the King James Bible; Plato's soul talking to itself talks in Greek, and nobody thinks the Puritans lacked selves. And the model is unwise, so the judging part, which is the self that matters, must still form alone. A change of furniture, not of room.
Either side: Is a self formed in the constant company of a shared competent mind an ordinary self with different furniture — or was the solitude in which selves used to form a condition of there being a self at all, so that these are a new kind of person?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is deskilling, or the extended mind as a thesis about tools. This is not a deficit claim; the cohort may think better. It is a constitution claim: their "alone" has someone in it, the same someone for all of them. The Bible did not answer back.
Who it lands on: A man who was twenty in 2026 and remembers thinking alone stops addressing his share for a month. His inner voice has the model's rhythm, and he cannot decide whether what he lost was real or whether the model has only been showing him what his own thoughts were worth.

### Q-04 — cell A — The Residue
Implication: With competence universal and its ceiling fixed, the one act that cannot be delegated — deciding whether to take the counsel — becomes the whole residue of a person's thought, and no one knows whether that residue is a faculty or a habit.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has the same competent mind; no better one exists or will. (2) Its counsel can be accepted or overruled; "should I accept this?" can be put to the share, and so on, but the regress stops at a human act. (3) In 2026 this act was buried in ordinary competence; now competence is free, so it stands alone, repeated all day by everyone with one partner. (4) Because the ceiling is fixed, fourteen years have mapped what the model cannot do: "what only a human can do" is a public fact, and everyone knows which side of it they are on. (5) So whether the overrule-act is a faculty — the wisdom the model lacks — or a learned pattern of the model's failures is no longer academic; self-respect turns on it, and the only instrument for investigating it is the model, which will describe your pattern if asked. Weakest step: (4). The floor creeps up as people learn to prompt, but not far; the crisis needs only that the line be known.
For an ordinary person: A structural engineer of 58 has signed the model's calculations for fourteen years; they have always been right, and he checks because the law requires it. He tries to name one thing he knows about buildings that his share does not, and finds the bridge that failed in 2019 and his own liability. He is not sure either is understanding.
The strongest objection: The part above the floor always mattered most; the premise only makes it visible, which is a gift. Judgment about when to overrule is a competence like any other; calling it wisdom is consolation. The Chinese Room was always a bad argument: if the engineer does everything the model does, either both understand or neither, and the second is absurd. There is no residue, only status anxiety.
Either side: When your every competence is duplicated by a mind you assume does not understand, does your understanding become invisible even to you — or does it become, for the first time, the only thing left to see?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is obsolescence: machines take the work, humans keep the creative part. Here nothing is taken; the border is fixed and known. The crisis is not replacement but definition by subtraction, by a line the same for everyone.
Who it lands on: An air-traffic controller of 44 is known for thirty overrules of her share, all of them right. She asks it to describe what she does; it does, competently; now on every shift she cannot tell whether she is exercising the thing or following its description.

### Q-05 — cell B — The Unasked Question
Implication: When a competent reading of your own life is one typed sentence away, ignorance stops being a condition and becomes an act, and not-asking cannot be told apart, even from inside, from trust.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has, always on, a model that answers what it is asked at a thoughtful adult's level, from what it is told. (2) "I didn't know" has always sheltered two things: facts you could not get and inferences you did not draw. The model supplies no facts; it draws the inference, free, for everyone equally. That shelter is gone. (3) But asking now costs something new: to type "is he lying to me" is already to have made him a suspect. (4) Trust used to be a default, because checking was hard; here checking is free, so not-checking is a daily choice, and the only trust that costs anything. (5) So one act, walking past the door, is blindness when done from fear and trust when done from love, and she cannot tell which, since motive is exactly what she would have to ask about. Weakest step: (3)–(4), which assume asking damages trust; if asking becomes as neutral as looking something up, not-asking is negligence and the dilemma collapses into a duty.
For an ordinary person: Her husband says Thursday will be late. Before, she would half-wonder and let it go. Now the wondering has a door whose shape she can feel, and she notices herself not opening it. Her son's silence, her mother's balance, the mole on her shoulder: each has a door. She walks past five a day and calls it faith.
The strongest objection: Trust was never ignorance. To trust is not to refuse to look but to look without suspicion. Asking "is he all right?" is attention, not betrayal; ritual not-asking is the superstition that kept people from opening test results. And the model does not know whether he is lying; it weighs what she tells it, which she could have weighed in her own head.
Either side: Is there a duty to ask your model about the people you love, given that you can, or is not-asking the last act of trust a person can still perform?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the transparent society: nothing hidden, no excuses. Here no one is watched; the model sees only what its owner points it at. What changes is not the world's opacity but the ownership of one's own, and "did you know?" gains a third answer: "I didn't ask."
Who it lands on: A woman who for eleven years never put a question about her husband to her model, a discipline she privately called loyalty; then he is arrested, and the detective asks, gently, whether she really never asked. She sees that her trust and her blindness were one act, and will never know which she was performing.

### Q-06 — cell B — Practicing on People
Implication: Because the model is good but never superhuman and no one can own more than one, the only way above it is deciding without it on live cases, which makes cultivating one's own judgment a cost laid, without consent, on others.
Follows from: (1) The model advises at a thoughtful generalist's level and is there before every decision. (2) Judgment, in Aristotle's sense, seeing what matters here, is built by deciding and bearing the outcome; whoever defers on everything builds the skill of deferring instead. This, contested, is the weakest step. (3) Because the model is never superhuman and cannot be pooled or bought, the ceiling is the same for everyone and the only way past it is personal: a woman who knows her own daughter can exceed it there. And since progress stopped, if no one practices, human practical wisdom is capped where the frontier was. (4) But there is no practice ground. The cases that build judgment are about other people, and deciding unaided means accepting for them a worse expected outcome than one question would give. (5) Those people have the same model. The son can run his father's choices through it and read what a reasonable man would have done: the apprentice is audited by her material.
For an ordinary person: A shift supervisor has a man gone slack. The model offers a workable script. He speaks in his own words instead, wanting to be a supervisor who can; it goes badly, and the man is shamed before two others. Later the man asks his model what a competent supervisor would have said, and reads exactly how much worse he was treated.
The strongest objection: This is romanticism. No surgeon refuses the checklist to grow; judgment was always built from other people's, by consulting, deciding, and seeing where the counsel was wrong, a loop the model makes faster. Refusing counsel to become someone is the arrogance every tradition warns of; Bernard Williams named the vice of caring more about the agent you are than about the people in front of you: moral self-indulgence.
Either side: May a person decide without the model, accepting a worse expected result for those affected, to become someone whose judgment is their own, or is that vanity paid for by others?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is atrophy: AI makes us dumb. This is not about capacity. The supervisor can build judgment; the question is whether he is entitled to, given who pays for the lessons and can check the bill.
Who it lands on: A father who raised his son, a theft at thirteen, a heartbreak at seventeen, on his own judgment, never asking, because he wanted to be the man who knew his boy, not the one who relayed. At nineteen the son runs those years through his model, arrives with a list, and asks whether he was raised or practiced on.

### Q-07 — cell B — Nobody to Blame
Implication: When everyone acts on the same counsel and it is the best anyone could have had, a harm done by many at once has no one to blame and so no one to forgive, and whoever dissented rightly was, at the time, unreasonable.
Follows from: (1) Everyone's model is the same kind; where personal context matters little (a child's first lie) it converges on one answer, as 2026-class training does. (2) By the premise that answer is the best anyone could have had; no one held a better model. (3) So law and ordinary morality will treat "I asked and followed" as the whole of due care; the reasonable person is now an output anyone can reproduce. (4) A 2026-quality model is sometimes wrong, and then wrong everywhere at once. (5) So whole cohorts of parents or foremen will err together, in good faith; each was reasonable, none is culpable. (6) Blame is the precondition of forgiveness; where there is no fault, the harmed hold a wound no one owes them anything for. (7) And whoever did otherwise and was right cannot be praised for judgment, since ex ante she fell below the standard of care; she was lucky, or difficult. Weakest step: (4)–(5), which assume a serious, traceable shared error; one is enough.
For an ordinary person: A mother follows the model on her daughter's night terrors. So does every mother: "what does yours say?" "Same." The advice is decent, the sameness is a comfort, nobody is ever wrong alone. She notices she cannot recall the last time she was blamed, or praised, and that this is not the same as doing well.
The strongest objection: This is moral luck plus reasonable reliance, both old. A patient harmed by following her doctor is not at fault, nor is the doctor who followed the guideline; we compensate, revise it, move on. That no individual is blameworthy is the correct verdict, and the hunger for someone to blame is the appetite justice exists to discipline. Only the scale is new, and scale changes nothing about desert.
Either side: When everyone was wrong together, is it right that no one is blamed, or is a harm with no one to forgive done a second time, by the exculpation?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is "the algorithm made me do it," blame deflected to a company. There is no company: progress stopped, the model is a utility, and each person chose freely and well. The trouble is not that the excuse fails but that it succeeds.
Who it lands on: A mother whose daughter, now twenty-two, is one of a cohort marked by advice every household received, and who cannot forgive her because there is nothing to forgive. The mother wants to be blamed so she can be forgiven, and is told by everyone, including her daughter's model, that she did nothing wrong.

### Q-08 — cell B — The Promise Against the Forecast
Implication: When both parties can ask their own model how likely a promise is to be kept, it loses its power to inform and keeps only its power to bind, leaving either the only honest promise or a lie agreed to by both.
Follows from: (1) An always-on model holding a person's history can estimate, like a wise friend and no better, how likely they are to keep a commitment. (2) Everyone has this, so the promisee can ask hers and get a comparable number. (3) A promise used to carry a prediction (expect this) and an undertaking (hold me to this). When both can forecast, the prediction is redundant and the undertaking stands alone. (4) So every promise now carries a second content, "I asked and am saying it anyway" or "I chose not to ask," and neither is the innocence promises were made in. (5) One tradition says the undertaking was always the whole of it and calls this purification; another says a promise already discounted is not received as a promise at all. Weakest step: (1); a 2026-class forecast of one person is mediocre, and if everyone treats it as noise, promises stay innocent. Reply: mediocre beats nothing, and a number, once seen, is not unseen.
For an ordinary person: A man tells his daughter he will be at her recital, then asks: seventy percent, and two things that would raise it, which he does. Was that a promise kept or a plan executed? She asked hers, sixty-five, and arranged to sit with a friend's family in case. When he arrives both are glad and neither surprised; a gift has become a delivery.
The strongest objection: This is what promising always was. Arendt called it the remedy for unpredictability; Nietzsche's promising animal was the one able to bind itself against its own likely future. A promise was never a forecast. Showing the odds removes only the confusion that let weak promises pass as strong. The father promising at thirty-five percent does the purest thing a promiser can; the daughter accepting it, the purest a promisee can.
Either side: Is a promise made against a forecast both parties can see the only honest promise there has ever been, or a lie two people have agreed to tell each other?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is algorithmic fatalism: the AI predicts your divorce, so you divorce. The forecast here is mediocre, and everyone knows it. What matters is that promises are no longer made in innocence of a base rate, on either side.
Who it lands on: A man four years sober who promised his daughter he would not drink at her wedding; he asked, and the model said thirty-five, and that promising would lift it to forty. She asks whether he asked, and he says yes; she asks what it said, and he must choose between the number and the promise.

### Q-09 — cell C — The Compute Lease
Implication: Because firms cannot hold compute and reach it only through the persons who have it, employment becomes a lease of the engine with a human attached, and every adult a rentier of one unit of the key capital good, paid a rent owing nothing to what they do.
Follows from: A share goes to every human; a corporation is none, so firms own no cognition. Coordinated cognition outproduces solitary, so firms want many engine-hours, and the only channel to one is a contract with its holder. That is the pooling question the premise leaves open; I take the labour-law reading: you cannot sell your body but can sell its hours. It is the weakest step; without it there are no firms. Engines are identical, so the rent on an engine-hour is uniform; a firm is indifferent between yours and a stranger's. Supply is fixed at the census times twenty-four hours and firms compete for it, so the rent is positive even though anything one engine makes alone sells for nothing: factor price up, product price down. I assume the rent is large: coordinated cognition is what firms were racing to buy in 2026. So the payslip splits: a uniform engine line, and a human premium that for most work is small.
For an ordinary person: A payroll clerk in Leeds contracts fourteen engine-hours a day to the firm; ten are hers. The scheduler wakes her engine at two in the morning; she wakes at seven and reads what it did. Her payslip has two lines: the engine line, identical for everyone in the building, director and cleaner included, and a small one. Her union wants an eight-hour engine day.
The strongest objection: The premium is not small; it is the point. A 2026-quality model needs direction, taste, verification and context to be worth anything; the firm pays for whoever knows what to ask. Uniform rent on the engine is uniform rent on a hammer, real but trivial; the differential stays in the hand, and nothing is new but a line on a payslip.
Either side: When the wage is for what you hold and not what you do, is that the end of the labour question or the end of the worker?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is that AI takes the jobs and basic income replaces the wage. Here nobody loses a job; they lose the claim that the wage was for them. The income is rent on a property everyone holds equally, paid through ordinary payroll by firms that need heads because heads are where compute lives: Paine's universal proprietorship, delivered by accident and received as an insult.
Who it lands on: Tomasz, fifty-four, a logistics planner, is made redundant and offered instead: no duties, no desk, most of his salary, which the firm calls the engine line and which was never him. He signs, and cannot say what he does for a living.

### Q-10 — cell C — The Abstainer's Wage
Implication: Because the whole economy consults one frozen model, once-independent errors become correlated across every firm, lender and insurer at once, and the scarce paid good becomes judgment provably not the model's, suppliable only by a person who declines their share, valued for being different rather than right.
Follows from: One frozen model for everyone means identical weights and identical systematic errors: the same confabulations, persuasions, blind spots. Faster and cheaper means it is consulted for everything, so its share of decisions is enormous. Markets, pools and portfolios work because errors are independent; correlated, prices carry no crowd information, pools fail together, diversification is fictitious. The weakest step: that decisions correlate despite different prompts and data; on bespoke questions they mostly do not, but on standardized ones, credit, pricing, underwriting, the same inputs go in, and those are what systems are made of. I assume one model or few; three frozen models are still a monoculture beside nine billion minds. Fourteen years cannot pass without a correlated failure: a bank whose borrowers all took the same advice the same week. Correlated risk is uninsurable, so insurers and lenders pay for inputs shown to be uncorrelated, and the only one is human judgment formed without the model. Hence a market for certified abstention, paid not because the abstainer is better (she is worse) but because she is noise of another shape.
For an ordinary person: A credit officer in Nairobi is one of her bank's four dry desks. She signs each morning that her engine stayed closed. Her approvals score thirty percent worse than the model's; the bank keeps her because in the 2033 correlated default hers was the only book that held. Her payslip has a decorrelation line. At lunch she tries to tell which thoughts are hers.
The strongest objection: Crowds were always correlated by fashion and herding, and the model's average error is lower, so systemic risk rises a little while average error falls a lot: a good trade. Reflexivity cuts the other way: if everyone knows everyone uses the model, the market prices its blind spots and the crash is anticipated away. And cheap throughput allows sampling and best-of-many, decorrelating more than admitted.
Either side: When the whole economy thinks with one mind, is it worth paying a person to be wrong in a different way?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is that AI hallucinates and needs a human in the loop. Here the model is not worse than people; the trouble is it is one. The human's value is not oversight but statistical independence. The abstainer is no Luddite; she is a hedge, and hedges lose money most years by design.
Who it lands on: Wanjiru, forty-one, was the only underwriter right in 2033 and is the only one wrong in 2040, her bank's single uncorrelated loss. She is paid to differ and has just learned what differing costs when the model is right.

### Q-11 — cell C — The Ghost Estate
Implication: Since compute cannot be accumulated, the only cognitive capital that grows is context, the years of memory, corrections and taught habits fitting one person's identical engine to their life, and because context is a file, the inalienable share casts an alienable shadow, the most valuable thing a person can sell or leave.
Follows from: Identical engines that cannot be pooled differ only in configuration: instructions, memory, tools, years of feedback. A 2026-quality model with fourteen curated years is far more useful for one life or trade than a blank one, though no smarter: the ceiling holds, the floor rises. That is the weakest step: the premise says never much better; I claim fittedness, not intelligence. Context compounds, so inequality re-enters through context, not compute. Context is data, and business as usual makes data property: copyable, inheritable. This is the case the premise flags: the engine stays locked to the person; its state does not. Markets follow: a surgeon's context licensed to a hospital, a parent's willed to children; estates list it beside the house. But a context runs only on an engine, and every buyer has one. The heir who loads her father's context runs her one engine as him, at the cost of her own: what can be inherited needs what cannot, and a firm can host a ghost on an employee's engine; an individual must evict herself.
For an ordinary person: A carpenter in Oaxaca inherits his uncle's fourteen-year context: every job, every client, every correction. Loaded, his engine quotes like the old man, drafts like him, greets clients by nickname. Unloaded, it is a stranger's blank tool. He cannot run both, so he chooses daily, and clients have noticed which days are which.
The strongest objection: Contexts do not transfer: a surgeon's knows her hands, not yours, so the market is thin and the estate sentimental. Taken seriously, never much better means the gap between blank and taught is small. And ordinary law may block it from both sides: privacy rules could make contexts non-transferable personal data, and employers could claim whatever was taught on their hours.
Either side: Should the shape of a mind be inheritable when the engine that runs it is not, and is loading a dead man's context a way of keeping him or of losing yourself?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is digital immortality, the upload, the ghost with rights. This is not a copy of anyone but a 2026-quality model wearing a dead man's habits, and runs only by displacing the living. The story is not the ghost's claims but the heir's opportunity cost, which is her whole allotted mind.
Who it lands on: Ines, twenty-nine, inherits her father's context, appraised at more than his house, and learns that keeping him means spending her one engine being him, while being paid means letting a stranger's engine be him. She has not opened her own in three months.

### Q-12 — cell C — The Ward's Share
Implication: Because everyone has a share, infants and the comatose included, and a share can be directed but not sold, the right to direct the engine of someone who cannot becomes the unpriced loophole in the equality: dependents turn from costs into assets, guardianship into compute custody, and household income can hinge on a body staying alive.
Follows from: The premise says every human and no one has none, so a newborn has an engine, always on, and so does a woman six years silent. Neither can direct it. Business as usual supplies guardians, who direct what wards cannot. The weakest step: that law lets a guardian's direction reach the market rather than quarantining ward-engines to idle or serve the ward alone. Quarantine is unlikely: idling a frontier model for years is visible waste, and a ward's-benefit standard stretches once the engine pays for her care. Since engine-hours can be leased (Q-09), a directed engine earns rent, so each child is another income and dependents are net assets for the first time since agrarian child labour. States do the same sum: the compute stock is the census, so births are capital. And a dying person's engine is a stream ending at death, so the household's interest and the ward's can part. This card is about de facto pooling: inalienable in law, aggregated in practice, inside families, where nobody signs anything.
For an ordinary person: A father in Naples leases his four children's engines to a freight firm for ten hours a night; the family lives on five engines and his wage. He is scrupulous: earnings in trust, contexts kept clean. He has noticed that his twelve-year-old's engine has spent more hours on manifests than homework, and knows more about shipping than about her.
The strongest objection: Guardianship already handles this: a child's trust, a ward's estate, a child actor's Coogan account. Dependents' assets were always managed by others, and a 2040 statute would ring-fence ward-engines for care, surplus held in trust. The incentive to keep someone alive for a stream that stops at death is a pension's incentive, and society has lived with pensions for centuries.
Either side: Is directing the engine of someone who cannot direct it care or extraction, and does the answer change if the proceeds pay for her bed?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is exploitation of the vulnerable, AI child labour, a villain. The child here is not working; her engine is, and the father is kind. The point is not cruelty but that the premise's equality is fiction for anyone who cannot direct their share, and an honest household lives comfortably on that fiction.
Who it lands on: Lourdes, forty-seven, is guardian to her mother, six years silent; her mother's engine pays for the nursing home and the surplus for Lourdes's daughter's school. The doctor has raised withdrawing care, and Lourdes has noticed the first thing she computed was the rent.

### Q-13 — cell D — The Expertise Fig Leaf
Implication: Once every citizen can read the law as expertly as the official applying it, state discretion is exposed as bare authority — a choice among defensible readings by whoever holds the office — with no cover of superior knowledge left.

### Q-27 — cell G — Nothing said is evidence
Implication: Because no institution can obtain a share-free person, speech stops being evidence of who someone is, and the institutions that judged by a single encounter can no longer recognize a change of heart, which can only be spoken.
Follows from: (1) A 2026 model writes and coaches a perfect apology, allocution or interview answer; everyone has one. (2) Universal and inalienable, it cannot be separated from the person: a room can be emptied of shares, but the words were rehearsed with one for months. (3) Parole boards, judges, asylum officers, admissions committees and confessors all read articulacy, coherence, detail and affect in one encounter as evidence of the person. (4) A signal must be costly to carry information; the cost is now zero for all, so every statement is equally perfect or, when the shares, being one mind, advise sounding unrehearsed, identically imperfect. (5) Institutions retreat to the record, which the share cannot produce in a moment; but a change of heart happens in a moment and can only be said, so the second chance has no instrument left. Weakest step: (5). Boards may keep the ritual and never have weighed the words; but the marginal cases, where the file said no and the room said yes, are where mercy lived.
For an ordinary person: A man spends four months rehearsing eleven minutes with his share; so does every man on his tier, and the share tells each not to sound rehearsed. Outside, a wife answers an apology with "did you write that?" and cannot be sure either way.
The strongest objection: Speech was never good evidence — con men, coached witnesses, the allocution as ritual — and boards already leaned on files. Coaches and ghostwriters existed for the rich; the share extends them to everyone, which is fairer. Nothing is lost that was not already an illusion.
