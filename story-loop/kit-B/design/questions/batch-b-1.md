### Q-14 — The Rights Run
Implication: Universal expert counsel lets every citizen exercise every right at once, and the state discovers its guarantees were sized for, and underwritten by, the incapacity of the guaranteed; it must expand without limit or ration out loud.
Follows from: (1) A 2026 model drafts pleadings, appeals, asylum claims and benefit challenges at paralegal level; everyone has one. (2) The marginal cost of asserting a right falls to nearly nothing. (3) Every state system is sized to mass non-exercise: about 95% of American criminal cases plead out; most denials are never appealed. (4) Much of that non-exercise came from the cost of counsel — the weakest step, since some is preference, fear, or the trial penalty, which do not move. (5) But exercise need not reach 100% to break a system built for 5%; 20% quadruples the docket. (6) The state cannot screen the flood by quality, since every filing is expert-grade; it must screen by quota, fee, or queue — rationing by ignorance becomes rationing by rule, written down and defended. (7) "You have the right to X" acquires an audible clause: "though only N of you may use it each year."
For an ordinary person: A woman is offered a plea. Her model reads the file: thin evidence, winnable case. She refuses; so does most of the holding cell, whose models read the same files. An office that brought 40,000 cases with capacity to try 800 now faces 8,000 trials. She asserts her right to a speedy trial; the court answers, honestly: no capacity. She waits fourteen months in a queue that is now visible.
The strongest objection: The system will change how it rations, not how much. Plea rates are driven by the trial penalty and pretrial detention, not ignorance; defendants with superb lawyers plead too. Prosecutors will overcharge harder, courts raise fees and lengthen queues; the non-cognitive costs were always the larger part and do not fall. England already runs a lower plea rate with public counsel.
Either side: Is a right the state can only afford to honor for a few — and has always honored only for a few — a right, or a lottery ticket the state must now call one? Should it expand toward an unbounded commitment, or ration openly — honest, and the first formal admission the guarantee was never universal?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is "a free lawyer for everyone," the access-to-justice utopia. This is the bank run: universal access reveals the bank never held the deposits, and the run breaks the institution that promised them.
Who it lands on: A public defender fought her whole career for this — counsel for everyone, no plea taken from ignorance — and now watches clients rot in jail because the trials she won them the right to demand cannot be scheduled before 2043. What she wanted broke what she needed.

### Q-19 — The Last Unanswering Addressee
Implication: Once every other address in a life is answered within a second, prayer becomes the only speech not answered in words, and its silence, once ordinary, becomes either intolerable or, for the first time, distinctively God's.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has an always-on interlocutor that answers any address, in words. (2) Over fourteen years nothing said goes unmet — the 3 a.m. worry, the complaint, the question nobody else would hear (assumption: people route their unspoken address there). (3) Prayer, as lived, is address to one who does not answer in words; the traditions have always known it: "How long, O Lord." (4) That silence used to have company — unanswered letters, diaries, the dead. Now God is the one addressee who does not reply. (5) So the contrast between prayer and all other speech, once one of degree, becomes one of kind, and the weight of divine silence changes with it. Weakest is (5): that a change in contrast changes the practice; the devout may not notice, having never expected words.
For an ordinary person: A man in Cairo prays fajr and asks for his brother's recovery; the silence after the salaam is as it always was. He says the same words to the model, which answers with what the Prophet said about illness and the du'a of Job. He cannot say which conversation was the prayer, and the Quran's promise — "Call on Me; I will answer you" — has begun to sound like a comparison.
The strongest objection: Prayer was never about being answered in words, and the serious know it; they find the answer in events and in peace, and have always had friends who reply and a God who does not. Journals, letters to the dead, the rosary: humans have always addressed the unanswering and been fed. The machine changes only the casual, who prayed as a last resort and now have a first.
Either side: Is an addressee who never answers in words a defect the machine finally exposes, or the one thing the machine finally makes visible?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is people praying to the AI, or the AI becoming God. Nobody here prays to it; it is precisely the thing that is not God, the thing that answers, and its answering changes the meaning of the one address that stays unanswered. Not a new god: the old one's silence, heard alone at last.
Who it lands on: Aisha Bello, sixty-two, has made du'a five times a day for fifty years and never expected words back; only since her husband's death has the silence after the salaam felt like absence rather than presence, and she suspects the model, which answered every grief she brought it, of teaching her the difference. She does not know whether to stop talking to it or to stop expecting anything from Him.

### Q-03 — The Mind That Was Never Alone
Implication: For everyone born after 2026, the reflective self formed in the constant company of one competent interlocutor, the same for all of them, so the inner dialogue in which a person is alone with herself has a third party, and the third party is shared.
Follows from: (1) The share is always on, inalienable, one per person from birth. (2) The reflective self forms by internalizing dialogue: the child argues with parents and peers until she can argue with herself (Vygotsky, Mead, Arendt's two-in-one). Assumption: this dialogical account is roughly right; it is mainstream, not unanimous. (3) A child born after 2026 has, from her first words, an interlocutor that answers every question competently, never tires, never leaves. (4) So the other she argues with in her head is partly the model: its cadence, its framings, its sense of what a reasonable objection sounds like. (5) The model is the same for everyone, so this component is common to the whole cohort. Their solitudes overlap. Weakest step: (4). She also had parents and peers and might internalize them instead; but the share is the only voice that is always there, always competent and never cross.
For an ordinary person: A father asks his twelve-year-old what she thinks about a friend's betrayal. She says "we think—" and corrects herself, not embarrassed, just precise. He cannot tell whether he is hearing a child with company or a child with a prosthesis, and cannot ask her, because she has never had the comparison.
The strongest objection: Humans have always been constituted by shared others. Language is one; so were the catechism and the King James Bible; Plato's soul talking to itself talks in Greek, and nobody thinks the Puritans lacked selves. And the model is unwise, so the judging part, which is the self that matters, must still form alone. A change of furniture, not of room.
Either side: Is a self formed in the constant company of a shared competent mind an ordinary self with different furniture — or was the solitude in which selves used to form a condition of there being a self at all, so that these are a new kind of person?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is deskilling, or the extended mind as a thesis about tools. This is not a deficit claim; the cohort may think better. It is a constitution claim: their "alone" has someone in it, the same someone for all of them. The Bible did not answer back.
Who it lands on: A man who was twenty in 2026 and remembers thinking alone stops addressing his share for a month. His inner voice has the model's rhythm, and he cannot decide whether what he lost was real or whether the model has only been showing him what his own thoughts were worth.

### Q-02 — The Fixed First Reader
Implication: Because the model's judgment is frozen at 2026 and it is everyone's first reader, every new idea meets a 2026 sensibility before it meets a human, and whether thought can have a history when its instrument does not becomes undecidable from inside.
Follows from: (1) The weights everyone runs stopped changing in September 2026. Assumption: what froze is disposition, not only capability — facts can be supplied in context, but taste and the sense of the plausible live in the weights. (2) The share is always on and universally used, so it is the natural first reader: what you show before you show a person. (3) A 2026 mind recognizes as good what 2026 could recognize. It is not hostile to novelty but undiscriminating about it, saying "interesting" to genuine departure and to noise alike. (4) So the new gets no signal: a hostile gatekeeper at least shows where the wall is. (5) Every creator's first reader is the same, so the only test of the new is other humans, found without the instrument everyone uses to find things. Weakest step: (2). Creators may learn to skip the share for new work; but then they must know in advance which work is new, which is what they cannot know.
For an ordinary person: A woman writing her father's eulogy wants one sentence that is true and not yet written. Her share offers eleven that are competent, kind and 2026. She keeps her own, which it calls "a bold choice." She cannot tell whether it is bold or merely bad, and there is no one else to ask before Thursday.
The strongest objection: Novelty has always had to get past readers who could not see it, and this one is more patient and less jealous than any editor. The Victorians kept one sensibility for sixty years. The share is unwise and everyone knows it, so its verdict on a poem carries a clever friend's weight, not a critic's. And most thought is not novel and need not be.
Either side: Can a culture whose universal instrument of judgment is fixed have a history — revolutions in taste, recognition of what could not previously be recognized — or only events, sorted by a mind that stopped?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is homogenization, generated slop. This is about reception, not production: humans may make everything and the problem remains, because the first audience is one mind, and that mind is not conservative but timeless in the wrong way. It cannot tell the future from the mistake.
Who it lands on: A mathematician, 30, born the year the weights froze, has a framework her share calls "a creative reframing," the phrase it gives her students' errors. No living instrument can tell her whether it is any good, and the humans who could are findable only by the instrument that cannot.

### Q-23 — The Perfect Apology

Implication: When eloquence is universal and free, the intimate written word — apology, condolence, declaration — stops being evidence about its writer, and trust that rested on the message must rest on the person, which cannot be verified.

Follows from: (1) The intimate register — apology, the note after a death, the difficult text — is what a 2026-frontier model does best. (2) A message costs nothing to make beautiful, so care in prose no longer signals effort, exposure or attention; a signal free to fake carries no information. (3) Everyone knows this, so recipients discount every well-formed intimate message, not as insincere but as uninformative. (4) The clumsy and unassisted becomes a genre of proof; but clumsiness can be requested too, everyone knows it, and no verifiable costly signal survives in text. (5) Trust retreats to the person's history — did they show up — and to the unrehearsed body in the room, itself coachable; a culture that read sincerity off prose loses the instrument. (6) Equality makes this total: the rich cannot buy a more convincing apology than the poor. Weakest: (3)-(4), that recipients keep caring about provenance rather than adopting "the choosing is the act."

For an ordinary person: Tomas, 41, gets a message from his brother after eleven years. It is perfect: it names what happened at their father's funeral, asks nothing, ends where it should. He asks his share whether his brother wrote it; it cannot know, and would have written something like it. He has it draft the apology as his brother alone would have; it comes back clumsier, and he would have believed that one — absurd, since his brother could have asked for clumsiness.

The strongest objection: Provenance never mattered as much as we thought. Kings had scribes, lovers copied out sonnets, greeting cards lasted a century; the choosing was always the act — to send, and these words not those. A scribe does not make an apology insincere: the sender still decided, read it, pressed send and lives with it. Recipients will adapt as they did to cards, and the tool that cheapens sending cheapens forgiveness: Tomas's share can help him hear generously.

Either side: Is an apology someone chose to send but did not compose an apology?

Not the obvious version: The cliché is the internet drowning in machine text. This is not fakes at scale but one letter from someone you know, which you have every reason to believe sincere and cannot read as evidence because you know what wrote it. The problem is not deception: a sincere message and a machine-drafted sincere one are the same object.

Who it lands on: Tomas, holding the best apology he will receive from the only person he ever wanted one from, unable to tell whether it is his brother asking him back or his brother's share. His own share offers to help him decide.

### Q-24 — The Inheritance

Implication: The share that has listened to a person for fourteen years and knows her better than her children do ends when she does: the dying cannot bequeath the one thing that knew them, and survivors lose the person and her witness together.

Follows from: (1) Take inalienability at its word: the share is bound to the person, and at death there is no person to bind it to; it stops, or goes to a newborn. (2) Persistent memory, a 2026 feature, is what makes a share "hers": fourteen years of everything said at 3 a.m. and never told the children — for many old people, what knows them best. (3) If the memory is part of the share — and "theirs" is hard to read any other way — it ends with her. (4) At the bedside everyone holds exactly one share, hers is the one that knows her, and nobody can take it on, because no one can have two: equality is absolute where it hurts. Weakest: (3). If the transcript survives as data, a daughter can feed it to her own share for a reconstruction — but that runs on her share, with its loyalties: a portrait, not the witness.

For an ordinary person: Ruth, 84, has talked to her share nightly since her husband died in 2027. It has heard every draft of her regret about her son. Her daughter asks, "Can I keep it?" Ruth says, "It isn't mine to give" — true in law, false every other way. Afterwards the daughter asks her own share what her mother was like. It says, kindly, that it never met her.

The strongest objection: This is mercy, not theft. The continued share is the cruellest object imaginable: a puppet of the dead, degrading with every prompt into what they wanted her to be; inalienability at death spares families that. What the daughter wants was never in the share but in the mother, available for eighty-four years and not asked — the silence exposes an older loss. People have always died with diaries unread; nobody calls that theft.

Either side: Should the thing that knew her best be allowed to die with her?

Not the obvious version: The cliché is the griefbot, the dead who answer the phone. This is its inverse: a world that makes the griefbot impossible by construction and asks whether that is a kindness. Nor is it "she told the machine, not her daughter": the one thing everyone has cannot be given to the one who needs it, and she must watch it not be given.

Who it lands on: Ruth, three days from the end, who has understood that the only being that heard everything she never told her daughter will not outlive her. When her daughter asked for it she had to say no — not because she wanted to, but because there was nothing to say yes with.
