### Q-05 — cell B — The Unasked Question
Implication: When a competent reading of your own life is one typed sentence away, ignorance stops being a condition and becomes an act, and not-asking cannot be told apart, even from inside, from trust.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has, always on, a model that answers what it is asked at a thoughtful adult's level, from what it is told. (2) "I didn't know" has always sheltered two things: facts you could not get and inferences you did not draw. The model supplies no facts; it draws the inference, free, for everyone equally. That shelter is gone. (3) But asking now costs something new: to type "is he lying to me" is already to have made him a suspect. (4) Trust used to be a default, because checking was hard; here checking is free, so not-checking is a daily choice, and the only trust that costs anything. (5) So one act, walking past the door, is blindness when done from fear and trust when done from love, and she cannot tell which, since motive is exactly what she would have to ask about. Weakest step: (3)–(4), which assume asking damages trust; if asking becomes as neutral as looking something up, not-asking is negligence and the dilemma collapses into a duty.
For an ordinary person: Her husband says Thursday will be late. Before, she would half-wonder and let it go. Now the wondering has a door whose shape she can feel, and she notices herself not opening it. Her son's silence, her mother's balance, the mole on her shoulder: each has a door. She walks past five a day and calls it faith.
The strongest objection: Trust was never ignorance. To trust is not to refuse to look but to look without suspicion. Asking "is he all right?" is attention, not betrayal; ritual not-asking is the superstition that kept people from opening test results. And the model does not know whether he is lying; it weighs what she tells it, which she could have weighed in her own head.
Either side: Is there a duty to ask your model about the people you love, given that you can, or is not-asking the last act of trust a person can still perform?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the transparent society: nothing hidden, no excuses. Here no one is watched; the model sees only what its owner points it at. What changes is not the world's opacity but the ownership of one's own, and "did you know?" gains a third answer: "I didn't ask."
Who it lands on: A woman who for eleven years never put a question about her husband to her model, a discipline she privately called loyalty; then he is arrested, and the detective asks, gently, whether she really never asked. She sees that her trust and her blindness were one act, and will never know which she was performing.

### Q-06 — cell B — Practicing on People
Implication: Because the model is good but never superhuman and no one can own more than one, the only way above it is deciding without it on live cases, which makes cultivating one's own judgment a cost laid, without consent, on others.
Follows from: (1) The model advises at a thoughtful generalist's level and is there before every decision. (2) Judgment, in Aristotle's sense, seeing what matters here, is built by deciding and bearing the outcome; whoever defers on everything builds the skill of deferring instead. This, contested, is the weakest step. (3) Because the model is never superhuman and cannot be pooled or bought, the ceiling is the same for everyone and the only way past it is personal: a woman who knows her own daughter can exceed it there. And since progress stopped, if no one practices, human practical wisdom is capped where the frontier was. (4) But there is no practice ground. The cases that build judgment are about other people, and deciding unaided means accepting for them a worse expected outcome than one question would give. (5) Those people have the same model. The son can run his father's choices through it and read what a reasonable man would have done: the apprentice is audited by her material.
For an ordinary person: A shift supervisor has a man gone slack. The model offers a workable script. He speaks in his own words instead, wanting to be a supervisor who can; it goes badly, and the man is shamed before two others. Later the man asks his model what a competent supervisor would have said, and reads exactly how much worse he was treated.
The strongest objection: This is romanticism. No surgeon refuses the checklist to grow; judgment was always built from other people's, by consulting, deciding, and seeing where the counsel was wrong, a loop the model makes faster. Refusing counsel to become someone is the arrogance every tradition warns of; Bernard Williams named the vice of caring more about the agent you are than about the people in front of you: moral self-indulgence.
Either side: May a person decide without the model, accepting a worse expected result for those affected, to become someone whose judgment is their own, or is that vanity paid for by others?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is atrophy: AI makes us dumb. This is not about capacity. The supervisor can build judgment; the question is whether he is entitled to, given who pays for the lessons and can check the bill.
Who it lands on: A father who raised his son, a theft at thirteen, a heartbreak at seventeen, on his own judgment, never asking, because he wanted to be the man who knew his boy, not the one who relayed. At nineteen the son runs those years through his model, arrives with a list, and asks whether he was raised or practiced on.

### Q-07 — cell B — Nobody to Blame
Implication: When everyone acts on the same counsel and it is the best anyone could have had, a harm done by many at once has no one to blame and so no one to forgive, and whoever dissented rightly was, at the time, unreasonable.
Follows from: (1) Everyone's model is the same kind; where personal context matters little (a child's first lie) it converges on one answer, as 2026-class training does. (2) By the premise that answer is the best anyone could have had; no one held a better model. (3) So law and ordinary morality will treat "I asked and followed" as the whole of due care; the reasonable person is now an output anyone can reproduce. (4) A 2026-quality model is sometimes wrong, and then wrong everywhere at once. (5) So whole cohorts of parents or foremen will err together, in good faith; each was reasonable, none is culpable. (6) Blame is the precondition of forgiveness; where there is no fault, the harmed hold a wound no one owes them anything for. (7) And whoever did otherwise and was right cannot be praised for judgment, since ex ante she fell below the standard of care; she was lucky, or difficult. Weakest step: (4)–(5), which assume a serious, traceable shared error; one is enough.
For an ordinary person: A mother follows the model on her daughter's night terrors. So does every mother: "what does yours say?" "Same." The advice is decent, the sameness is a comfort, nobody is ever wrong alone. She notices she cannot recall the last time she was blamed, or praised, and that this is not the same as doing well.
The strongest objection: This is moral luck plus reasonable reliance, both old. A patient harmed by following her doctor is not at fault, nor is the doctor who followed the guideline; we compensate, revise it, move on. That no individual is blameworthy is the correct verdict, and the hunger for someone to blame is the appetite justice exists to discipline. Only the scale is new, and scale changes nothing about desert.
Either side: When everyone was wrong together, is it right that no one is blamed, or is a harm with no one to forgive done a second time, by the exculpation?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is "the algorithm made me do it," blame deflected to a company. There is no company: progress stopped, the model is a utility, and each person chose freely and well. The trouble is not that the excuse fails but that it succeeds.
Who it lands on: A mother whose daughter, now twenty-two, is one of a cohort marked by advice every household received, and who cannot forgive her because there is nothing to forgive. The mother wants to be blamed so she can be forgiven, and is told by everyone, including her daughter's model, that she did nothing wrong.

### Q-08 — cell B — The Promise Against the Forecast
Implication: When both parties can ask their own model how likely a promise is to be kept, it loses its power to inform and keeps only its power to bind, leaving either the only honest promise or a lie agreed to by both.
Follows from: (1) An always-on model holding a person's history can estimate, like a wise friend and no better, how likely they are to keep a commitment. (2) Everyone has this, so the promisee can ask hers and get a comparable number. (3) A promise used to carry a prediction (expect this) and an undertaking (hold me to this). When both can forecast, the prediction is redundant and the undertaking stands alone. (4) So every promise now carries a second content, "I asked and am saying it anyway" or "I chose not to ask," and neither is the innocence promises were made in. (5) One tradition says the undertaking was always the whole of it and calls this purification; another says a promise already discounted is not received as a promise at all. Weakest step: (1); a 2026-class forecast of one person is mediocre, and if everyone treats it as noise, promises stay innocent. Reply: mediocre beats nothing, and a number, once seen, is not unseen.
For an ordinary person: A man tells his daughter he will be at her recital, then asks: seventy percent, and two things that would raise it, which he does. Was that a promise kept or a plan executed? She asked hers, sixty-five, and arranged to sit with a friend's family in case. When he arrives both are glad and neither surprised; a gift has become a delivery.
The strongest objection: This is what promising always was. Arendt called it the remedy for unpredictability; Nietzsche's promising animal was the one able to bind itself against its own likely future. A promise was never a forecast. Showing the odds removes only the confusion that let weak promises pass as strong. The father promising at thirty-five percent does the purest thing a promiser can; the daughter accepting it, the purest a promisee can.
Either side: Is a promise made against a forecast both parties can see the only honest promise there has ever been, or a lie two people have agreed to tell each other?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is algorithmic fatalism: the AI predicts your divorce, so you divorce. The forecast here is mediocre, and everyone knows it. What matters is that promises are no longer made in innocence of a base rate, on either side.
Who it lands on: A man four years sober who promised his daughter he would not drink at her wedding; he asked, and the model said thirty-five, and that promising would lift it to forty. She asks whether he asked, and he says yes; she asks what it said, and he must choose between the number and the promise.
