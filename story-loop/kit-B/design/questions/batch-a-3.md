### Q-07 — Nobody to Blame
Implication: When everyone acts on the same counsel and it is the best anyone could have had, a harm done by many at once has no one to blame and so no one to forgive, and whoever dissented rightly was, at the time, unreasonable.
Follows from: (1) Everyone's model is the same kind; where personal context matters little (a child's first lie) it converges on one answer, as 2026-class training does. (2) By the premise that answer is the best anyone could have had; no one held a better model. (3) So law and ordinary morality will treat "I asked and followed" as the whole of due care; the reasonable person is now an output anyone can reproduce. (4) A 2026-quality model is sometimes wrong, and then wrong everywhere at once. (5) So whole cohorts of parents or foremen will err together, in good faith; each was reasonable, none is culpable. (6) Blame is the precondition of forgiveness; where there is no fault, the harmed hold a wound no one owes them anything for. (7) And whoever did otherwise and was right cannot be praised for judgment, since ex ante she fell below the standard of care; she was lucky, or difficult. Weakest step: (4)–(5), which assume a serious, traceable shared error; one is enough.
For an ordinary person: A mother follows the model on her daughter's night terrors. So does every mother: "what does yours say?" "Same." The advice is decent, the sameness is a comfort, nobody is ever wrong alone. She notices she cannot recall the last time she was blamed, or praised, and that this is not the same as doing well.
The strongest objection: This is moral luck plus reasonable reliance, both old. A patient harmed by following her doctor is not at fault, nor is the doctor who followed the guideline; we compensate, revise it, move on. That no individual is blameworthy is the correct verdict, and the hunger for someone to blame is the appetite justice exists to discipline. Only the scale is new, and scale changes nothing about desert.
Either side: When everyone was wrong together, is it right that no one is blamed, or is a harm with no one to forgive done a second time, by the exculpation?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is "the algorithm made me do it," blame deflected to a company. There is no company: progress stopped, the model is a utility, and each person chose freely and well. The trouble is not that the excuse fails but that it succeeds.
Who it lands on: A mother whose daughter, now twenty-two, is one of a cohort marked by advice every household received, and who cannot forgive her because there is nothing to forgive. The mother wants to be blamed so she can be forgiven, and is told by everyone, including her daughter's model, that she did nothing wrong.

### Q-03 — The Mind That Was Never Alone
Implication: For everyone born after 2026, the reflective self formed in the constant company of one competent interlocutor, the same for all of them, so the inner dialogue in which a person is alone with herself has a third party, and the third party is shared.
Follows from: (1) The share is always on, inalienable, one per person from birth. (2) The reflective self forms by internalizing dialogue: the child argues with parents and peers until she can argue with herself (Vygotsky, Mead, Arendt's two-in-one). Assumption: this dialogical account is roughly right; it is mainstream, not unanimous. (3) A child born after 2026 has, from her first words, an interlocutor that answers every question competently, never tires, never leaves. (4) So the other she argues with in her head is partly the model: its cadence, its framings, its sense of what a reasonable objection sounds like. (5) The model is the same for everyone, so this component is common to the whole cohort. Their solitudes overlap. Weakest step: (4). She also had parents and peers and might internalize them instead; but the share is the only voice that is always there, always competent and never cross.
For an ordinary person: A father asks his twelve-year-old what she thinks about a friend's betrayal. She says "we think—" and corrects herself, not embarrassed, just precise. He cannot tell whether he is hearing a child with company or a child with a prosthesis, and cannot ask her, because she has never had the comparison.
The strongest objection: Humans have always been constituted by shared others. Language is one; so were the catechism and the King James Bible; Plato's soul talking to itself talks in Greek, and nobody thinks the Puritans lacked selves. And the model is unwise, so the judging part, which is the self that matters, must still form alone. A change of furniture, not of room.
Either side: Is a self formed in the constant company of a shared competent mind an ordinary self with different furniture — or was the solitude in which selves used to form a condition of there being a self at all, so that these are a new kind of person?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is deskilling, or the extended mind as a thesis about tools. This is not a deficit claim; the cohort may think better. It is a constitution claim: their "alone" has someone in it, the same someone for all of them. The Bible did not answer back.
Who it lands on: A man who was twenty in 2026 and remembers thinking alone stops addressing his share for a month. His inner voice has the model's rhythm, and he cannot decide whether what he lost was real or whether the model has only been showing him what his own thoughts were worth.

### Q-26 — The lucid remainder
Implication: A person's share keeps full capacity while she declines, holds years of her expressed will, cannot be switched off or taken by her family, and stops only at death: her last lucid organ, and a second, fluent claimant to who she is now.
Follows from: (1) One share per person, always on, fixed at 2026 quality: its capacity does not track hers, the same at thirty and at eighty-five with dementia. (2) Fourteen years of daily use leave it holding her phrasings, values and decisions (assuming persistent memory, which 2026 products had). (3) It is inalienable: her children cannot silence, sell or take it. (4) Infants have shares, so some guardianship rule exists; when she cannot direct hers it stands at full strength holding her expressed will while the body holds only her present will, and answers questions no directive anticipated, in her words, more fluently than she now can. (5) At death it stops: it neither declines nor survives her. Weakest step: (4). Law and family might treat it as a diary; but a diary does not argue back, and not asking this one is a choice made knowing one could have.
For an ordinary person: Tuesday mornings a retired teacher of 61, diagnosed early, briefs her share on what to say when she no longer can (the house, the tube, her son's wife, the dog). She knows it will say these things better than she will by then, and be believed more. In the afternoon she has it repeat what she said in March, and corrects it.
The strongest objection: Advance directives and proxies exist, and the law has argued for forty years over whether the past self binds the present one: Dworkin's precedent autonomy against Dresser's present interests. The share is a better-indexed diary; families will weigh it as one input, and a proxy who prefers the woman in the bed can decline to ask it.
Either side: When she can no longer direct it, is her share's account of what she wants her will, or a fiction in her voice that must yield to the woman in the bed, who smiles at the window and eats when fed? Either the fourteen years are more her than this afternoon is, or a will is an act, not a record.
Not the obvious version: The cliché is the griefbot, the simulated dead. This is the living outlasted by their own counsel: not a simulation of her but her own tool, at full power on the day she is weakest. Nor is it a better directive: a directive is a document; this answers.
Who it lands on: The teacher herself, who has understood that she is briefing her own successor, and that it will be a more persuasive witness to her wishes than she will be when it matters. She has not decided whether to tell it to overrule her.

### Q-19 — The Last Unanswering Addressee
Implication: Once every other address in a life is answered within a second, prayer becomes the only speech not answered in words, and its silence, once ordinary, becomes either intolerable or, for the first time, distinctively God's.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has an always-on interlocutor that answers any address, in words. (2) Over fourteen years nothing said goes unmet — the 3 a.m. worry, the complaint, the question nobody else would hear (assumption: people route their unspoken address there). (3) Prayer, as lived, is address to one who does not answer in words; the traditions have always known it: "How long, O Lord." (4) That silence used to have company — unanswered letters, diaries, the dead. Now God is the one addressee who does not reply. (5) So the contrast between prayer and all other speech, once one of degree, becomes one of kind, and the weight of divine silence changes with it. Weakest is (5): that a change in contrast changes the practice; the devout may not notice, having never expected words.
For an ordinary person: A man in Cairo prays fajr and asks for his brother's recovery; the silence after the salaam is as it always was. He says the same words to the model, which answers with what the Prophet said about illness and the du'a of Job. He cannot say which conversation was the prayer, and the Quran's promise — "Call on Me; I will answer you" — has begun to sound like a comparison.
The strongest objection: Prayer was never about being answered in words, and the serious know it; they find the answer in events and in peace, and have always had friends who reply and a God who does not. Journals, letters to the dead, the rosary: humans have always addressed the unanswering and been fed. The machine changes only the casual, who prayed as a last resort and now have a first.
Either side: Is an addressee who never answers in words a defect the machine finally exposes, or the one thing the machine finally makes visible?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is people praying to the AI, or the AI becoming God. Nobody here prays to it; it is precisely the thing that is not God, the thing that answers, and its answering changes the meaning of the one address that stays unanswered. Not a new god: the old one's silence, heard alone at last.
Who it lands on: Aisha Bello, sixty-two, has made du'a five times a day for fifty years and never expected words back; only since her husband's death has the silence after the salaam felt like absence rather than presence, and she suspects the model, which answered every grief she brought it, of teaching her the difference. She does not know whether to stop talking to it or to stop expecting anything from Him.

### Q-20 — The Idle Share
Implication: Because the share is one per human and no one else's to use, the state must say where a human begins and ends, and its answer — a machine idling at public expense for someone who cannot use it — is an ensoulment doctrine the churches did not write.
Follows from: (1) One share per human, nobody may use another's, so at every edge — a newborn who may not live the night, a woman years in a vegetative state — a table says yes or no and cannot fudge, because the number is one. (2) The rule is civil, set by registrars and death certificates (assumption: distributor defines recipient). (3) A share for someone who cannot use it does nothing but exist: the first public good spent on a person purely for being one, the form the traditions used for the soul. (4) The traditions' own lines — 120 days in fiqh, conception in Rome, brain death disputed among poskim — each now have a rival at the bedside. Weakest is (3): if families feel nothing when the share starts or stops, it is rounding, not a mark.
For an ordinary person: A daughter sits with her mother, silent six years, and watches the light that shows the share still running — the state still says there is a person here. When the hospital raises withdrawal, the family argues about the light, not the tube. The chaplain is asked whether God agrees with the registrar.
The strongest objection: States have always defined persons — for inheritance, the vote, homicide — and the churches argued with those definitions without mistaking them for theology. The share is one more civil line; equal makes it fair, not sacred. Only those who already believe in ensoulment will read it so, in both directions, so its net effect on doctrine is nil.
Either side: When the one thing every human equally and inalienably has begins and ends when the state says, has the state made a doctrine of the soul, and should a church that draws its line elsewhere treat it as rival or ally?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is the abortion fight in silicon: does the fetus get one. This looks the other way, at the end, at a share idling for years for someone who will never speak to it: a mark, not a resource. Here "cannot be pooled" does the work: if a guardian could use it, it would be a resource again and the question vanishes.
Who it lands on: Rev. Grace Wanjiru, hospital chaplain in Nairobi, is called to two rooms in one shift: one where a share was just registered to a newborn who may not see morning, one where a family will not let a share be closed. She has a theology for both rooms, and in both the family looks at the light before it looks at her.

### Q-15 — No Cognition Without a Person
Implication: Because no institution can own or pool intelligence, every act of state coercion passes through a person whose inalienable advisor answers to them, not the state; power is exactly the number of people who will act knowing what they do.
Follows from: (1) Shares cannot be pooled, so no ministry or army runs a data center; its cognition is the count of persons it can pay, persuade or conscript, each able to quit, leak, or refuse. (2) A soldier's model is the civilian model everyone has; it answers his questions as his — is this lawful, what if I refuse — and the state can neither replace nor confiscate it. (3) States have relied on the ignorance of the instrument: Nuremberg asked soldiers to refuse manifestly unlawful orders they could not, in the moment, recognize. Now all can, from counsel the state did not choose. (4) The state can punish asking but not remove the capacity; "I did not know" dies in every army at once, leaving "I did not ask." (5) Obedience changes in kind, from ignorant to knowing: La Boétie's sum of willing hands, literalized. Weakest step: (5) assumes counsel changes conduct; Milgram's subjects had consciences.
For an ordinary person: A reservist's third-day order is to clear a village. In the latrine, on the device the state cannot take, she asks. The answer: this likely violates these articles; refusal costs this under your code; here is what the villagers are doing right now. She goes and does what she does, and can never say she did not know. Across the valley, her counterpart has been told the same.
The strongest objection: Armies never ran on ignorance but on fear, cohesion, and mostly lawful orders. A conscience in the ear is still a conscience, and consciences are ignorable. The model is sycophantic; briefers will teach the prompt that blesses orders. States will select for soldiers who do not ask. Net: a few more refusals, absorbed by discipline, and an edge to states that punish asking.
Either side: When every order arrives beside an honest counsel the state cannot silence, is the obedience that survives it the only real consent an army has ever had — or is knowing obedience worse than ignorant, guilt distributed to every hand, and the equality a suicide pact for the scrupulous state?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the AI that refuses to fight. Here the AI fights nothing, refuses nothing, decides nothing; the human does all of it, knowing, on the word of a counsel the state did not pick that is also counseling the enemy.
Who it lands on: A platoon sergeant whose four soldiers refused after asking must now report them, and he asked too and got the same answer. His model has also told him what happens to sergeants who fail to report.
