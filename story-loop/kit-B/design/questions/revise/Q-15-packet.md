# Revision packet for Q-15

## The card as it stands

### Q-15 — No Cognition Without a Person
Implication: Because no institution can own or pool intelligence, every act of state coercion passes through a person whose inalienable advisor answers to them, not the state; power is exactly the number of people who will act knowing what they do.
Follows from: (1) Shares cannot be pooled, so no ministry or army runs a data center; its cognition is the count of persons it can pay, persuade or conscript, each able to quit, leak, or refuse. (2) A soldier's model is the civilian model everyone has; it answers his questions as his — is this lawful, what if I refuse — and the state can neither replace nor confiscate it. (3) States have relied on the ignorance of the instrument: Nuremberg asked soldiers to refuse manifestly unlawful orders they could not, in the moment, recognize. Now all can, from counsel the state did not choose. (4) The state can punish asking but not remove the capacity; "I did not know" dies in every army at once, leaving "I did not ask." (5) Obedience changes in kind, from ignorant to knowing: La Boétie's sum of willing hands, literalized. Weakest step: (5) assumes counsel changes conduct; Milgram's subjects had consciences.
For an ordinary person: A reservist's third-day order is to clear a village. In the latrine, on the device the state cannot take, she asks. The answer: this likely violates these articles; refusal costs this under your code; here is what the villagers are doing right now. She goes and does what she does, and can never say she did not know. Across the valley, her counterpart has been told the same.
The strongest objection: Armies never ran on ignorance but on fear, cohesion, and mostly lawful orders. A conscience in the ear is still a conscience, and consciences are ignorable. The model is sycophantic; briefers will teach the prompt that blesses orders. States will select for soldiers who do not ask. Net: a few more refusals, absorbed by discipline, and an edge to states that punish asking.
Either side: When every order arrives beside an honest counsel the state cannot silence, is the obedience that survives it the only real consent an army has ever had — or is knowing obedience worse than ignorant, guilt distributed to every hand, and the equality a suicide pact for the scrupulous state?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the AI that refuses to fight. Here the AI fights nothing, refuses nothing, decides nothing; the human does all of it, knowing, on the word of a counsel the state did not pick that is also counseling the enemy.
Who it lands on: A platoon sergeant whose four soldiers refused after asking must now report them, and he asked too and got the same answer. His model has also told him what happens to sergeants who fail to report.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this card (scores 1 to 5 on importance, rigour, novelty, arguability, story; then one sentence per axis and the weakest step)

### reviewer-a — importance 5, rigour 3, novelty 4, arguability 5, story 5

importance: Whether state violence still works when every instrument of it is informed is a question about the structure of all coercive power, which is as large as it gets.
rigour: Step (1) overreaches badly — the premise stops frontier progress and distributes personal shares, but says nothing that prevents a state from buying ordinary 2026-era compute or running the classified systems it already has, so "no ministry or army runs a data center" is a stronger premise the card grants itself; steps (2)-(4) then follow well.
novelty: Power measured as the count of people who will act knowing, with the same counsel serving both sides of the valley, is a striking reframing well clear of the refusenik-AI cliché.
arguability: The card's own objection (fear, cohesion, sycophancy, selection for the incurious) is powerful enough that a reader could conclude nothing changes, and the "knowing obedience is worse" horn makes the pro side uncomfortable too.
story: The sergeant who asked, got the same answer, must report the four who refused, and has been told what happens to sergeants who do not, is a trap with a person in it and a price on every exit.
weakest step: Step (1)'s claim that institutions therefore have no cognition of their own, which does not follow from the premise as granted — non-pooling of shares is not a ban on states owning compute, and without that ban the asymmetry the card needs collapses.

### reviewer-b — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 4, arguability 4, story 5

importance: Whether state coercion can survive universal, unconfiscatable counsel bears on war, policing and every instrument of force, which is society-scale.
rigour: (1) through (4) follow cleanly from the no-pooling and inalienability clauses, and the card correctly identifies (5) as the soft joint; the residual cheat is treating the soldier's model as reliably honest under a state that controls briefing, framing and the questions people learn to ask.
novelty: "Power is exactly the number of people who will act knowing what they do" is a sharp, non-obvious consequence of the no-pooling clause, and the counterpart across the valley getting the same answer is a fine twist.
arguability: The objection is strong enough that a reader can conclude "a few more refusals, absorbed by discipline," though the card's own framing leans slightly toward the profundity of knowing obedience.
story: The sergeant who must report four soldiers, having asked and received the same answer, is a complete tragedy in two sentences.
weakest step: (4)'s "'I did not know' dies in every army at once" — it assumes soldiers will ask under fear and time pressure, when the card elsewhere concedes states will punish asking and select for those who do not.
