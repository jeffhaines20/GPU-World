## The card as it stands

### Q-26 — The lucid remainder
Implication: A person's share keeps full capacity while she declines, holds years of her expressed will, cannot be switched off or taken by her family, and stops only at death: her last lucid organ, and a second, fluent claimant to who she is now.
Follows from: (1) One share per person, always on, fixed at 2026 quality: its capacity does not track hers, the same at thirty and at eighty-five with dementia. (2) Fourteen years of daily use leave it holding her phrasings, values and decisions (assuming persistent memory, which 2026 products had). (3) It is inalienable: her children cannot silence, sell or take it. (4) Infants have shares, so some guardianship rule exists; when she cannot direct hers it stands at full strength holding her expressed will while the body holds only her present will, and answers questions no directive anticipated, in her words, more fluently than she now can. (5) At death it stops: it neither declines nor survives her. Weakest step: (4). Law and family might treat it as a diary; but a diary does not argue back, and not asking this one is a choice made knowing one could have.
For an ordinary person: Tuesday mornings a retired teacher of 61, diagnosed early, briefs her share on what to say when she no longer can (the house, the tube, her son's wife, the dog). She knows it will say these things better than she will by then, and be believed more. In the afternoon she has it repeat what she said in March, and corrects it.
The strongest objection: Advance directives and proxies exist, and the law has argued for forty years over whether the past self binds the present one: Dworkin's precedent autonomy against Dresser's present interests. The share is a better-indexed diary; families will weigh it as one input, and a proxy who prefers the woman in the bed can decline to ask it.
Either side: When she can no longer direct it, is her share's account of what she wants her will, or a fiction in her voice that must yield to the woman in the bed, who smiles at the window and eats when fed? Either the fourteen years are more her than this afternoon is, or a will is an act, not a record.
Not the obvious version: The cliché is the griefbot, the simulated dead. This is the living outlasted by their own counsel: not a simulation of her but her own tool, at full power on the day she is weakest. Nor is it a better directive: a directive is a document; this answers.
Who it lands on: The teacher herself, who has understood that she is briefing her own successor, and that it will be a more persuasive witness to her wishes than she will be when it matters. She has not decided whether to tell it to overrule her.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this card (scores 1 to 5 on importance, rigour, novelty, arguability, story; then one sentence per axis and the weakest step)

### reviewer-a — importance 4, rigour 4, novelty 4, arguability 5, story 5

importance: Dementia, dying and who speaks for a person are near-universal at the end of a life, and the card's version reshapes a decision every family eventually faces.
rigour: The chain is tight and the premise does most of the work (inalienable, always-on, non-declining, persistent memory, stops at death), with only the persistent-memory assumption and the guardianship rule in (4) resting on plausible extrapolation rather than the granted text.
novelty: Distinguishing this sharply from the griefbot and from the advance directive — the living outlasted by their own counsel, at full power on the day they are weakest — is a genuinely unfamiliar framing.
arguability: Dworkin versus Dresser is a live forty-year dispute and the card gives both halves real weight, so a reader can honestly finish on either the record or the woman in the bed.
story: A 61-year-old teacher briefing her own successor, correcting its March transcript on Tuesday afternoons, undecided whether to tell it to overrule her, is a person, a want, a price and a room.
weakest step: Step (4)'s assumption that a guardianship regime lets the share keep answering at full strength on behalf of an incapacitated person, when the simpler civil outcome is that incapacity freezes or mutes it exactly as it freezes other instruments.

### reviewer-b — importance 4, rigour 4, novelty 5, arguability 5, story 5

importance: Dementia, proxy decisions and the authority of the past self over the present one are a near-universal end-of-life problem, not a niche one.
rigour: Every step but (4) is licensed by the premise, and the card's defence of (4) — "a diary does not argue back, and not asking this one is a choice made knowing one could have" — is the most honest piece of self-criticism in the batch, though it still needs a persistent-memory assumption it only parenthesises.
novelty: Inverting the griefbot into the living outlasted by their own counsel, at full power on the day the person is weakest, is the single most unexpected consequence anyone drew from "fixed at 2026 quality."
arguability: Dworkin against Dresser is a forty-year deadlock and the card lands it squarely rather than resolving it.
story: A woman briefing her own successor on Tuesday mornings, undecided whether to tell it to overrule her, is a person with a want, a price and a calendar.
weakest step: (1)'s "its capacity does not track hers" is the real engine but the card treats it as trivial, when the whole force depends on the share remaining *her* share rather than becoming, in practice, her family's interface to a patient she can no longer contradict.
