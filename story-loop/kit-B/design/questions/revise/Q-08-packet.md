## The card as it stands

### Q-08 — The Promise Against the Forecast
Implication: When both parties can ask their own model how likely a promise is to be kept, it loses its power to inform and keeps only its power to bind, leaving either the only honest promise or a lie agreed to by both.
Follows from: (1) An always-on model holding a person's history can estimate, like a wise friend and no better, how likely they are to keep a commitment. (2) Everyone has this, so the promisee can ask hers and get a comparable number. (3) A promise used to carry a prediction (expect this) and an undertaking (hold me to this). When both can forecast, the prediction is redundant and the undertaking stands alone. (4) So every promise now carries a second content, "I asked and am saying it anyway" or "I chose not to ask," and neither is the innocence promises were made in. (5) One tradition says the undertaking was always the whole of it and calls this purification; another says a promise already discounted is not received as a promise at all. Weakest step: (1); a 2026-class forecast of one person is mediocre, and if everyone treats it as noise, promises stay innocent. Reply: mediocre beats nothing, and a number, once seen, is not unseen.
For an ordinary person: A man tells his daughter he will be at her recital, then asks: seventy percent, and two things that would raise it, which he does. Was that a promise kept or a plan executed? She asked hers, sixty-five, and arranged to sit with a friend's family in case. When he arrives both are glad and neither surprised; a gift has become a delivery.
The strongest objection: This is what promising always was. Arendt called it the remedy for unpredictability; Nietzsche's promising animal was the one able to bind itself against its own likely future. A promise was never a forecast. Showing the odds removes only the confusion that let weak promises pass as strong. The father promising at thirty-five percent does the purest thing a promiser can; the daughter accepting it, the purest a promisee can.
Either side: Is a promise made against a forecast both parties can see the only honest promise there has ever been, or a lie two people have agreed to tell each other?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is algorithmic fatalism: the AI predicts your divorce, so you divorce. The forecast here is mediocre, and everyone knows it. What matters is that promises are no longer made in innocence of a base rate, on either side.
Who it lands on: A man four years sober who promised his daughter he would not drink at her wedding; he asked, and the model said thirty-five, and that promising would lift it to forty. She asks whether he asked, and he says yes; she asks what it said, and he must choose between the number and the promise.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this card (scores 1 to 5 on importance, rigour, novelty, arguability, story; then one sentence per axis and the weakest step)

### reviewer-a — importance 4, rigour 3, novelty 5, arguability 5, story 5

importance: Promising is the load-bearing device of every marriage, contract, recovery and friendship, so a change to its internal structure reaches the whole of a life rather than a niche.
rigour: Step (1) is where the premise is quietly stretched — a 2026-class model with one person's history is a mediocre forecaster of a single future act, and the card's own reply ("mediocre beats nothing") asserts rather than shows that a soft number acquires the social authority the argument needs.
novelty: The reframing of a promise as prediction-plus-undertaking, and the observation that the innocence of not knowing the base rate is what is destroyed, is genuinely not in the standard AI commentary.
arguability: The Arendt/Nietzsche objection is strong enough that a reader could finish the card convinced the forecast purifies promising rather than dissolving it, and the two sides sit at roughly equal weight.
story: The sober father at the wedding with a thirty-five percent and a daughter asking "what did it say" is a scene with a want, a price and a moment of choice already built in.
weakest step: Step (4)'s claim that "I chose not to ask" is itself a second content — it requires that refusal be legible to the promisee, which the card asserts through the daughter's question rather than deriving from the premise.

### reviewer-b — importance 4, rigour 3, novelty 5, arguability 5, story 5

importance: Promising is load-bearing for marriage, contract, parenthood and every relation built on the future, so a change in what a promise conveys reaches whole lives, even if the mechanism is intimate rather than institutional.
rigour: The card names its own weakest step honestly, but the reply ("mediocre beats nothing, and a number, once seen, is not unseen") is a psychological claim doing philosophical work, and step (1) still asks a 2026 model with a person's history to forecast individual behaviour better than the person's own spouse, which the premise does not grant.
novelty: Splitting the promise into prediction and undertaking and observing that universal forecasting strips the first and exposes the second is an argument I have not seen made about AI, and it is put with real precision.
arguability: "The only honest promise there has ever been" versus "a lie two people agreed to tell each other" are both fully defensible and the card gives each its strongest form, including the Arendt/Nietzsche defence.
story: The father at thirty-five percent, asked by his daughter what the number was, having to choose between the number and the promise, is the most compressed crisis in the batch.
weakest step: Step (2)'s assumption that the promisee's share can produce a "comparable number" about someone else requires her share to have his history, which the premise's one-share-per-person privacy structure does not obviously supply.
