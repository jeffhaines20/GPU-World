# Revision packet for Q-06

## The card as it stands

### Q-06 — Practicing on People
Implication: Because the model is good but never superhuman and no one can own more than one, the only way above it is deciding without it on live cases, which makes cultivating one's own judgment a cost laid, without consent, on others.
Follows from: (1) The model advises at a thoughtful generalist's level and is there before every decision. (2) Judgment, in Aristotle's sense, seeing what matters here, is built by deciding and bearing the outcome; whoever defers on everything builds the skill of deferring instead. This, contested, is the weakest step. (3) Because the model is never superhuman and cannot be pooled or bought, the ceiling is the same for everyone and the only way past it is personal: a woman who knows her own daughter can exceed it there. And since progress stopped, if no one practices, human practical wisdom is capped where the frontier was. (4) But there is no practice ground. The cases that build judgment are about other people, and deciding unaided means accepting for them a worse expected outcome than one question would give. (5) Those people have the same model. The son can run his father's choices through it and read what a reasonable man would have done: the apprentice is audited by her material.
For an ordinary person: A shift supervisor has a man gone slack. The model offers a workable script. He speaks in his own words instead, wanting to be a supervisor who can; it goes badly, and the man is shamed before two others. Later the man asks his model what a competent supervisor would have said, and reads exactly how much worse he was treated.
The strongest objection: This is romanticism. No surgeon refuses the checklist to grow; judgment was always built from other people's, by consulting, deciding, and seeing where the counsel was wrong, a loop the model makes faster. Refusing counsel to become someone is the arrogance every tradition warns of; Bernard Williams named the vice of caring more about the agent you are than about the people in front of you: moral self-indulgence.
Either side: May a person decide without the model, accepting a worse expected result for those affected, to become someone whose judgment is their own, or is that vanity paid for by others?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is atrophy: AI makes us dumb. This is not about capacity. The supervisor can build judgment; the question is whether he is entitled to, given who pays for the lessons and can check the bill.
Who it lands on: A father who raised his son, a theft at thirteen, a heartbreak at seventeen, on his own judgment, never asking, because he wanted to be the man who knew his boy, not the one who relayed. At nineteen the son runs those years through his model, arrives with a list, and asks whether he was raised or practiced on.

## The two reviewers' critiques of this card (scores 1 to 5 on importance, rigour, novelty, arguability, story; then one sentence per axis and the weakest step)

### reviewer-a — importance 5, rigour 4, novelty 5, arguability 5, story 5

importance: Whether a person may ever build their own judgment at others' expense governs every parent, manager, doctor and teacher in the world the premise describes, and the card correctly notes that with progress stopped this is also the ceiling of human practical wisdom.
rigour: Steps 1, 3 and 4 follow cleanly from the granted premise (one non-poolable share, never superhuman), and the card names its contested step (2, that judgment is built by bearing outcomes) instead of hiding it; the residual weakness is that it assumes deferring is available on every consequential case, which "thoughtful generalist" advice does not obviously cover.
novelty: The audit step — that the people you practiced on hold the same instrument and can retroactively price your self-cultivation — is genuinely new and inverts the familiar atrophy essay rather than restating it.
arguability: The Williams objection (moral self-indulgence) is strong enough that a reader could finish siding against the supervisor, and both the "no surgeon refuses the checklist" and "no one practices, so wisdom is capped forever" arguments have real force.
story: The son with the list at nineteen is a scene with a want, a bill and a person holding it, and the supervisor scene already has a room, two witnesses and a shamed man.
weakest step: (2) — that deferring builds only the skill of deferring, since the objection's counter-loop (consult, decide, see where the counsel was wrong) would build judgment too, and the card asserts rather than argues why the model's constant presence breaks that loop.

### reviewer-b — importance 4, rigour 4, novelty 5, arguability 5, story 5

importance: If practical wisdom can only be built at others' expense, that is a permanent condition of every parent, manager, doctor and judge, and the card ties it to a species-level ceiling.
rigour: The chain is tight and the card names its own soft joint honestly; the one unearned move is treating the model's advice as reliably better in expectation on particular, relationship-laden cases, which a thoughtful-generalist ceiling does not guarantee.
novelty: "Judgment can only be practiced on people who can now audit the practice" is the freshest formulation in the batch and I have not seen the auditability half of it anywhere.
arguability: Williams on moral self-indulgence versus the claim that no one becomes a person by relaying makes both sides genuinely inhabitable.
story: The son arriving at nineteen with a list is a crisis with a price, a place and two people who both think they are the wronged one.
weakest step: (4)'s assumption that unaided deciding means "a worse expected outcome" for the other person — on exactly the intimate cases the card cares about, the parent's private knowledge may beat the generalist, which would dissolve the moral debt.
