Q-06 | importance=5 | rigour=4 | novelty=5 | arguability=5 | story=5
importance: Whether human practical wisdom can still be acquired, and at whose expense, decides what a person can become in this world and is permanently load-bearing given that the frontier froze.
rigour: The premise-dependence is unusually tight — the freeze creates the cap, the one-share rule makes the only route past it personal, and the audit step follows from everyone else having the same model — but step (2)'s Aristotelian claim that judgment requires departure on live cases, rather than simulation, rehearsal or post-hoc comparison, is assumed and is exactly what a critic would contest.
novelty: The move from "AI makes us dumb" to "self-cultivation is now a non-consensual experiment on others, auditable by the subjects" is the freshest reframing here and is available nowhere in the standard commentary.
arguability: Williams on moral self-indulgence against the capping of human wisdom is a real standoff, and neither side is a straw man.
story: The son with his list at nineteen, asking which mistakes his father knew in advance, is a scene whose whole weight is in one question.
weakest step: The unargued assumption that departure must be on live cases — that no amount of consulting, simulating against the model, or reviewing one's own past decisions builds the same judgment, which if false collapses the debt to nothing.

importance: Whether the state's capacity for coercion reduces to the number of people who will act knowing what they do is a first-order political question about obedience itself.
rigour: Steps (1) through (4) follow cleanly from the premise's uniform ceiling and non-confiscability, but the card's own flagged step (5) is the load-bearing one and Milgram, cited in the card, points the other way; worse, the strongest objection (models are led by the briefing prompt, and the state writes the brief) undercuts the claim that the counsel "the state did not choose" is actually independent.
novelty: Nuremberg's manifest-illegality standard made operable, and the enemy counselled identically, is a good turn, though "AI and the lawful order" is nearer to existing military-ethics discussion than the rest of this batch.

weakest step: The claim that the soldier's counsel is not capturable — since the same model that answers "is this lawful" answers on the facts as briefed, the state controls the facts and can teach the prompt, which means the state may effectively own the share in the hand after all.

importance: Dementia, guardianship and substituted judgment reach nearly every long life, and a fluent second claimant to a person's will would change how that is decided everywhere.
rigour: The reasoning about capacity that does not track the person's decline, and about instruments coming into force at incapacity, is sound, but step (2) imports fourteen years of persistent, richly indexed memory that the premise grants only as "business as usual," and the card's own admission that a 2026 model goes where it is led makes the artifact the guardian's voice rather than hers — which quietly dissolves the implication.

weakest step: That the share's answers are hers rather than her prompter's — once she cannot hold the prompt, a leadable model answering her son is her son's instrument wearing her phrasings, which makes the "second claimant" a mirror rather than a rival.
