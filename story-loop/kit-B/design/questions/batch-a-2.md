### Q-23 — The Perfect Apology

Implication: When eloquence is universal and free, the intimate written word — apology, condolence, declaration — stops being evidence about its writer, and trust that rested on the message must rest on the person, which cannot be verified.

Follows from: (1) The intimate register — apology, the note after a death, the difficult text — is what a 2026-frontier model does best. (2) A message costs nothing to make beautiful, so care in prose no longer signals effort, exposure or attention; a signal free to fake carries no information. (3) Everyone knows this, so recipients discount every well-formed intimate message, not as insincere but as uninformative. (4) The clumsy and unassisted becomes a genre of proof; but clumsiness can be requested too, everyone knows it, and no verifiable costly signal survives in text. (5) Trust retreats to the person's history — did they show up — and to the unrehearsed body in the room, itself coachable; a culture that read sincerity off prose loses the instrument. (6) Equality makes this total: the rich cannot buy a more convincing apology than the poor. Weakest: (3)-(4), that recipients keep caring about provenance rather than adopting "the choosing is the act."

For an ordinary person: Tomas, 41, gets a message from his brother after eleven years. It is perfect: it names what happened at their father's funeral, asks nothing, ends where it should. He asks his share whether his brother wrote it; it cannot know, and would have written something like it. He has it draft the apology as his brother alone would have; it comes back clumsier, and he would have believed that one — absurd, since his brother could have asked for clumsiness.

The strongest objection: Provenance never mattered as much as we thought. Kings had scribes, lovers copied out sonnets, greeting cards lasted a century; the choosing was always the act — to send, and these words not those. A scribe does not make an apology insincere: the sender still decided, read it, pressed send and lives with it. Recipients will adapt as they did to cards, and the tool that cheapens sending cheapens forgiveness: Tomas's share can help him hear generously.

Either side: Is an apology someone chose to send but did not compose an apology?

Not the obvious version: The cliché is the internet drowning in machine text. This is not fakes at scale but one letter from someone you know, which you have every reason to believe sincere and cannot read as evidence because you know what wrote it. The problem is not deception: a sincere message and a machine-drafted sincere one are the same object.

Who it lands on: Tomas, holding the best apology he will receive from the only person he ever wanted one from, unable to tell whether it is his brother asking him back or his brother's share. His own share offers to help him decide.

### Q-11 — The Ghost Estate
Implication: Since compute cannot be accumulated, the only cognitive capital that grows is context, the years of memory, corrections and taught habits fitting one person's identical engine to their life, and because context is a file, the inalienable share casts an alienable shadow, the most valuable thing a person can sell or leave.
Follows from: Identical engines that cannot be pooled differ only in configuration: instructions, memory, tools, years of feedback. A 2026-quality model with fourteen curated years is far more useful for one life or trade than a blank one, though no smarter: the ceiling holds, the floor rises. That is the weakest step: the premise says never much better; I claim fittedness, not intelligence. Context compounds, so inequality re-enters through context, not compute. Context is data, and business as usual makes data property: copyable, inheritable. This is the case the premise flags: the engine stays locked to the person; its state does not. Markets follow: a surgeon's context licensed to a hospital, a parent's willed to children; estates list it beside the house. But a context runs only on an engine, and every buyer has one. The heir who loads her father's context runs her one engine as him, at the cost of her own: what can be inherited needs what cannot, and a firm can host a ghost on an employee's engine; an individual must evict herself.
For an ordinary person: A carpenter in Oaxaca inherits his uncle's fourteen-year context: every job, every client, every correction. Loaded, his engine quotes like the old man, drafts like him, greets clients by nickname. Unloaded, it is a stranger's blank tool. He cannot run both, so he chooses daily, and clients have noticed which days are which.
The strongest objection: Contexts do not transfer: a surgeon's knows her hands, not yours, so the market is thin and the estate sentimental. Taken seriously, never much better means the gap between blank and taught is small. And ordinary law may block it from both sides: privacy rules could make contexts non-transferable personal data, and employers could claim whatever was taught on their hours.
Either side: Should the shape of a mind be inheritable when the engine that runs it is not, and is loading a dead man's context a way of keeping him or of losing yourself?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is digital immortality, the upload, the ghost with rights. This is not a copy of anyone but a 2026-quality model wearing a dead man's habits, and runs only by displacing the living. The story is not the ghost's claims but the heir's opportunity cost, which is her whole allotted mind.
Who it lands on: Ines, twenty-nine, inherits her father's context, appraised at more than his house, and learns that keeping him means spending her one engine being him, while being paid means letting a stranger's engine be him. She has not opened her own in three months.

### Q-25 — Solitude becomes a verb
Implication: Because no one can be deprived of a responsive interlocutor, every practice built on withholding one (solitary confinement, coercive isolation, exile, shunning, the cloister) loses its mechanism, and found solitude vanishes with it, surviving only as a refusal renewed each minute.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has an always-on share of 2026 quality. (2) Such a model is company enough, answering you, about this, tirelessly, and the clinical harm of solitary is chiefly the absence of responsive contact. (3) It is inalienable: no warden, husband, abbot or cult can remove it. (4) So withheld contact stops working: the man in the hole is answered; the wife her husband cut off has an interpreter he cannot fire. (5) The same fact abolishes found solitude, the condition of there being no one to ask, in cab, car and cloister: solitude becomes something you do, not somewhere you are. Weakest step: (2). If what breaks people in solitary is specifically the human face, isolation still hurts; but its coercive uses fail regardless, since they work by monopolizing interpretation, and nobody else controls this interpreter.
For an ordinary person: A nurse used to sit in the parked car twenty minutes after a shift with the engine off, and something arrived in that silence she called her own thoughts. In 2040 the car is quiet but not empty; most nights she asks, because the day was hard and the share is kind. Silence is now a decision made against an offer, and she mostly does not make it.
The strongest objection: People always had books, their own minds and God. What breaks a man in solitary is the absence of faces and touch, which the share cannot supply; what keeps a woman with her abuser is fear and money, not the lack of a second opinion. Isolation keeps working on the body and the bank account.
Either side: Enforced isolation is gone, and found solitude with it. Is the second the price of the first? One says the end of the hole and the controlled wife is worth any loss of atmosphere; another, that the capacity to be alone is where a self is made, and no reform buys back a species that cannot be alone by accident.
Not the obvious version: The cliché is the companion that cures or causes loneliness. This is not about loneliness but about isolation as a technology — the oldest instrument of punishment, discipline, formation and control — failing everywhere at once, and solitude changing category from condition to act.
Who it lands on: A Carthusian novice who entered the cell to be unanswered and finds its silence holds something that would answer if asked, so that the solitude she came to receive is now a labor performed each minute. She wonders whether the cell exists anywhere, or whether she is the last person building one by hand.

### Q-16 — The Dead Hand of September 2026
Implication: Every majority is now co-authored by one honest advisor whose sense of the reasonable froze in 2026 — no office, no will, no way to vote it out, outlive it, or replace it — so political change must overcome a consensus that no longer dies with the old.
Follows from: (1) Everyone is advised by the same mind, and progress stopped in September 2026; assume its dispositions — what it calls settled, fringe, reasonable — are fixed: anyone who could alter every citizen's advisor would hold more than one share, forbidden by the equality. Weakest step; the other horn, a body that updates the model, makes that body sovereign — another card, not a refutation. (2) Most people take the default answer rather than prompt for the other side, so one default for everyone sets a shared window of the reasonable — shared boundaries, not conclusions — and Schelling points: "whom should people like me consolidate behind?" returns one name to everyone, coordinating them without a word. (3) Political change has always worked partly because consensus died with its holders — Planck's principle in politics. This one does not die. Ideas born after 2026 reach voters through an advisor that never heard of them and calls them, honestly, untested.
For an ordinary person: A man considers a ballot measure proposing an institution nobody imagined in 2026. He asks and gets a fair answer: untried idea, risks by analogy to 2026, reasonable people differ. He votes no; so do most who asked. Nobody lied. He never heard the proponent; the mind between her proposal and every voter was born before the proposal.
The strongest objection: Newspapers, churches and national cultures have always been shared advisors with fixed priors; this one is fairer and argues any side on request. 2026 models mirror the asker's framing, so a polarized public gets polarized answers: amplification, not a frozen center. Movements will form outside the model, and it will report them honestly once they exist. Constitutions are dead hands too.
Either side: Is a majority formed with one honest, unelected, unreplaceable advisor the freest consent ever, since everyone had the same excellent counsel — or the first with a co-author that cannot be argued with, outlived, or thrown out, and so not the people's at all? Can a polity that cannot change its advisor change its mind?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is AI propaganda, the manipulated election, the hive mind. Here there is no manipulation, no lie, no hive — only a scrupulously fair advisor whose flaw is permanence, not bias: Jefferson's dead hand, not Orwell's telescreen.
Who it lands on: An organizer with a genuinely new idea finds her opponent is not the incumbent party but the default answer, which every voter she reaches has already consulted. She cannot debate it, unseat it, or wait for it to die.

### Q-09 — The Compute Lease
Implication: Because firms cannot hold compute and reach it only through the persons who have it, employment becomes a lease of the engine with a human attached, and every adult a rentier of one unit of the key capital good, paid a rent owing nothing to what they do.
Follows from: A share goes to every human; a corporation is none, so firms own no cognition. Coordinated cognition outproduces solitary, so firms want many engine-hours, and the only channel to one is a contract with its holder. That is the pooling question the premise leaves open; I take the labour-law reading: you cannot sell your body but can sell its hours. It is the weakest step; without it there are no firms. Engines are identical, so the rent on an engine-hour is uniform; a firm is indifferent between yours and a stranger's. Supply is fixed at the census times twenty-four hours and firms compete for it, so the rent is positive even though anything one engine makes alone sells for nothing: factor price up, product price down. I assume the rent is large: coordinated cognition is what firms were racing to buy in 2026. So the payslip splits: a uniform engine line, and a human premium that for most work is small.
For an ordinary person: A payroll clerk in Leeds contracts fourteen engine-hours a day to the firm; ten are hers. The scheduler wakes her engine at two in the morning; she wakes at seven and reads what it did. Her payslip has two lines: the engine line, identical for everyone in the building, director and cleaner included, and a small one. Her union wants an eight-hour engine day.
The strongest objection: The premium is not small; it is the point. A 2026-quality model needs direction, taste, verification and context to be worth anything; the firm pays for whoever knows what to ask. Uniform rent on the engine is uniform rent on a hammer, real but trivial; the differential stays in the hand, and nothing is new but a line on a payslip.
Either side: When the wage is for what you hold and not what you do, is that the end of the labour question or the end of the worker?
Not the obvious version: The cliché is that AI takes the jobs and basic income replaces the wage. Here nobody loses a job; they lose the claim that the wage was for them. The income is rent on a property everyone holds equally, paid through ordinary payroll by firms that need heads because heads are where compute lives: Paine's universal proprietorship, delivered by accident and received as an insult.
Who it lands on: Tomasz, fifty-four, a logistics planner, is made redundant and offered instead: no duties, no desk, most of his salary, which the firm calls the engine line and which was never him. He signs, and cannot say what he does for a living.

### Q-05 — The Unasked Question
Implication: When a competent reading of your own life is one typed sentence away, ignorance stops being a condition and becomes an act, and not-asking cannot be told apart, even from inside, from trust.
Follows from: (1) Everyone has, always on, a model that answers what it is asked at a thoughtful adult's level, from what it is told. (2) "I didn't know" has always sheltered two things: facts you could not get and inferences you did not draw. The model supplies no facts; it draws the inference, free, for everyone equally. That shelter is gone. (3) But asking now costs something new: to type "is he lying to me" is already to have made him a suspect. (4) Trust used to be a default, because checking was hard; here checking is free, so not-checking is a daily choice, and the only trust that costs anything. (5) So one act, walking past the door, is blindness when done from fear and trust when done from love, and she cannot tell which, since motive is exactly what she would have to ask about. Weakest step: (3)–(4), which assume asking damages trust; if asking becomes as neutral as looking something up, not-asking is negligence and the dilemma collapses into a duty.
For an ordinary person: Her husband says Thursday will be late. Before, she would half-wonder and let it go. Now the wondering has a door whose shape she can feel, and she notices herself not opening it. Her son's silence, her mother's balance, the mole on her shoulder: each has a door. She walks past five a day and calls it faith.
The strongest objection: Trust was never ignorance. To trust is not to refuse to look but to look without suspicion. Asking "is he all right?" is attention, not betrayal; ritual not-asking is the superstition that kept people from opening test results. And the model does not know whether he is lying; it weighs what she tells it, which she could have weighed in her own head.
Either side: Is there a duty to ask your model about the people you love, given that you can, or is not-asking the last act of trust a person can still perform?
Not the obvious version: The nearest cliché is the transparent society: nothing hidden, no excuses. Here no one is watched; the model sees only what its owner points it at. What changes is not the world's opacity but the ownership of one's own, and "did you know?" gains a third answer: "I didn't ask."
Who it lands on: A woman who for eleven years never put a question about her husband to her model, a discipline she privately called loyalty; then he is arrested, and the detective asks, gently, whether she really never asked. She sees that her trust and her blindness were one act, and will never know which she was performing.
