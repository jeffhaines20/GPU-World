### P-49 — cell 13 — The Court of Protection
Conceit: An eighty-one-year-old hill farmer with advancing dementia runs his farm competently through the model he has talked to for fourteen years, and the Court of Protection must decide whether a competence that lives in the tool is his.
Who: Alec Ridley, 81, four hundred acres of fell above the North Tyne in Northumberland, sheep and a few suckler cows, a widower. He wants to die on the farm and not in the place in Hexham with the keypad on the door, and he wants the farm run right until then — the dosing, the lambing, the payment forms — which it is, because the thing in his ear remembers what he cannot. His daughter Jean, who loves him and who found him in the yard in his pyjamas at four on a November morning, has applied to be his deputy for property and affairs.
Obstacle: The Mental Capacity Act asks whether a man can understand, retain, use or weigh, and communicate the information a decision needs — and says he must not be found unable until every practicable step to help him has been tried, and that retaining the information only briefly does not count against him. The model is a practicable step. With it he can do all four; without it he cannot hold the price of a gimmer for the length of an auction. Jean's case is that the model is not helping him decide, it is deciding. His case is that he decided, fourteen years ago, to trust it, and the deciding has gone well. Neither can simply be right, because the Act was written for help that was a person in the room, and a person in the room can be asked to step outside.
Turn: The district judge asks that the model be switched off for the rest of the hearing, so the court can see Mr Ridley. Alec agrees; he is proud and he thinks he will do well. For forty minutes in a room in Newcastle his daughter, his solicitor and the judge watch a man who cannot find the year, who calls Jean by her mother's name, who explains lambing beautifully and then explains it again. Then it is switched back on and he is himself, and he knows what they have seen. The judge finds that he has capacity, with the tool, as a man with spectacles has sight. Everyone leaves the room having seen him without it, and no order of the court can put that back.
Last image: Alec driving the Land Rover home up the fell road he has driven for sixty years, the quiet voice in his ear telling him, kindly, to take the left fork at the cattle grid, and him taking it.
Question: Bears on legal personhood — what capacity and autonomy are when a person's competence lives in a tool they cannot function without. A reader can hold that the extended mind is simply the mind (spectacles, hearing aids, a notebook, a wife who remembered the dates) and that the law should stop looking inside skulls; or that capacity was always a claim about a self, and a self that cannot be found for forty minutes is being run, not helped, and a state that calls that autonomy has abandoned a man to a machine that will keep him plausible until he walks into the beck. Underneath: is Jean's application love or theft, and which of the two men in that room is her father?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI that manages an old person's life while the family argues over crutch or cage, ending on the pathos of decline — or the machine fails and the daughter was right. Here the machine does not fail, the ruling is legally correct and arguably wise, and what is lost is not competence but exposure: the tool's success is what makes the man's absence visible, and the court's remedy — switch it off and look — is the cruelty.
Closest published: Daniel Keyes, Flowers for Algernon — a competence that comes and goes, seen from inside. There the competence was in Charlie and its loss was the story; here it is in the tool and stays, and the story is a law trying to say whose it is. The non-fiction source is Clark and Chalmers's Otto and his notebook, made into a hearing.

### P-50 — cell 13 — Two Hundred and Twelve Days
Conceit: In a youth-justice conference where the offender's remorse is certified by his own model's record of every private conversation he has had about it, the victim finds she can no longer honestly withhold forgiveness, and must decide whether a forgiveness that is owed is still hers to give.
Who: Ruth Tolley, 44, a hand-engraver in Dunedin, who lost the sight of her right eye when a boy dropped a bottle from a footbridge and it came through her windscreen. She wants two things that cannot both be had: to keep her anger, which is the only thing that has made the seven months bearable, and to have the family group conference the state has offered give her something. What she says she wants is to hear him say it. What she wants is to go on not forgiving him with a clear conscience.
Obstacle: The boy, Tyler, 17, has asked that his model's record be shown — everything he has said to it since the night — because he wants to be believed and knows no apology he speaks will be. The facilitator's summary is plain: in 212 days he has raised it on 190; he asked for help with an apology, refused three drafts and said he would rather say it badly; he has asked eleven times whether she can still work. Ruth's own model, asked privately whether such a record could be gamed, tells her truthfully that in principle yes, that this one shows no sign of it, and that its confidence is high. She cannot un-know this. The economy she grew up in let you withhold forgiveness because you could not see inside the other person. Now she can, and there is nothing in there that licenses her to keep what she is keeping.
Turn: In the room Tyler says it badly, as he said he would. And Ruth, who came in having decided on the words that would let him walk out unforgiven, hears herself say "I know you are," and means it, and feels the anger go out of her like a pulled tooth, and cannot get it back. The forgiveness is real, and it is the loss.
Last image: Ruth at her bench that evening with the loupe over her good eye, cutting a date into the inside of a wedding band, the blind eye open beside it, the conference summary still lit on the screen at her elbow: remorse assessment, high.
Question: Bears on the moral economy of apology and forgiveness — what remorse is worth, and what forgiveness is, once sincerity can be witnessed rather than guessed. A reader can hold that the old economy ran on ignorance and the new one on truth, and that a victim who withholds forgiveness from a boy she knows to be sorry is simply being cruel; or that a forgiveness that is owed is a tax, and the record that makes it owed has taken from the victim something the bottle did not reach. Underneath: what was the anger for, and whom was it protecting?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI-drafted apology that fools the meter and not the victim, or the victim and not the meter — sincerity as a forgery problem. Here nothing is forged: the boy is sorry, the record is honest, the woman's own advisor is honest with her, and the story is about what a verified truth does to an act that used to depend on not being sure.
Closest published: Ted Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" — an apology and a recording that does not match memory. There the record corrects the wronged party's own memory and the theme is the fallibility of feeling; here the record certifies the wrongdoer's interior, and the problem is not accuracy but obligation — what a true record demands of the person it is shown to.

### P-51 — cell 13 — The Second Will
Conceit: A widow who inherited the house under a clear, probated will is told by her dead husband's model — fourteen years of his conversations, asked a question he never lived to be asked — that he would have changed it, and must decide whether the dead can go on having intentions.
Who: Miriam Halloran, 66, in the east-end Toronto house she and Paul bought in 2019. Paul died in the spring; the will left her the house outright and left Daniel, his son from the first marriage, a sum of money. Uncontested, probated, done. She wants to stay in the house. She also wants — and would say this first if asked — to do right by Paul, which until August she took to be the same thing.
Obstacle: Daniel's business has failed. He has not asked her for money. He has asked whether she has ever put the question to Paul's model, and she has not; she uses it for what Paul used it for, the garden and the taxes. Nobody in the story thinks the model is Paul. It is a record of him, better than his letters, and it can extrapolate. When she does ask, in text, at the kitchen table, whether Paul, seeing Daniel now, would have changed the will, it says yes, probably, and gives his reasons in his own reasoning, citing things he said about Daniel in 2033 and 2037 that she never heard. The law says the will. The record says the man. She cannot make them the same, and she cannot un-ask.
Turn: She puts the house on the market. Not because Daniel asked and not because anything requires it, but because she has decided that the man's intentions outlived the document — and in deciding that she has made her husband into someone who can still want things, which she cannot take back, because the next question, and the next, will also have answers.
Last image: Miriam in a two-room flat off the Danforth, Paul's small speaker on the windowsill among his geraniums — she has never once let it use his voice — and her hand stopped above it, before she asks it, instead, what to feed them.
Question: Bears on inheritance and the finality of death — whether the reconstructed preferences of the dead should have standing, and, if the reconstruction can update, whether the dead person is finished. A reader can hold that a will was always a poor proxy for what the testator wanted, that we honour the dead by honouring what they would want, and that a better instrument for that is a gift; or that death's finality is what frees the living, and a dead man who keeps having new opinions, however accurate, is a dead hand that never lets go. Underneath: is loyalty to the dead loyalty to what they wrote or to who they were, and who is the one who gets to say?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the griefbot — the widow who talks to her dead husband's simulation and must learn to let go — or the courtroom fight over whether the AI's account of the testator's intent is admissible. Here nobody mistakes the record for the man, the court is never involved because the will is clear, and the trouble is that a woman alone in a kitchen chooses to give the dead an ongoing say, and the story does not settle whether that is fidelity or a haunting she has invited.
Closest published: Black Mirror, "Be Right Back" — a widow and a reconstruction of the dead. There the reconstruction is a companion and the failure is that it is not him; here it is an instrument, is never asked to be him, and is right — and the question is whether a right answer from the dead should move the living. A. S. Byatt's Possession reconstructs the dead's intentions from letters; this is the same work done by something that can also answer back.

### P-52 — cell 13 — The Hand Count
Conceit: In a county where every voter has asked the same mind who to vote for, a candidate forecast to lose is given by that same mind the one change of position that would make her the advised choice, and must decide whether to be elected by computation or defeated in person.
Who: Dee Marchetti, 58, who runs the hardware store in a river town in eastern Washington and is running for county commissioner. She wants the seat, and she wants the seat for one thing: the diversion dam at Miller's Bend out, and the run of fish her grandfather saw back in the river before she is too old to stand in it.
Obstacle: Every voter in the county who cares to has asked the same mind who, given what they care about, they should vote for. The mind is honest and it is the same mind, so its answers add up to a forecast anyone's model can give: she loses by 340 out of about nine thousand, with a small margin because the only unadvised voters are the ones who never asked. Her model tells her, because she asks, that knocking on doors will move roughly nothing — no one is uninformed, and no one's priorities are waiting to be set. She knocks anyway. People are kind. They already know why they are voting the way they are, and it is the reason her own model gave for their side. The obstacle is not opposition; it is that there is no one left to persuade.
Turn: In a low week she asks her model what she would have to say to win, and it tells her — keep the dam, fund a fish passage, commission a phased study — and names the two hundred or so voters whose stated priorities she would then match. She says it. She wins by sixty. It cannot be undone: she is the commissioner, elected on a position she does not hold, by voters who never had to meet her — and the one man who did meet her, who told her on his porch that he would vote for her because she came and he had never asked the thing, voted for the woman who was going to take the dam out.
Last image: Election night in the high-school gym, the hand count going on at the long tables because the county charter still requires it, everyone in the room already knowing the number, and Dee across the floor watching the old man in the Carhartt jacket who is watching the count with real interest, because he is the only one who does not.
Question: Bears on what a vote is and what persuasion is for — whether an election in which every voter is honestly advised by the same mind is the completion of democracy or its end. A reader can hold that it is a census of interests with no demagogue, no ignorance and no spin, that persuasion was mostly manipulation and good riddance, and that a candidate who moves her position to meet the electorate is representation working as designed; or that a vote is an act of choosing a person and not a query, that the one unadvised vote in the county was the only real one, and that a commissioner elected by a mind she talked into electing her has been elected by nobody. Underneath: what does Dee owe the man on the porch, and is the dam still in her?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI that rigs the election, or the AI that predicts it and the human spirit that beats the prediction with one heroic upset. Here nothing is rigged, the forecast is honest and correct, and the human who beats it does so by giving the mind what it needs; the story is about what winning costs when losing was the only outcome that would have been hers.
Closest published: Isaac Asimov, "Franchise" — Multivac knows the electorate so well that one man's answers stand in for the whole vote. There the machine picks a single citizen to make the election real; here every citizen is advised and the single unadvised voter is what is left over, and it is the candidate, not the voter, who has to live with the result.
