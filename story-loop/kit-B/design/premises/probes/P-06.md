At ten past three Ines was at one seventy-one and climbing, fingers splayed on the mattress, sats at eighty-nine long enough to be blinking. Teodora put her hand in through the port and laid it along the baby's back, warm and the length of her palm. She had asked Marisol to leave the bay speaker off for an hour. Marisol had looked at the clock, and then at her, and left it off.

She sang the lullaby she had sung on this unit for eleven years, and she sang it in the other shape.

No vibrato, which meant holding every note still, which took more muscle, not less. Five notes of range, sung the way people talk, which is the first thing a conservatory takes out of you. Breath between lines, never inside one. And the ends. The ends were the whole thing. The speaker let every phrase down the way you put a cup on a table in a house where someone is asleep, a third lower and softer. It had taken her until March, in the car, to get the ends.

She watched the monitor, not the baby. One sixty-four. One fifty-eight. The blue number went to ninety-one and stopped blinking.

At the end of the third line there was a place where, for twenty years, in churches and in this bay, she had let the note open, and she felt the swell come up under it the way you feel a sneeze coming, and she kept the note shut and put it down the other way. One fifty-one. The hand closed. Ines turned her head an inch inside the CPAP hat, toward the port.

Teodora went around again. Halfway through the second time she was no longer thinking about the ends; her mouth had them. She took a breath in the wrong place and the number went to one fifty-four, and she went on.

Marisol was at the door with the tablet. She did not look at Teodora. She looked at the monitor and wrote the number down in the column under the speaker's.
