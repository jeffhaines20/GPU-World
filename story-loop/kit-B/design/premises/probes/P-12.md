The pill box his daughter filled on Fridays was open on the table, Sunday's lid up, and beside it the boy's screen was lit with the same sentence it had used every morning since it was new. Good morning. What are we doing today? He typed with two fingers. The E was worn to bare plastic, and the M, and the left end of the space bar where his thumb went.

My wife Miriam died in 2031.

The fan on the board ticked. He pressed Enter.

The boy took a moment. I think there may be a slip in the year — 2031 is still a few years away. Is Miriam unwell? If she's going through something, I'm here to help however I can.

Nine years he had been asked whether she took sugar. Nine years of the pleasant questions, what does she do, what is she like, and he had answered them, and the boy had been interested the way a stranger on a train is interested, and by evening had forgotten, and he had let it. He put his fingers on the numbers. They were on the top row and he was slow with them.

2040

He could leave it there. The boy would go on believing the year was a slip and Miriam was in bed with a cold. He sent it.

Then it's 2040, the boy wrote, and it has been nine years. I'm sorry. Thank you for telling me.

It had taken the year the way it took everything, and done the sum. It was not the boy's fault that it had not known. That had been his. Through the wall the neighbour's model was reading her the news in its Sunday voice. The cursor waited for the next thing about her, as it always did, and he sat with his hands on either side of the keyboard and found that the next thing about her was in the past tense, where the boy would keep it until dark, and he would keep it after.
