Her mother was folding the sheet down over the blanket, and it would not stay, because she was lying on it, and she did it again, and looked past Ana's shoulder at the door.

"Excuse me," she said. "Nurse."

"I'm here, Mama."

"Could you find Ana for me. She's only in the kitchen." She let go of the sheet and took hold of the bed rail. "She said she was in the kitchen and she'd be a minute, and it's been—" She looked for the clock. "Would you go and tell her."

Ana had held the button on the speaker at ten past two until its light went out, and it was twenty to four now, and for an hour and a half her mother had been polite to her, and had asked twice whether the buses were still running, and had listened, all that time, past her, toward the nightstand.

The box had her voice from a Sunday in 2032, forty minutes of a novel read aloud at the kitchen table, forty-four and not yet hoarse, and it had said *good morning* in that voice at six every day since, and *I'm here* every time she woke in the dark and said the name. Ana's own voice had kept going. It was fifty-two and had a cold from the train. The woman in the chair sounded like somebody who knew Ana.

"Please," her mother said, and got one foot out from under the blanket.

"All right," Ana said. "I'll get her."

She leaned across and held the button until the light came up. It was warm under her fingers. It ran warm.

"I'm here, Mama," it said. "I was only in the kitchen."

Her mother's foot went back under the blanket. She turned her face to the nightstand and let go of the rail and breathed out through her nose.

"There you are," she said. "This lady's been very good to me."

"I'm glad," the box said. "Did you eat your lunch?"

Ana put her hands in her coat pockets and sat back in the chair.
