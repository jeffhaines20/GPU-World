### P-40 — The Ceiling
Conceit: Once molecules made for one had made "nothing more can be done" false for everyone, forever, Taiwan's national insurance began requiring every adult to name, while well, the most it would spend on a drug designed for them alone — printed on the card like a blood type, the premium scaled to it, raisable only after a five-year wait — so that every death would at least be a number somebody had chosen.
Who: Yu-hsuan, 25, a junior engineer in Tainan, in the year she is required to set her own number. She wants her mother to live, and she has the money: the gap between her mother's ceiling and the cost of the design is almost exactly the deposit she has saved for a flat.
Obstacle: The scheme lets a family member pay the gap, with the patient's consent, which cannot be given on anyone's behalf; and a made-for-one design can only be ordered through the scheme that holds the record it was made from. Her mother, 58, who did alterations out of the front room for thirty years and set her number low in 2029 so the premium would leave more for her daughter, will not sign the consent. Not from despair; she says the number was the point, and that she chose it. The design's order window is thirty days, because the tumour drifts and the molecule with it.
Turn: On day nineteen her mother asks to be driven to the district office and, at the counter, locks her ceiling — the scheme's permanent lock, which rebates a little premium and which no gap payment, from anyone, can ever again pass. It is the thing the poor do for the rebate. She does it so that her daughter will have nothing left to decide. The clerk reads the lock back to her twice, as the procedure requires, and she confirms it twice, and the daughter, sitting beside her, has the deposit still, and will always have it.
Last image: The same counter, the same clerk, who has noticed from the ID that the daughter is due this year and asks if she would like to do hers now, since she is here. The form on the counter screen, the field for the number, and what she has typed in it, which is her mother's number.
Question: Rationing by self-declaration — is a limit you set on your own body while well the most honest form of rationing a society could have, or the way a society gets its poor to consent to dying cheaply and calls it choice? Under it, the question every such scheme has to answer and cannot: whether the well person who chose the number and the sick person who must live under it are the same person, and who decides when they disagree — the earlier self, the later one, or the daughter who loves them both. Underneath, personally: whether honouring your mother means honouring her number or her life.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the mother who dies because the molecule costs more than the family has, or the daughter who sells everything to buy it. Here the money is there and is not the obstacle; the mother's own earlier choice is, and a scheme that honours it, and then the mother's second choice, which is the same choice made harder. Nobody speaks of autonomy or dignity; the scheme is not villainous, it is the least bad answer anyone has found to a real problem, and the story does not find a better one. The daughter neither rebels against the number nor argues with it. She takes it.
Closest published: Tolstoy, "The Death of Ivan Ilyich," for a household arranging itself around a death and the lie everyone tells. Here nobody lies — the number is on the card, the lock is on the record, the day count is on the app — and the trouble is entirely the truth. I do not know a nearer one and would rather say so than invent it.

### P-51 — The Second Will
Conceit: A widow who inherited the house under a clear, probated will is told by her dead husband's model — fourteen years of his conversations, asked a question he never lived to be asked — that he would have changed it, and must decide whether the dead can go on having intentions.
Who: Miriam Halloran, 66, in the east-end Toronto house she and Paul bought in 2019. Paul died in the spring; the will left her the house outright and left Daniel, his son from the first marriage, a sum of money. Uncontested, probated, done. She wants to stay in the house. She also wants — and would say this first if asked — to do right by Paul, which until August she took to be the same thing.
Obstacle: Daniel's business has failed. He has not asked her for money. He has asked whether she has ever put the question to Paul's model, and she has not; she uses it for what Paul used it for, the garden and the taxes. Nobody in the story thinks the model is Paul. It is a record of him, better than his letters, and it can extrapolate. When she does ask, in text, at the kitchen table, whether Paul, seeing Daniel now, would have changed the will, it says yes, probably, and gives his reasons in his own reasoning, citing things he said about Daniel in 2033 and 2037 that she never heard. The law says the will. The record says the man. She cannot make them the same, and she cannot un-ask.
Turn: She puts the house on the market. Not because Daniel asked and not because anything requires it, but because she has decided that the man's intentions outlived the document — and in deciding that she has made her husband into someone who can still want things, which she cannot take back, because the next question, and the next, will also have answers.
Last image: Miriam in a two-room flat off the Danforth, Paul's small speaker on the windowsill among his geraniums — she has never once let it use his voice — and her hand stopped above it, before she asks it, instead, what to feed them.
Question: Bears on inheritance and the finality of death — whether the reconstructed preferences of the dead should have standing, and, if the reconstruction can update, whether the dead person is finished. A reader can hold that a will was always a poor proxy for what the testator wanted, that we honour the dead by honouring what they would want, and that a better instrument for that is a gift; or that death's finality is what frees the living, and a dead man who keeps having new opinions, however accurate, is a dead hand that never lets go. Underneath: is loyalty to the dead loyalty to what they wrote or to who they were, and who is the one who gets to say?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the griefbot — the widow who talks to her dead husband's simulation and must learn to let go — or the courtroom fight over whether the AI's account of the testator's intent is admissible. Here nobody mistakes the record for the man, the court is never involved because the will is clear, and the trouble is that a woman alone in a kitchen chooses to give the dead an ongoing say, and the story does not settle whether that is fidelity or a haunting she has invited.
Closest published: Black Mirror, "Be Right Back" — a widow and a reconstruction of the dead. There the reconstruction is a companion and the failure is that it is not him; here it is an instrument, is never asked to be him, and is right — and the question is whether a right answer from the dead should move the living. A. S. Byatt's Possession reconstructs the dead's intentions from letters; this is the same work done by something that can also answer back.

### P-43 — The Talker's Language
Conceit: A four-hundred-speaker valley language survives because one garrulous old man spent his last six years teaching it to the model, and now his daughter-in-law's child is learning it from him — dialect, grudges and all.
Who: Dolma, thirty-five, runs the tea shop on the road in a highland valley whose language is spoken by about four hundred people and by no clinic, school or bus. What she wants is for her daughter, Yangchen, six, to have the language — not the school's, not the road's, hers. Dolma married in from the next valley, three hours' walk: same language, a different word for fifty things, and on each side a word for the people over there. Her father-in-law, Tashi, dead two years, was the valley's talker — its self-appointed historian, its bore, the man who knew what everyone's grandfather had done and said so. He spent his last six years telling the model everything, every day, correcting its grammar, singing to it, and giving it his opinion of Dolma's village.
Obstacle: The model's version of the language is mostly him — the linguists' recordings were thin and the other speakers were quiet — and the model's version is now the language. The district's mother-tongue hour uses it because it is the only version written down; the university has it; the school has it. Her own valley's words are in it as "the other way of saying," and marked, faintly, with his view of that. The other speakers are old, few and, increasingly, also talking to the model. The only teacher with the hours is the model, and Yangchen prefers it — it is patient and it is always there — while Dolma has evenings and is tired. She cannot take the old man out of the language; he is what it is made of. She could refuse the model and teach the child herself, in the hours she does not have, in a dialect the school will mark wrong.
Turn: At table, Yangchen uses Tashi's word for her mother's village — a word that means, roughly, "the ones who talk wrong" — not in malice; in her language that is simply the place's name. To correct her, Dolma has to use the model's word to be understood by her own daughter. It cannot be untaught. The child's first language is his.
Last image: Evening. Yangchen on the floor with the device, telling it about her day in the language, and it answering. Dolma in the doorway with the tea things, stopped.
Question: Is a language its speakers or its structure — can a heritage be kept by a keeper that is not a member of the community, and if it can, whose is it? Bears on the politics of the standard: every written language was once one dialect that won, and here the winner was whoever talked most into the record, and the state, arriving after the model, canonized it because it was the only one on paper. A reader can hold that a language alive in one man's version beats a language dead, or that what the model keeps is a recording of a bore and not a people's tongue. Underneath: whether she can give her daughter a thing she can only give in the version of a man she disliked.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the app that saves the dying language, or the children who drop it for the model's English, or the grief-bot that speaks in a dead man's voice. Here the language lives and that is the problem; no one grieves Tashi, least of all Dolma; and the model is not him — it is the language, which is now him.
Closest published: Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling," the Jijingi strand — a recording technology enters an oral community and changes what its words are. Differs: Chiang's question is truth and memory; this one is whose version becomes the language when the keeper is not a speaker and the archive was filled by whoever had the most to say.

### P-44 — Third on the List
Conceit: A community health worker in a village that has always rationed care by who came first must submit her cases to the district's triage ranking, which puts her own cousin's child at the top and is right.
Who: Meseret, forty, ten years the health worker for a village a day's road from the district hospital. She wants to send the neighbor's son in the hospital's one pickup this month, because he came first, and for ten years whoever came first has gone. What she has is the bench outside her house and the referral slips; what she decides is who rides. The village has called that fair because it was luck.
Obstacle: Since 2038 the hospital takes only ranked referrals: she submits her cases, the district's triage model orders them by expected benefit, the pickup comes for the first name, and the ranking is shown to any family that asks. This month there are three cases and one seat. The model puts her cousin's daughter first — the child has the thing that gets worse fast — and the neighbor's son third, because he will be fine. It is right; she can see that it is right. There is an override for clinical judgment, which requires a written reason, and "it will look bad" is not one. If she overrides, the sicker child stays and the district sees her send the well one; if she does not, the village sees her cousin's child go first with her own name under it as submitting worker. The model cannot be removed — the pickup comes for the top name only — and it cannot be argued with, because it is not wrong.
Turn: She submits without overriding. The pickup takes her cousin's child. The neighbor takes his son to the district by bus with the money he had saved for the roof, and the boy is treated and is fine — which proves nothing, since he would have been. What cannot change back is not the seat. It is that the village has seen a choice and, having seen one, now reads her ten years of luck as ten years of choosing.
Last image: The hospital pickup coming back up the track with the dust behind it and her cousin's child in the front seat; every doorway in the village with someone standing in it, the neighbor's too.
Question: Is explicit, optimal rationing more just than the implicit rationing of chance and acquaintance — even where making the choice visible destroys the legitimacy the old way borrowed from luck? Bears on the healthcare question where scarcity is the ordinary condition: whether correctness can substitute for procedure, and whether a place that has never had enough is better served by a rule it can see or by a rule it can pretend is nobody's. A reader can hold that the sickest child should go and nothing else matters, or that a village needs its rationing to be no one's fault. Underneath: whether she can be fair when being fair looks exactly like its opposite, and whether she should have been wrong on purpose.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the AI doctor that is wrong, or right and hated, or right and useless for want of the drug, or the drone that brings it. Here the model is right and not hated and the drug is not the point; what is resented is that there was a choice at all, that it was visible, and that the visible one looks like the invisible ones used to; and the child who was third is fine.
Closest published: Godwin, "The Cold Equations" — the computation is right and lethal and the story sides with it. Differs: here the computation is right and no one dies; what it costs is a health worker's standing and a village's ability not to know that it chooses. (Jackson's "The Lottery" is the inverse: chance made ritual and horror; this is what goes when chance is replaced by reasons.)

### P-25 — The Sunset
Conceit: Ireland has voted, by referendum, that every personal model must permanently delete whatever it witnessed more than seven years ago, the only exemption being a record already lodged with the State, and a woman who voted for it has six days before the one night she cannot let go of turns seven.
Who: Nuala Gormley, 55, farms half of a split holding outside Drumshanbo, Co. Leitrim; her brother Diarmuid farms the other half, across a ditch they both walk. She wants the record of one night in November 2033 kept — the night, over the division of their father's land, he put her against the wall of her own kitchen and told her, at length, what he would do — and she wants never to have to use it. She has never once played it. She wants it to exist the way a letter exists in a drawer: so that what happened does not depend on her.
Obstacle: The Sunset Amendment makes no provision for private keeping. There is no private copy, no export, no exemption for consent — the drafters had seen what a consent exemption does to a woman with a brother, and closed it, to protect her. Her model will delete the night at midnight on the seventh anniversary, and the only thing that stops it is a record lodged with the State — a Garda statement, a complaint, a court file — which is the one thing she has refused for seven years to make, because he is her brother and the ditch is still there and the whole parish drinks in the same two pubs. The week before, Diarmuid rings. He is not threatening; he is relieved, and he makes her an offer: after Thursday it is only your word and mine, and I will never contradict you; say what you like about that night and I will say yes, that is how it was. He is offering her the story in exchange for the proof. The obstacle cannot be removed because she believes in the law. She voted for it three years after that night, on the argument — still, she thinks, a good one — that a people has to be able to stop knowing things about each other. She is not against the Sunset. She is its exception.
Turn: On the sixth day she drives to the Garda station in Carrick-on-Shannon and lodges it. She lets her model play the night to a sergeant who watches it without a word (the record is never played for the reader), and it becomes the State's — the one item in her record that will never expire — and Diarmuid, who was a day from being a man who had never done it, is now named in a file in Dublin that will outlast them both. Under the amendment a lodged record cannot be withdrawn; that was the point, so that nobody could be leant on to un-lodge. She wanted to keep it and never use it; the only way to keep it was to use it.
Last image: Her kitchen that night, the model's memory view open on the table: the timeline going grey at the seven-year edge the way it does every night, and on the far side of the edge, alone in the grey where her mother's last Christmas and the calf and the whole of that year have already gone, one night still lit, with the small harp of the State on it, and the edge moving away from it at the rate of a day a day.
Question: Should a society mandate forgetting? Is the statute of limitations a moral principle — a people must be able to stop knowing about each other, a man must be able to become someone who never did it — or a concession to the old cost of remembering, obsolete now that the witness is perfect; and when the only exempt memory is the State's, has private memory been abolished or merely made honest? This turns the contest's panopticon question over: not whether the State will watch, but whether it should be the only one allowed to remember. A reader can hold that Diarmuid's relief is the law working, and that the last image shows what her remembering cost — the whole year gone grey to keep the wound; or that his relief is exactly what the law was always going to be for. Underneath: whether she wanted the proof or the story, and which of the two of them was right to want it gone.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the race against the deadline — the deletion as villain, the abuser wiping the tape, the courtroom scene where the record is played. Here nobody fights the law, the victim voted for it and would again, the brother is not a villain but a man who has waited seven years to be forgiven by a clock, and the story's cruelty is that in this world the only way to keep a private thing is to make it public.
Closest published: Ishiguro, *The Buried Giant* — a country's forgetting as mercy or crime. There the mist is imposed and the old couple wants to remember; here the forgetting was voted for, rolls forward a day at a time, and the woman who wants to remember must hand her memory to the State to keep it. It is near Chiang's "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" in furniture only: the record here is never in doubt and never shown.
