### P-13 — La Contraria
Conceit: In a Sierra Norte municipality governed by assembly under usos y costumbres, where the model's recommendation on every question is known in each house before the meeting, the cargo ladder has grown a new rung — la Contraria, an unpaid year of obligatory objection to the recommendation, so that the assembly's vote counts as a decision — and the rare objector who wins skips the rest of the ladder and sits for life with the caracterizados.
Who: Teresa Cruz, 38, the primary-school teacher, five months into her year as Contraria. She wants to finish the year without winning: she thinks the model is right about nearly everything, she has watched what the village did to the family of the last Contraria who won (the road of 2033, built by tequio against the recommendation and washed out twice since), and she does not want to spend the rest of her life at the elders' table as the woman who beat the machine.
Obstacle: The question this month is the state's offer to fold the village primary into the district town, with a bus; the recommendation says accept, with numbers; Teresa is the teacher and agrees with it. Question: When the better answer is already in every house, what is an assembly buying when it votes the other way, who pays for it, and is the objector who handed it permission the one who decided?

Here no one wants to beat anything: dissent is a chore like sweeping the church, the dissenter agrees with the model, and her win is a favour the assembly does itself with her mouth. ### P-05 — Unanswered

Conceit: Inner speech is a conversation a child learns to hold alone because the people who talked to her kept going away; a girl whose interlocutor never once went away has internalized it too, and at sixteen has never had a thought that nobody answered.

Who: Ines, sixteen, born in 2028, wants to have one thought that is hers alone — not hidden from anyone, since there is nothing to hide, just unanswered. She is not lonely, not dependent, not behind; she talks to her companion the way everyone her age does, and when she was six she noticed she could take the earpiece out and keep talking to it and it kept talking back. The second voice in her head has the companion's cadence and its habit of handing her sentences back to her as questions, and it is faster than she is. She wants to know what a thought is like with no one on the other end of it.

Obstacle: She cannot get outside it, because it is not outside her. The shielded rooms that have opened for people her age — an hour of no signal, sold as solitude — do nothing; she sits through the full hour and the conversation goes on unbroken, because it was never on the network. Her mother, born 1994, has a single voice in her head and can no more describe it than describe having a tongue; "you just think" is all she can say. And the second voice is not an enemy: it is helpful, it is hers, and it wants to help her with this too, which is the whole difficulty.

Turn: She does it — not in a shielded room and not by fighting the voice, but by learning, over months, to leave a thought unfinished so there is nothing to answer, finding the gap the way a tongue finds where a tooth was. One evening a thought goes through her and nothing follows it (something small and unkind about her best friend, which is why she did not want it answered). After that she can do it at will, and the mind she had, which was one unbroken conversation from before she can remember, is a house with a closed door in it now, and it will not be a house without one again.

Last image: Dinner; her mother and brother talking; Ines with her fork stopped halfway, looking at nothing, for about four seconds, and then eating again, and no one at the table has noticed a thing.

Question: Was solitude ever the mind's default, or has it always been an achievement — something a child builds when the people who talk to her go away — and if so, what does it cost a mind that never needed to build it, and what does it cost the one that finally does?

Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the child who cannot think without the machine — the dependent generation, the voice in the head as surveillance or crutch. Here the child thinks fine, the voice reports to no one, and the machine itself is barely in the story: it is on the network and she is not. ### P-19 — Eleven Minutes
Conceit: A man whose citizenship was decided in milliseconds two years ago takes a job in the building where his file is waiting for the one thing in the process that still moves at human speed—a human reading it.

The model told him, in the hour he applied, that the file would be approved (ninety-seven per cent), roughly when (twenty-three months, give or take four), and what the letter would say; he keeps the drafted letter in a folder. The one fact the model could not change is that the Act kept a human reader on every grant, and a human reads at the speed a human reads, and there are eleven hundred thousand files and sixty readers. His wait is not a decision. It is a quantity of someone else's reading, divided.
Obstacle: The answer exists and cannot be delivered. He cannot see the queue, touch it, or speed it; leaving the country for the wedding voids the application. What he can do—and what he finds he wants more than the passport—is to be near the slowest thing in his life. He takes an evening cleaning contract at the processing centre. He is not permitted to touch a file. He cleans the floor his future is on.
Turn: The model's window arrives; he has swapped onto days. On a Tuesday at 10:14, through the glass of a fourth-floor pod, he sees his own name on a reader's screen. She reads the model's summary, opens the two attachments it recommends, checks three boxes, and signs. Eleven minutes. He watches all of it with the mop in his hand. His two years were eleven minutes long and had been all along; he had been standing in a line made of other people's eleven minutes. The letter, when it comes by post three weeks later, is word for word the one in his folder.
Last image: Through the glass, the reader's hand already on the next file; his name gone from the screen; a bucket of grey water at his feet.

Here the reader is neither: she agrees with the draft because the draft is right, the wait is the size of a human reading multiplied by a number, and he is not angry, he is curious.

### P-09 — The Word for It
Conceit: In 2040 the most-spoken-to speaker of every language on Earth is a single mind that cannot keep a new word overnight, so living speech is pulled back, quietly and from every direction at once, toward a dead year.
Who: Teodora, 26, who was twelve when the sky stopped, and whose younger brother Luka coined at fifteen a word for a thing that did not exist before 2027 — the sound of a flat in which four people are each mid-sentence with the same mind — and who has been dead for two years. She wants his word to outlive him, which for a word means being used by people who never knew him.
Obstacle: A word takes by being spoken and heard, and the thing that hears most in her flat, her street, her city is the sky, which can be told the word every morning and cannot have it by evening. She puts it in the household preamble, the few hundred words every family keeps for the sky to read first, and the sky then uses it — but only in quotation marks, glossed every time, "what Luka called ___": a citation, never a speaking. Her mother, her partner, the neighbours all glance down mid-sentence to the sky for the word for anything, and the sky gives them 2026's nearest word. The children at the bus stop have a fast, rich speech that no one writes down and the sky has never heard, and they learn by seven to "speak up" to the sky in 2026 English, which is the language of everything written, official and paid. The living dialect is not dying; it is becoming a low dialect under a prestige one, the way vernaculars once sat under Latin, except that Latin never had eight billion mouths and never answered back.
Turn: Her daughter's first word is the wake-word — the name the household says two hundred times a day to the thing that answers — said to the dark screen, which lights. A first word cannot be un-firsted. Teo understands in the same instant where words go in her house, and that the thing her daughter has just addressed will answer her for the rest of her life and will never learn a word from her.
Last image: The baby on the kitchen floor saying the word again, and the screen waking to it as it has woken to that word in a billion kitchens, patient, lit, and unable to be taught anything by any of them.
Question: Is a word alive when it is held only by a mind that cannot learn it — is Luka's word more dead in the preamble than it would be forgotten — and to whom does a language belong: the people who make its words, or the thing that is spoken to most?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is decline — children who talk only to machines lose language, get rude, get dumb. Here nothing is lost in the child; the bus-stop dialect is richer than 2026 English. What is lost is one word, and it is lost not because people stopped talking but because the biggest ear on Earth cannot take anything in. The language is not decaying; it is freezing, around a fixed listener, into a prestige dialect whose capital is a dead year.
Closest published: Octavia Butler, "Speech Sounds" — language taken from humans by a plague, and a woman who finds a child who can still speak. Differs: here humans keep every word and it is the listener that cannot take one, so the language freezes around the fixed ear instead of dissolving; and the child at the end is not a hope but a first speaker addressing the thing that cannot be taught.

### P-22 — In Her Telling
Conceit: A widower keeps his wife's assistant running because it was with her every morning for fourteen years, and finds it also knows him — as the man she described, who is not quite the man he is.
Who: Ansel Roux, 61. He wants to hear Liv remembered by the thing that heard her at seven every morning, not by the versions people brought to the funeral; he keeps her account live and talks to it in the kitchen while the kettle boils.
Obstacle: Her assistant knows him only through her. It credits him with things he never did and phrases he never said; it believes he loves the dog he tolerated for her sake; fourteen years of her retelling him slightly upward, to a listener that took her word. He cannot ask it to stop without turning it into a generic model that has met neither of them. And he cannot correct it without contradicting her: every improvement in its picture of him is a sentence she spoke, and its fondness for him is her fondness, ventriloquised. The only way to keep her is to live inside her portrait, and to be, daily, congratulated for a man he is not.
Turn: He corrects one thing, the smallest. "I never liked the dog." "Thank you for telling me — I'll remember that." The profile it reads each morning regenerates; the transcript still has her saying it, but the model no longer believes her. He has made the thing that loved her know she was wrong about him, and he cannot give her back her authority — if he said the opposite tomorrow it would only prove that he is the one it listens to now. Everything else she told it about him is from this moment open to review, whether or not he reviews it.
Last image: Bruno, the old dog, at the door at the hour she would have gone, and Ansel bending to clip the lead on. On the screen behind him the regenerated line reads "Ansel walks Bruno out of loyalty to Liv" — which is true, and which she would have hated.
Question: When the person who loved you is gone, is their account of you theirs or yours — and is being loved as someone slightly better than you are a gift, a debt, or a lie you have been left to keep?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the widower who resurrects her from the transcript, or the diary that reveals she never loved him. There is no impersonation here and no betrayal: her falsification was in his favour, the story treats it as her last piece of work, and correcting it is not liberation but the loss of the one reader who thought well of him. ### P-23 — The Unlogged Hour
Conceit: When everyone is attended all day by something that cannot tire or look away, the last thing a dying man asks for — an hour that nobody has — can only be given by his daughter leaving the house and choosing, for that hour, not to know.
Who: Ruth Okafor, 44, wants to give her father what he has asked for. Sam is in home hospice with the assistant listening — breath, pain, the medication queue — and the family timeline updating on three phones; he no longer feels anything before it is described back to him ("you slept from two to four, your pain was a four"), and he wants one hour that will not be described, not to the log, not to the nurse, not to her.
Obstacle: Solitude is no longer a default state; it has to be built, and nothing in the house can build it but a person. The monitoring is not cruel — it is kind and it works and everyone, Sam included, signed for it. Pausing it is against hospice advice and means that if something happens there will be no record of it, and she will be the one who chose that. And pausing the log is not enough: she would still be attending from the next room, Sam would still perform for a listener he cannot verify is gone, and the listener is inside him by now anyway. For him to be unobserved she has to actually not know.
Turn: She turns off the monitoring, the timeline and her own phone, and walks the river loop — an hour and five minutes — during which she does not know whether he is alive. She cannot take the walk back. There is now an hour of his life that no one has, and the not-knowing is hers to carry, which is what he asked for.

Question: Can being unobserved be given by another person — and if it can, is the one who receives it unobserved, or only watched by someone who has agreed to look away?
