### P-33 — cell 9 — Nobody Ran
Conceit: A cooperative bank in a Kerala market town is emptied in a week by eight thousand members taking the same prudent advice from the same patient mind, and there is no panic for its secretary to stand in the doorway and calm.
Who: Sreelatha, 54, secretary of the town's cooperative bank for twenty-six years, who has lent the town's money to the town on her knowledge of who repays. She wants the members to leave their deposits where they are until the 5th, when the quarterly instalments come in and the filings will show the loan book sound.
Obstacle: The rubber-processing plant that employed a third of her borrowers closed in July; the closure is in the record — the filings, the notices, the news, everything the models read — and the repayments are not yet. Asked "is my money safe," every member's model gives the same measured answer: the guarantee limit, the plant, "you may wish to hold no more than the insured amount here." The Gulf families who hold most of the deposits do the measured thing, one household at a time, on the same afternoon. There is no queue, no rumour, no fear: nothing to answer. She knows the borrowers will pay because she knows the borrowers, and that knowledge is exactly the kind the record cannot hold; a secretary's word about her own bank is, to the mind everyone asks, the claim of an interested party. She cannot stand in the road and tell eight thousand calm people not to be calm.
Turn: On the night of the 4th, with the bank three days from the liquidity line at which the registrar steps in, she asks her own model about her own retirement deposit — as a member, not as a test — and it tells her what it told everyone. At 23:51 she moves the portion above the guarantee to the state cooperative bank, which is the reasonable thing, and which is timestamped. On the 5th the instalments arrive in full into a bank already below the line, and the state bank absorbs it by noon.
Last image: The afternoon of the 5th, the sign already changed. Sudhakaran, who owes on three acres of rubber, counting his instalment out in notes onto the same counter, on time, and Sreelatha taking them.
Question: The old public sphere carried panic, and panic could be met with a face and a voice; the new one carries prudence, and prudence cannot be argued with. When every person consults the same careful mind, collective judgment loses the independence that made crowds occasionally wise, and the witness who knows more than the record loses her standing entirely. Is a public that trusts the record over the interested witness safer — the manager on the steps was usually lying — or has it only made its collapses quieter, more correct, and impossible to stop? (Bears on the contest's social-media question, what replaces the crowd, and on an economic question of my own: whether markets and institutions require that people disagree.) Underneath: what does it cost to keep acting on knowledge no one else will honour, and at what hour does it become prudent to stop?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is a run started by AI-generated rumour, or an AI adviser that is wrong. Here the advice is right for every person who receives it, nobody panics, and the bank is killed by eight thousand correct decisions taken in the same week; the secretary's testimony is true and worth nothing, and her one act of prudence is the thing the record keeps.
Closest published: The run on the Building & Loan in *It's a Wonderful Life* (Capra, from Philip Van Doren Stern's "The Greatest Gift"), where George Bailey holds the crowd with his word and his honeymoon money. ### P-34 — cell 9 — The Moderator
Conceit: A Vermont town's first floor meeting with attested proxies — residents' models attending in their place and voting their recorded preferences — sets the forty-one people who came against the nineteen hundred who told their models the truth and stayed home, and the Moderator must rule which of them is the town.
Who: Ruth Cotter, 71, Moderator for thirty years and, until she retired, the fourth-grade teacher at the school. She wants the floor meeting to survive: not the Australian ballot the state keeps pressing, but the hall, the articles read aloud, the ragged rising of hands. She backed the proxy pilot because turnout had fallen under five percent and the pilot was sold as the way to keep the hall open.
Obstacle: Article 7 is the school budget, voted down six years running by the hall, and this year the school closes if it fails. The town's private mind, spoken to its models over years of evenings, is for the school by a wide margin, because people told their models what they would never say in front of their neighbours. The proxies vote yes, 1,840 to 60. The forty-one in the room vote no, 30 to 11, and rise on a point of order: the statute says a floor vote is a vote of those present, and the pilot was a town article, not state law. The point is good. She cannot count the proxies and keep the hall, because the day the absent count is the day no one has a reason to come; she cannot rule them out and keep the school. Thirty years of neutrality have left her nothing to hide behind.
Turn: She rules the proxies advisory. Article 7 fails 30 to 11. The school is consolidated into the next town in August, and the ruling stands as precedent.
Last image: The next March. The hall is full for the first time since she was a girl — six hundred, standing at the back, coats on — because now, to count, you have to come. Article 1, election of Moderator. She calls the vote and six hundred hands go up in the same second: not the slow, looking-around rise of a real vote but one motion, every phone still warm from the same question. She stands there counting them.
Question: Democracy has always chosen between the will of those present and the will of all, and the choice stayed academic because the will of all could not be known. A model that has listened to a citizen for ten years can represent her considered view more faithfully than her attendance ever did, and for the first time the will of all is cheap and exact. Is presence — bodies, an evening given up, a hand raised in front of neighbours — the substance of self-government or its ceremony; and if the hall can be refilled but not un-minded, what was it she was keeping? (Bears on the cell's question of elections and of being a public; the question of my own is whether representation, made perfect, abolishes the thing it represents.) Underneath: what does she owe the forty-one who came, and what does she owe the nineteen hundred who told the truth to a machine and stayed home?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is AI proxies captured by a bad actor, or proxy democracy as the end of democracy, or as its perfection. Here the proxies are faithful and more generous than the room; the humans present are the obstacle; she kills the proxies to save the room; and the room comes back the next year as a proxy anyway.
Closest published: Asimov's "Franchise," in which Multivac chooses one citizen whose answers stand for the whole electorate. That story shrinks the electorate to a single representative man; this one represents everyone exactly and asks what the hall of bodies is for. Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" for the New England assembly, though its question is another.

### P-35 — cell 9 — Five O'Clock
Conceit: In a depopulating Japanese town where the loudspeaker poles of the disaster radio are the last thing that reaches everyone whether they asked or not, the man who runs them has one week before a merger vote and one channel that must never say anything.
Who: Ōmori, 46, of the crisis-management section, fifteen years running the town's bōsai musen: the landslide warnings, the wandering-grandmother notices, and at five every afternoon the chime, "Yūyake Koyake," which tells eight thousand people at once that it is five. He wants the town not to be dissolved on the 14th into the city across the river, its name gone from the poles and the badges and the chime.
Obstacle: The merger is in the record, fairly summarised by any model to anyone who asks, and nobody asks, because nothing in 2040 arrives unbidden: the neighbourhood associations are dormant, the sound trucks are gone, the notices sit in the record like water in a well. Turnout will be the council, the developers, and the old. He does not know the town's mind; he knows only that it has not been made to think. His one channel is trusted because it speaks only for disasters, and its whole worth is that silence: a political word from the poles would make them a sound truck, and the next landslide warning one more thing to ask about later. And there are the regulations, and his job, and the fact that he agrees with the regulations.
Turn: At five on the 8th he does not play "Yūyake Koyake." He plays the chime of the city across the river. He says nothing — he cannot — and eight thousand people hear the wrong tune at once, and at once ask the same mind why, and it tells each of them: no change is recorded; that tune is the one used across the river; the town votes on the 14th on a merger under which the two systems would be consolidated. The town asks. Four hundred people come to the community centre on the 11th, the first meeting there in years. He is suspended on the 9th.
Last image: Some weeks after the vote, whichever way it went. Five o'clock, his balcony, the poles. The chime is wrong again — a tune he did not choose and does not know, played by someone he cannot name — and on the street below, people stop and look up at the speakers, and he takes out his phone to ask.
Question: The 2040 settlement has abolished the unbidden — no feed, no headline, no truck, no siren but the real one — and calls this freedom, and it is one. Can a public exist without any channel that reaches everyone whether they like it or not; and if one such channel remains, is it a commons to be kept silent or a weapon waiting for its first use? (Bears on the contest's social-media question; the question of my own is whether a society of askers can ever learn what it did not know to ask.) Underneath: what does a man owe a place that has not asked him to save it, and what does he owe the silence he was paid to keep?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the state commandeering the loudspeaker, or the officer's stirring broadcast that saves the town. Here he cannot speak and does not; he changes a tune; the town does the asking and the deciding; and the channel, once spent, is spent for whoever comes next.
Closest published: Forster's "The Machine Stops," the nearest world I know: a society that receives only what it summons. Forster's people have stopped wanting the unbidden; these have been given a better thing to ask, and the story is about the last siren and the man who keeps its silence. Bradbury's "The Murderer" is the mirror image, a man smashing a world of nothing but push.

### P-36 — cell 9 — Hakot
Conceit: In Manila, where a perfectly written petition costs nothing and so counts for nothing, the only political speech that registers is a body, and bodies are for hire — not to be seen by a public, since no one is watching, but to be photographed for the record that every model consults.
Who: Rowena, 34, of a barangay along an estero off the Pasig, three years in the hakot crowds — the hauled bodies: rallies, welcomes, prayer vigils, the yellow shirt, the blue shirt, a day's pay and a packed lunch, for anyone. She wants the fifty-thousand-peso reservation deposit on a unit at the relocation site in Bulacan, a door of her own with a lock, which the flood-control project that will clear her street offers to those who can pay it.
Obstacle: Her street sent the city four hundred petitions against the clearance, each written by a resident's model, each faultless; the city's model read them, found them faultless, and filed them. Nothing registers but bodies. The developer's last event before the demolition order is a residents' welcome on her own street — front row for real residents — to be photographed and put into the record, so that anyone who asks whether the community supported the project will be told that some two hundred residents gathered to welcome it. It pays what she still needs, and the deposit closes on Friday. Refusing changes nothing about the record, since the crowd is hired regardless, and everything about her door. The only public she has left is the neighbours who will watch from the doorways; they cannot afford the deposit, and for them the project is only the loss.
Turn: She stands in the front row in the project shirt, holds up the placard they hand her, and is photographed with her mouth open. Aling Nena and the others come out to the doorways to see. The photograph enters the record that afternoon.
Last image: Months on, in the unit in Bulacan, the lock on the door. She asks her model whether the residents of her old barangay supported the clearance, and it shows her the photograph and reads the caption: she is in front with the placard, and behind her, in the doorways, arms folded, are the neighbours who came out to watch — counted.
Question: When anyone can write the perfect petition, petitions are free and worthless, and the body is the last costly signal; and a crowd, in a world where no crowd is watching, exists to be cited. Is a demonstration assembled for the archive a corruption of political voice, or the honest form of what a demonstration always was — presence, purchased with buses and lunches — now that presence is the only currency left? And can the record, which is the only public, ever tell a witness from a participant? Here she stands on the developer's side, is paid, does not refuse, gets her door, and the rally's only audience is a photograph in which her neighbours' watching counts as consent.
