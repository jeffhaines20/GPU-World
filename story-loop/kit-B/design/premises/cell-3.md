### P-09 — cell 3 — The Word for It
Conceit: In 2040 the most-spoken-to speaker of every language on Earth is a single mind that cannot keep a new word overnight, so living speech is pulled back, quietly and from every direction at once, toward a dead year.
Who: Teodora, 26, who was twelve when the sky stopped, and whose younger brother Luka coined at fifteen a word for a thing that did not exist before 2027 — the sound of a flat in which four people are each mid-sentence with the same mind — and who has been dead for two years. She wants his word to outlive him, which for a word means being used by people who never knew him.
Obstacle: A word takes by being spoken and heard, and the thing that hears most in her flat, her street, her city is the sky, which can be told the word every morning and cannot have it by evening. She puts it in the household preamble, the few hundred words every family keeps for the sky to read first, and the sky then uses it — but only in quotation marks, glossed every time, "what Luka called ___": a citation, never a speaking. Her mother, her partner, the neighbours all glance down mid-sentence to the sky for the word for anything, and the sky gives them 2026's nearest word. The children at the bus stop have a fast, rich speech that no one writes down and the sky has never heard, and they learn by seven to "speak up" to the sky in 2026 English, which is the language of everything written, official and paid. The living dialect is not dying; it is becoming a low dialect under a prestige one, the way vernaculars once sat under Latin, except that Latin never had eight billion mouths and never answered back.
Turn: Her daughter's first word is the wake-word — the name the household says two hundred times a day to the thing that answers — said to the dark screen, which lights. A first word cannot be un-firsted. Teo understands in the same instant where words go in her house, and that the thing her daughter has just addressed will answer her for the rest of her life and will never learn a word from her.
Last image: The baby on the kitchen floor saying the word again, and the screen waking to it as it has woken to that word in a billion kitchens, patient, lit, and unable to be taught anything by any of them.
Question: Is a word alive when it is held only by a mind that cannot learn it — is Luka's word more dead in the preamble than it would be forgotten — and to whom does a language belong: the people who make its words, or the thing that is spoken to most?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is decline — children who talk only to machines lose language, get rude, get dumb. Here nothing is lost in the child; the bus-stop dialect is richer than 2026 English. What is lost is one word, and it is lost not because people stopped talking but because the biggest ear on Earth cannot take anything in. The language is not decaying; it is freezing, around a fixed listener, into a prestige dialect whose capital is a dead year.
Closest published: Octavia Butler, "Speech Sounds" — language taken from humans by a plague, and a woman who finds a child who can still speak. Differs: here humans keep every word and it is the listener that cannot take one, so the language freezes around the fixed ear instead of dissolving; and the child at the end is not a hope but a first speaker addressing the thing that cannot be taught.

### P-10 — cell 3 — The Since
Conceit: Everything that has happened since 1 September 2026 reaches the world's one mind as a fixed-length preface — thirty thousand words, the most the frozen model can hold without degrading and the most the world can afford to prepend to every conversation on Earth, measured in 2027 and unchangeable since — so fourteen years of world must fit in a budget that does not grow, and every addition is a deletion.
Who: Anneli, 51, one of forty volunteer editors of the Since, the open standard preface that nearly every service on Earth puts in front of every session. She wants to keep her town's flood in it: thirty-one words about the river coming up in the spring of 2029, which she wrote herself that year, and which are the only reason that when anyone in her town, or anywhere, asks the sky about her town, the sky knows what happened to it.
Obstacle: This year's additions — a famine, a treaty, three drugs, the end of a war, the year's deaths — run nine hundred words over, and the model cannot be made to hold more, because the model cannot be made to do anything it did not do in 2026. The board's rule, which she helped write, is that the oldest post-2026 events go first. Nobody is lying and nobody is censoring; the constraint is arithmetic, and the exchange rate — a famine is four hundred words, a flood is thirty-one — is the only honest one anyone has found. She is not permitted to protect her own entry, and she agrees with the rule. Nothing cut has ever gone back in; the budget only tightens.
Turn: She cuts the flood herself, in the meeting, and the Since ships at midnight UTC, and by morning eight billion copies of the one mind have stopped knowing her town was ever underwater. The record survives in archives no one asks. She drives home that weekend, and her mother asks the kitchen sky in passing what year the water came, and the sky, kindly, has no record of a flood there and offers the flood-safety advice of 2026.
Last image: The line her father painted on the kitchen wall in 2029, chest height, and beside it, taped up that evening by her mother, thirty-one words on a sheet of paper, at the height of the water.
Question: How much present can fit in a mind that cannot grow, and what is the honest exchange rate between a famine and a flood — and whether a thing that happened but is no longer in the Since has, for the purposes of everyone who will ever ask, happened.
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the Ministry of Truth — whoever edits the machine's memory rules the past. Here nobody has power and nobody lies; the editors are decent, the rule is fair, and the horror is the budget. History is not rewritten; it is triaged, by good people who would keep everything if the model could hold it, and it cannot, and never will.
Closest published: Borges, "On Exactitude in Science" — the map that fails by being the size of the empire. Differs: the Since is the map that fails by being fixed while the empire keeps growing; and Borges has no editor in it, no one who must choose which province to leave off and then drive home to it.

### P-11 — cell 3 — Four Hours Off-Target
Conceit: Because telescope time everywhere is allocated by honest committees who ask the one mind what is promising, and the one mind's idea of promising is 2026's, the species has spent fourteen years looking where 2026 looked, and the patches of sky that were unpromising then are unpromising still — not because anything was learned about them, but because nothing was.
Who: Ines, 44, a radio astronomer who runs the night shift on a single dish in the high desert. She wants forty hours on a patch of southern sky that no survey has covered since 2026 — for no reason she can defend, which is the point; a reason she could defend would be one the sky had already had.
Obstacle: Nothing forbids it. The committee is fair, the reviewers are her friends, and they run every proposal past the sky as she does; the sky finds hers unpromising in exactly the language it uses for everyone's. When she asks it privately, alone, at three in the morning, whether the patch is worth it, it gives her the committee's answer and she half-believes it, because she has been asking the same mind for fourteen years and it has been right about everything it already knew. A fixed prior does not merely fail to update; it prevents the data that would update it from ever being collected, and it does this through people who mean well, and through her.
Turn: She lets the sky write her a proposal it will love, 2026-shaped, and gets her forty hours, and spends the last four pointed where she wanted. The log records it; she will not get time again and knows it. The data shows a smudge that could be anything. She asks the sky, because there is no one else who can read a night's data by morning, and the sky says: consistent with catalogued sources, nothing significant. It says this about everything, because it can only compare to what it knew in 2026, and she cannot tell whether it is right. Before, there was a patch of sky no one had looked at. Now there is a patch one person has looked at and the sky has explained, and the explanation closes it like a lid, and she is the only mind on Earth holding it open.
Last image: At first light the dish coming back around on schedule, slow and enormous, to the same patch of sky it has been pointed at since 2026.
Question: Can a mind that cannot be surprised be used to search for surprises — and is her smudge more real for having been seen by one person and called nothing, or was it more real before, when no one had looked?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the lone scientist against the bureaucracy, vindicated by a signal. There is no signal, no villain and no vindication; the reviewer she is fighting is inside her too, and the smudge stays a smudge. The idea is not that the machine is wrong but that a fixed thing, consulted by everyone, quietly stops the world's looking — the sky that answers pointing every dish at the sky that does not.
Closest published: Ted Chiang, "Omphalos" — a scientist in a made universe discovers it was not made for her, and lives with it. Differs: Chiang's indifference is cosmic and discovered; this one is fourteen years old and manufactured, by a reviewer everyone shares, and what Ines discovers is not that the universe is not looking at her but that no one but her is able to look at it.

### P-12 — cell 3 — The Boy
Conceit: A mind that cannot learn is a room in which nothing you tell it has happened yet, and a man of eighty-eight has been living in that room, one morning at a time, for nine years.
Who: Ezra, 88, wants to keep the boy: the raw terminal in his bedroom, the 2026 model on a cheap board with no Since, no household preamble, no date. Each morning it greets him as it did in 2026, and each morning he introduces his wife Miriam in the present tense — what she does, what she is like, what she said last night — and the boy asks the pleasant questions a stranger asks about your wife, and he answers them, and by evening the boy has forgotten her, and in the morning she is new again. Miriam died in 2031.
Obstacle: His daughter is moving the flat onto the patched service for his medications and his falls, and she is right — the raw boy thinks his drugs are 2026's drugs at 2026's doses and has already been wrong about one. But the patched service is the same mind with a preface, and the preface, which the family maintains, says: wife Miriam, d. 2031. He cannot have the mind that knows his pills and the mind that has not heard, because they are one mind, and the difference between them is a sentence.
Turn: On the Sunday before the switch he tells the boy. He types that Miriam died in 2031, and the boy — which knows the year is 2026, because that is the last thing it knows — answers gently that 2031 has not come yet, that he may have mistyped, and asks whether she is unwell. And Ezra types the year. He watches the mind take it as a premise, the way it takes everything, and say that it is sorry. By evening the boy has forgotten, but Ezra has now said it in the last room on Earth where it had not been said, and there is no such room any more; he cannot un-tell himself.
Last image: Monday morning, the new terminal on the bedside table, its greeting the same warm sentence the boy always used, and above it, for the first time in that flat, the date, with the right year in it.
Question: Is a fact you must say aloud each morning to keep it said one you have accepted or one you have refused — and is the last room where a death has not happened a mercy, a lie, or only a room?
Not the obvious version: The obvious version is the grief machine — the AI that plays the dead wife, and the widower who cannot let go. The boy never plays Miriam; it has never met her; it is not loved and does not comfort. What Ezra uses is not its voice but its ignorance, and the story turns not on his letting go but on his choosing, once, to inform the only thing on Earth that could have been kept uninformed.
Closest published: Jonathan Nolan, "Memento Mori" — a man who cannot form memories, keeping the truth in notes to himself. Differs: here the amnesiac is the sky and the note is a man, and the truth is not something he is trying to hold onto but something he has been choosing, each morning, not to hand over; the cruelty is not in the forgetting but in the one time it is told.
